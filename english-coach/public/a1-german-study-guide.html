1. Sich vorstellen — Self Introduction

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

These are the 33 most-asked A1 questions you will face in real conversations, oral exams, and registration offices in Germany. Every German A1 exam includes a "tell me about yourself" task — practise answering each question out loud about your own life.

  • Start with Q1–10 — they appear in every conversation and are tested in every exam.
  • Always answer in a full sentence, not just one word: "Ich heiße Maria." not just "Maria."
  • Practise the Q&A in both directions — you need to both ask and answer.
  • Memory tip: Write a short paragraph about yourself using Q1–15. Read it aloud every day until it flows naturally.

2. Begrüßung — Greetings

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Greetings are the very first thing you say in any German interaction. Getting them right immediately signals that you are polite and culturally aware.

  • Use formal greetings (Guten Tag, Auf Wiedersehen) with strangers, in shops, at the doctor's, and with older people.
  • Use informal greetings (Hallo, Tschüss) only with friends, family, and classmates.
  • Time of day matters: Guten Morgen (morning) → Guten Tag (daytime) → Guten Abend (evening). Using the wrong one sounds odd.
  • Memory tip: "Guten" means "good" — it's the same root as "gut" (good). Morning/Tag/Abend just add the time of day.

3. Alphabet & Aussprache

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

In Germany you must spell your name and address out loud constantly — at the doctor's, on the phone, at the Einwohnermeldeamt (registration office). If you cannot spell in German, people cannot help you.

  • Most confusing letters for English speakers: J = "yot", W = "veh", V = "fau", Z = "tsett", Y = "üpsilon".
  • The four special characters Ä, Ö, Ü, ß do not exist in English — practise their sounds carefully.
  • ß sounds like "ss" and is only used after long vowels or diphthongs: Straße, heißen, Fuß.
  • Memory tip: Practise by spelling your own name, your city, and your street name in German. Do it until it becomes automatic.

4. Zahlen — Numbers

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Numbers appear in every real-life situation: prices at the supermarket, bus numbers, your flat number, phone numbers, dates, and opening hours.

  • Key rule from 21 onwards: The units digit comes BEFORE the tens — like old English "one-and-twenty." 21 = einundzwanzig (one-and-twenty), 35 = fünfunddreißig (five-and-thirty).
  • 30 is dreißig (not dreißzig) — note the spelling change from drei.
  • Phone numbers are usually read in pairs: 0176 → "null eins sieben sechs."
  • Prices: 3,50 € is spoken as "drei Euro fünfzig" (Euro is not repeated for the cents).
  • Memory tip: Every time you see a price or number in daily life, say it aloud in German. This builds number fluency faster than any drill.

5. Fragewörter — Question Words

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Question words are the backbone of every German question. The one you choose determines which case the rest of the sentence uses — so getting it right matters.

  • Wer/Wen/Wem all mean "who" but differ by case: Wer = subject (nominativ), Wen = direct object (akkusativ), Wem = indirect object (dativ).
  • Wo vs Wohin vs Woher: Wo = where (staying still), Wohin = where to (moving away), Woher = where from.
  • Compound question words: Mit wem? (with whom?), Womit? (with what?), Bei wem? (at whose place?), Seit wann? (since when?) — these are formed from preposition + question word.
  • Memory tip: Learn each question word with ONE fixed example sentence. Anchor the meaning to a real question you would actually ask.

6. Personalpronomen — Personal Pronouns

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Pronouns replace nouns so you don't repeat the same word. German has three forms for each pronoun depending on what role it plays in the sentence — subject, direct object, or indirect object.

  • Nominativ = the doer of the action: Ich lerne Deutsch.
  • Akkusativ = the direct receiver: Er sieht mich. (He sees me.)
  • Dativ = the indirect receiver: Er gibt mir das Buch. (He gives me the book.)
  • Most learners forget the Dativ column. Memorise it: mir · dir · ihm · ihr · ihm · uns · euch · ihnen · Ihnen.
  • Memory tip: "mir" sounds like "me" in English — both are dative/indirect. This overlap helps anchor the pattern.

8. Wochentage, Monate & Jahreszeiten

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Days, months, and seasons are required for appointments, dates, and scheduling — all tested in A1 exams.

  • Days of the week use am: am Montag (on Monday). They are always masculine: der Montag.
  • Months use im: im Januar (in January). Always masculine: der Januar.
  • Seasons use im: im Sommer, im Winter. Always masculine.
  • Memory tip: Three prepositions to lock in — am (days/parts of day) · im (months/seasons) · um (clock time). Remembering these three covers 90% of time expressions.

9. Zeitwörter — Time Expressions

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Time expressions tell you WHEN something happens. German word order places time information near the start or in a fixed position — getting this right makes your sentences sound natural.

  • German time order rule (TeKaMoLo): Time comes BEFORE place in a sentence. ✓ Ich gehe heute ins Kino. ✗ Ich gehe ins Kino heute.
  • Frequency adverbs go after the verb: Ich gehe oft ins Kino.
  • seit (since/for) is special — it uses present tense in German for something still ongoing: Ich lerne seit drei Monaten Deutsch. (I have been learning German for three months.)
  • Sequence words (zuerst → dann → später → zum Schluss) connect steps in a story or instructions — essential for A1 writing tasks.
  • Memory tip: Practice the TeKaMoLo order with one sentence daily: "Ich fahre [time] [reason] [how] [where]."

10. Verben: sein & haben

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

sein (to be) and haben (to have) are the two most important verbs in German. They appear in almost every sentence, and you also need them as "helper verbs" to build the past tense (Perfekt).

  • sein is irregular and must be memorised completely: bin / bist / ist / sind / seid / sind.
  • haben is nearly regular — only the du and er/sie/es forms drop the b: hast / hat.
  • Präteritum (simple past): These two verbs use war/hatte in spoken German for past events — NOT the Perfekt form. Ich war müde. (I was tired.) Ich hatte Hunger. (I was hungry.)
  • Memory tip: Practise by completing these daily: "Heute bin ich ___." "Gestern war ich ___." "Ich habe ___." "Ich hatte ___."

12. Regelmäßige Verben — Regular Verbs

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Regular verbs (also called "weak verbs") follow a completely predictable pattern. Once you memorise the 6 endings, you can conjugate hundreds of German verbs automatically.

  • The pattern: take the verb stem (infinitive minus -en), then add: -e / -st / -t / -en / -t / -en
  • Special case: verbs whose stem ends in -t or -d (like arbeiten, antworten) add an extra -e- before -st and -t for pronunciation: du arbeitest, er arbeitet — not du arbeitst.
  • The ich and er/sie/es forms are the most tested — make sure these are automatic.
  • Memory tip: Say the full conjugation of ONE verb out loud every morning: "ich lerne, du lernst, er lernt, wir lernen, ihr lernt, sie lernen." This builds the pattern in muscle memory.

13. Unregelmäßige Verben — Irregular Verbs

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Irregular verbs are called "strong verbs" in German. They change their stem vowel in the du and er/sie/es forms only — all other forms stay the same as the regular pattern.

  • Three common vowel changes: e → i (sprechen → spricht), e → ie (lesen → liest), a → ä (fahren → fährt).
  • The ich, wir, and sie/Sie forms are the same as the infinitive for most irregular verbs.
  • Key irregular verbs to master first: sein, haben, werden, wissen, fahren, sprechen, essen, nehmen, sehen.
  • Non-separable verb prefixes (be-, er-, ver-, ent-, emp-, ge-, zer-) never separate and never add ge- in Partizip II.
  • Memory tip: Group verbs by their vowel change. All e→i verbs behave the same: sprechen/spricht, essen/isst, nehmen/nimmt, geben/gibt.

14. Modalverben — Modal Verbs

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Modal verbs are used in nearly every German sentence to express ability, necessity, permission, or desire. They work differently from normal verbs — they always appear with a second verb (infinitive) at the END of the sentence.

  • Sentence pattern: Subject + MODAL (position 2) + ... + INFINITIVE (last). Example: Ich kann gut Deutsch sprechen.
  • Modal verbs have irregular ich/er forms that look the same: ich kann / er kann. The plural forms are regular.
  • The six modal verbs: können (can/ability) · müssen (must/necessity) · wollen (want) · dürfen (may/permission) · sollen (should/supposed to) · mögen/möchten (like/would like).
  • In health and formal contexts, dürfen nicht = not allowed: Man darf hier nicht rauchen.
  • Memory tip: Remember the "verb bracket" — the two verbs are like the two ends of a bridge. Modal in position 2, infinitive at the end, everything else in the middle.

15. Trennbare Verben — Separable Verbs

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Separable verbs are made of a base verb + a detachable prefix (auf-, an-, ein-, mit-, ab-, etc.). The prefix completely changes the meaning of the original verb — and in a sentence, the prefix splits off and jumps to the very end.

  • Present tense: prefix goes to the END. Ich stehe um 7 Uhr auf. (aufstehen = to get up)
  • Partizip II: ge- is inserted BETWEEN prefix and stem. aufgestanden, eingekauft, angerufen
  • With a modal verb: the separable verb stays together as an infinitive at the end. Ich muss um 7 Uhr aufstehen. (NOT: Ich muss um 7 Uhr auf stehen.)
  • Common prefixes and their meanings: auf- (up/open), an- (on/begin), ein- (in/into), aus- (out/off), zu- (closed), mit- (along), ab- (away/depart).
  • Memory tip: When you learn a new separable verb, always write an example sentence showing the prefix at the end. Never learn it without context.
Separable verbs split in main clauses: the prefix moves to the end.

27. Uhrzeit — Clock Times

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Telling the time is tested in every A1 exam and comes up constantly in real life — appointments, timetables, opening hours, and scheduling.

  • The most important rule: halb vier = 3:30, NOT 4:30. Think of it as "halfway TO four" — you are halfway through the hour before four. This is the single most common mistake English speakers make.

  • Memory tip: Write out 10 random times on paper, then say them aloud in both the official and informal forms. Do this daily until automatic.

29. Artikel: Nominativ / Akkusativ / Dativ

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

This is the most important grammar table in German. Every noun has a case depending on its role in the sentence. The article changes to signal that role. This table must be memorised until it is automatic.

  • Nominativ = the subject (who is doing the action): Der Mann kauft Brot.
  • Akkusativ = the direct object (what is directly affected): Er kauft den Apfel. — Note: only masculine (der→den) changes in Akkusativ.
  • Dativ = the indirect object (the receiver): Er gibt dem Kind das Buch. — Also used after dative prepositions (mit, von, zu, bei, aus, seit, nach, gegenüber).
  • Plural Dativ rule: add -n to the noun if it doesn't already end in -n: den Kindern, den Männern, den Frauen.
  • Memory tip: Make a colour-coded card: der/den/dem · die/die/der · das/das/dem · die/die/den. Pin it somewhere you see every day.

30. Negation: kein & nicht

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

German has two negation words and choosing the wrong one is a very common mistake. The rule is simple once you understand it.

  • kein/keine negates a noun that would have an indefinite article (ein/eine) or no article: Ich habe einen Bruder → Ich habe keinen Bruder. / Ich habe Zeit → Ich habe keine Zeit.
  • nicht negates everything else: verbs, adjectives, adverbs, or a specific noun with a definite article: Ich gehe nicht ins Kino. / Das ist nicht schön. / Ich mag nicht den roten Pullover.
  • Quick test: if you could use ein/eine in the positive version, use kein/keine for the negative. Otherwise use nicht.
  • Memory tip: kein = k + ein. It's literally "no-a" (not a/an). If the positive uses "a/an", the negative uses "kein/keine".

31. Pronomen: Akkusativ & Dativ

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Once you can replace nouns with pronouns correctly, your German sounds dramatically more natural. This section also covers a special group of verbs that always take Dativ — these are heavily tested at A1.

  • Dative-only verbs (the person is always in Dativ): helfen, gefallen, gehören, passen, danken, antworten, schaden, schmecken, stehen, gratulieren. These never take Akkusativ for the person.

33. Akkusativpräpositionen — GODFU

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

These five prepositions always trigger Akkusativ — no exceptions. Memorise the acronym GODFU and you will never need to guess the case after them again.

38. Konjunktionen — USODA

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Conjunctions join two sentences or clauses. The USODA group (coordinating conjunctions) is the easiest because they do NOT change word order — the verb stays in position 2 in the second clause. This is the key difference from subordinating conjunctions like weil/dass/wenn (which push the verb to the end).

  • und (and) — adds information. aber (but) — contrasts. oder (or) — gives a choice. denn (because) — gives a reason. sondern (but rather) — corrects a negation.
  • Key distinction: aber vs sondern. Use sondern ONLY after a negative: Er ist nicht müde, sondern krank. (He is not tired, but rather sick.) For all other contrasts, use aber.
  • Subordinating conjunctions (weil, dass, wenn, obwohl, als) send the verb to the END: Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Deutschland arbeite.
  • Memory tip: USODA = Und · Sondern · Oder · Denn · Aber. Say it like a word. These five never change word order. Everything else (weil, dass, wenn...) does.

40. Präteritum — Simple Past

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

German has two past tenses. Präteritum (simple past) is the "written" past used in books and newspapers. For everyday spoken German, you only need Präteritum for two verbs: sein → war and haben → hatte. Everything else uses Perfekt in conversation.

  • Use war/hatte in spoken German for past events: Ich war müde. (I was tired.) Ich hatte keine Zeit. (I had no time.) — NOT: Ich bin müde gewesen. in everyday speech.
  • All other verbs in conversation → use Perfekt (Section 41). Saying Er sprach instead of Er hat gesprochen sounds literary and unnatural in daily speech.
  • Time markers for the past: früher (formerly/in the past), gestern (yesterday), letzte Woche (last week), vor zwei Jahren (two years ago).
  • Memory tip: Only memorise war/hatte for speaking. The full Präteritum table (for all verbs) matters for reading, not for A1 speaking tests.

41. Perfekt & Partizip II — Present Perfect

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

Perfekt is the main past tense for speaking in German. It's a two-part structure — a helper verb (haben or sein) + the Partizip II form of the main verb at the end of the sentence.

  • Structure: Subject + haben/sein (position 2) + … + Partizip II (last). Ich habe gestern Fußball gespielt.
  • When to use sein (not haben): Movement from A to B (gehen, fahren, fliegen, laufen, kommen), change of state (werden, aufwachen), and the verbs sein/bleiben/werden themselves.
  • When to use haben: Everything else — most verbs, including those with direct objects.
  • Partizip II 5-rule system: ① Regular: ge + stem + t (gemacht, gekauft) ② Irregular: ge + changed stem + en (gegangen, gefahren) ③ Separable: prefix + ge + stem (aufgestanden) ④ -ieren verbs: stem + t, NO ge- (telefoniert) ⑤ Inseparable prefix (be-/er-/ver-): stem + t/en, NO ge- (besucht, verstanden)
  • Memory tip: For haben vs sein — ask "Does this verb move me from place to place, or change my state?" → sein. All others → haben.
The Perfekt is used for past events in spoken German.

45. Imperativ — Imperative

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

The imperative is used to give commands, instructions, and advice. You need it for giving directions, following recipes, and in doctor/pharmacy conversations. Three forms exist for the three ways to address someone in German.

  • du-form (informal, one person): Remove -en, usually drop final -e. kommen → Komm! Verbs with e→i/ie vowel change keep the change: sprechen → Sprich! · lesen → Lies! · nehmen → Nimm!
  • ihr-form (informal, group): Same as the ihr conjugation. Kommt! · Sprecht! · Lest!
  • Sie-form (formal, one or more): Infinitive + Sie, always with capital S. Kommen Sie! · Sprechen Sie! · Lesen Sie! — This is the easiest form.
  • Separable verbs split in imperative too: Machen Sie das Fenster auf! (Open the window!)
  • Memory tip: For the du-form, just say the er/sie/es form and remove the -t: spricht → Sprich! isst → Iss! nimmt → Nimm!

51. Am Telefon — On the Phone

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

63. Sentence Structure — Word Order Summary

📌 What you are learning & why it matters

German word order is rule-based and very different from English. Understanding these rules is the single biggest step towards sounding natural in German. Every sentence you produce should pass these checks.

  • The V2 rule (most important): The conjugated verb is ALWAYS in position 2. ALWAYS. Even if the sentence starts with a time word, place, or adverb — the verb is still second. Heute GEHE ich ins Kino. (NOT: Heute ich gehe...)
  • Inversion: When anything other than the subject starts the sentence, the subject and verb swap (invert): Heute gehe ICH ins Kino. — the subject "ich" moves to position 3.
  • Verb bracket (Verbalklammer): In sentences with modal verbs, Perfekt, or separable verbs, the second verb/prefix goes to the very END, creating a "bracket" around the sentence content.
  • Subordinate clauses: After weil, dass, wenn, obwohl, als — the verb goes to the absolute end: …, weil ich krank BIN.
  • TeKaMoLo order for adverbs: Time → Cause → Manner → Place. Ich fahre morgen (time) wegen der Arbeit (cause) mit dem Bus (manner) nach Berlin (place).
  • Memory tip: V2 + Verb-at-the-end = the two golden rules. Get these automatic and most German sentences will fall into place.
German word order rules at a glance.
