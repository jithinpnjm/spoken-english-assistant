1. How German Verbs Work — The Big Picture

🎯 Why does the verb ending change?

In English, verbs barely change: "I eat / you eat / he eats" — only the third person adds -s. In German, the verb ending changes for every person. This is because the ending was originally the pronoun itself — it told you who was doing the action even before separate pronouns existed. Modern German kept both the pronouns and the endings.

The good news: once you know the 6 endings, they apply to hundreds of verbs. The pattern is completely predictable for regular verbs.

📌 The German verb structure

Every German verb has: stem + ending.

  • Infinitive (dictionary form): lern-en → stem = lern-
  • Strip the -en (or -n) to get the stem: wohn-en → wohn-
  • Add the correct ending for the person/tense: ich lern-e · du lern-st · er lern-t

🧠 The -t/-d stem rule — why "arbeite" not "arbeit"

If the verb stem ends in -t or -d (arbeiten, antworten, reden, finden, warten), an extra -e- is inserted before -st and -t endings. Why? Because "arbeitst" is impossible to pronounce! The extra -e is purely a pronunciation aid — not a grammar mystery. Once you know this, you'll never forget it.

Other -t stem verbs: warten → du wartest · er wartet · antworten → du antwortest · reden → du redest

🧠 The shortcut: wir = sie/Sie = infinitive

The wir and sie/Sie forms are ALWAYS identical to the infinitive. So you only need to learn 4 forms (ich, du, er, ihr) — the other two you already know from the dictionary. This cuts your memorisation load by a third.

3. Irregular Verbs — The Three Vowel Change Groups

🎯 Why do some verbs change their vowel?

These "strong verbs" are from the oldest layer of Germanic and change their stem vowel instead of following a predictable pattern. The critical fact: the vowel change ONLY happens in du and er/sie/es. All other forms (ich, wir, ihr, sie/Sie) follow the normal regular endings — no change.

🔄 English does this too

English has the same type of verbs: sing/sang/sung, run/ran/run, drink/drank/drunk. German strong verbs change vowel in the present tense (du/er forms), not just in the past. The pattern is: same idea, different timing.

🧠 Group memory tricks

  • e→i group: "Eating, giving, taking get shorter." essen→isst, geben→gibt, nehmen→nimmt. The vowel shortens and rises.
  • e→ie group: "Seeing and reading get longer." sehen→sieht, lesen→liest. The ie makes the vowel long.
  • a→ä group: "Just add two dots to the a." fahren→fährt — the rest of the word stays the same, only the a gets an umlaut.

🧠 Memorise sein and haben with chants

sein: bin · bist · ist · sind · seid · sind

haben: habe · hast · hat · haben · habt · haben

Say these out loud, rhythmically, every morning for one week. They become completely automatic. Notice haben is almost regular — only du/er lose the -b: habe → hast/hat.

🌍 Situations using sein and haben

Ich bin Lehrerin. Er ist müde. Wir sind aus Deutschland. I am a teacher. He is tired. We are from Germany. sein for: professions (no article!), states, origins
Ich habe Hunger. Sie hat keine Zeit. Habt ihr Fragen? I'm hungry. She has no time. Do you have questions? haben for: Hunger/Durst/Angst/Zeit/Fragen — no article with these set expressions
Wo warst du gestern? — Ich war zu Hause. Where were you yesterday? — I was at home. war/waren = Präteritum of sein. Use this in speech, not "bin gewesen".

5. Modal Verbs — Meaning, Conjugation & When to Use Each

🎯 What modal verbs do

Modal verbs express your attitude towards an action — whether it is possible, necessary, permitted, or desired. They NEVER appear alone — they always team up with an infinitive that describes the actual action. This creates the famous two-verb structure.

The 6 Modal Verbs — Full Conjugation

🧠 Pattern: ich = er/sie/es for modals (no ending in 3rd person singular!)

Unlike regular verbs where er/sie/es always gets -t, modal verbs use the same form for ich AND er/sie/es. This is a special feature of all modal verbs: ich kann = er kann, ich muss = er muss, ich darf = er darf. Never add -t to a modal in 3rd person!

Meaning & Nuance — When to use which

ModalCore meaningWhen to useExample
könnenability / possibilityPhysical ability OR logical possibility. Also polite requests.Ich kann schwimmen. / Kann ich helfen?
müssenmust / have toStrong inner necessity — cannot avoid. Also logical conclusion.Ich muss jetzt gehen. / Das muss falsch sein.
wollenwant toStrong personal desire. Plans and intentions. Direct — can sound demanding in requests.Ich will Arzt werden. / Er will nicht kommen.
dürfenmay / allowed toPermission given by someone else. Prohibition in negative.Darf ich rauchen? / Man darf hier nicht parken.
sollenshould / supposed toObligation from OUTSIDE — someone else requires or expects it.Du sollst nicht lügen. / Der Arzt sagt, ich soll ruhen.
möchtenwould like toPolite wish or desire. ALWAYS use in shops, restaurants, requests. Never sounds demanding.Ich möchte einen Kaffee, bitte. / Wir möchten zahlen.

🔄 müssen vs sollen — the one distinction that matters most

  • müssen = internal necessity: I feel/know I MUST. Ich muss schlafen. (I must sleep — I feel exhausted.)
  • sollen = external obligation: SOMEONE ELSE says I should. Ich soll schlafen. (The doctor says I should sleep.)
  • English equivalent: "I must go" (inner compulsion) vs "I'm supposed to go" (arranged by others).

🔄 wollen vs möchten — politeness level

  • Ich will einen Kaffee! — sounds like a demand. Rude in shops.
  • Ich möchte einen Kaffee, bitte. — polite and natural. Always use this in service situations.
  • Rule: Use möchten when talking to strangers, in shops, restaurants, offices. Use wollen for personal plans and intentions.

6. The Verb Bracket — How Two-Verb Structures Work

🎯 The fundamental sentence pattern with modal verbs

When a modal verb appears, it creates a "verb bracket" (Verbalklammer): the modal verb takes position 2 (as always), and the infinitive jumps to the very last position. Everything else fills the middle.

Ich  [kann]  heute Abend mit dir ins Kino  [gehen].
Er  [muss]  morgen früh um 6 Uhr  [aufstehen].
Darf  [darf]  ich hier  [rauchen]?

🧠 The bracket visual — always see it this way

Visualise a big bracket [       ] around the sentence content. The modal sits at the opening bracket (position 2). The infinitive sits at the closing bracket (end). The whole sentence content is trapped inside. Nothing can escape past the infinitive.

❌ Most common verb bracket mistakes

  • Ich kann sprechen gut Deutsch.
  • Ich kann gut Deutsch sprechen. ← infinitive to absolute end
  • Ich muss gehen jetzt.
  • Ich muss jetzt gehen. ← "jetzt" stays in middle; infinitive at end

7. Separable Verbs — Prefix Splits Off

🎯 Why does the prefix move?

German builds new verbs by adding prefixes to existing verbs. In a main clause (normal statement), the prefix "flies" to the end of the sentence — because German puts the main verb in position 2 and puts completions (what kind of action) at the end. The prefix IS the completion.

❌ Common dative verb mistake

Das gefällt mich. → Das gefällt mir.
These verbs always need the dative pronoun (mir/dir/ihm/ihr/uns/euch/ihnen), NOT the accusative (mich/dich/ihn/sie).

11. Common Verb Mistakes

❌ Top verb mistakes at A1

  • Er kann schwimmen gut. → Er kann gut schwimmen. ← infinitive ALWAYS goes to absolute end
  • Ich bin ein Lehrer. → Ich bin Lehrer. ← no article with professions after sein/werden/bleiben
  • Er ist hat krank. → Er ist krank. ← never combine sein and haben like this
  • Du musst nicht gehen (meaning "you don't have to") → Du brauchst nicht zu gehen. ← müssen nicht = MUST NOT (prohibition!)
  • Ich will einen Kaffee (in a café) → Ich möchte einen Kaffee, bitte. ← wollen sounds demanding; use möchten
  • Er weiß nicht die Antwort. → Er weiß die Antwort nicht. ← nicht at end (or before the specific negated element)
  • Das gefällt mich. → Das gefällt mir. ← gefallen takes Dativ: mich → mir
  • Ich habe Arzt gegangen. → Ich bin zum Arzt gegangen. ← gehen uses sein in Perfekt; and: zum Arzt (not just Arzt)
