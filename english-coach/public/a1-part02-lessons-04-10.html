Lesson 04

Numbers 20–100 — Zahlen Teil 2

Once you know the tens (zwanzig, dreißig…), German numbers follow a predictable compound pattern: units come before tens, joined by "und". This is the opposite of English.

Grammar Rules
1. Tens from 20–90 are: zwanzig, dreißig, vierzig, fünfzig, sechzig, siebzig, achtzig, neunzig. Note dreißig (not dreizehn) ends in -ßig, not -zig.
2. Compound numbers: units come FIRST, then "und", then tens. Example: 21 = einundzwanzig (one-and-twenty). This is the opposite of English "twenty-one".
3. 100 = hundert (or einhundert). Numbers above 100 add on after: 101 = hundertundeins, 110 = hundertzehn.
4. In shops and markets, prices are given as "X Euro Y (Cent)". Example: Das kostet drei Euro fünfzig. (That costs 3.50 euros.)
Formula
21–99 compound: [units] + und + [tens] → einundzwanzig, dreiundvierzig, siebenundneunzig
Price formula: Das kostet [amount] Euro [cents]. → Das kostet zwei Euro zwanzig.

Cultural Note

At German supermarket checkouts, the cashier will tell you the total and you are expected to pay quickly and bag your own groceries. Prices are spoken clearly: "Das macht drei Euro siebenundfünfzig" (That makes €3.57). Germans often pay cash; card terminals are common now but smaller shops may still prefer Bargeld (cash). It is polite to have the correct change ready.

Lesson 05

Alphabet — Das Alphabet

The German alphabet has 26 standard letters plus 4 special characters: ä, ö, ü, and ß. Knowing how to spell your name and understand spellings is essential for forms and phone calls.

Grammar Rules
1. Most vowels: A (ah), E (eh), I (ee), O (oh), U (oo). The umlauts ä/ö/ü modify the base vowel with two dots and change the sound.
2. J is pronounced like English "y" (as in "yes"). Never like English "j".
3. V is pronounced like "f" in most German words (Vogel = FOH-gel). W is pronounced like English "v" (Wasser = VAS-ser).
4. Extra letters: Ä = "ae", Ö = "oe", Ü = "ue", ß = "ss" (sharp S / Eszett — used after long vowels and diphthongs).
5. When spelling your name on a phone/form, use the standard phrase: "Ich buchstabiere: A wie Anton, B wie Berta…" (I spell: A as in Anton…)
Formula
Spelling phrase: Ich buchstabiere: [letter] wie [reference word] → M wie Martha, U wie Ulrich
Asking how to spell: Wie schreibt man das? / Können Sie das buchstabieren?

Lesson 06

Introducing Yourself — Sich vorstellen

Introducing yourself is the foundation of every German conversation. Learn the key verbs heißen, kommen, wohnen and use them to build your personal introduction.

Grammar Rules
1. "Ich heiße" means "my name is" — literally "I am called". Use heißen for names. Never say "Ich bin [name]" in a formal first introduction (though informal use is fine).
2. "Ich komme aus" + [country/city] = I come from. "Aus" requires the dative case, but at A1 level, country names typically have no article (aus Deutschland, aus Indien).
3. "Ich wohne in" + [city/place] = I live in. "In" also triggers dative, but again, city names have no article at this level.
4. Formal vs informal: with strangers/adults, use "Sie" forms (Wie heißen Sie? Woher kommen Sie?). With peers/friends, use "du" forms (Wie heißt du? Woher kommst du?).
Formula
Ich heiße [name]. Ich komme aus [country]. Ich wohne in [city].
Formal question: Wie heißen Sie? Woher kommen Sie? Wo wohnen Sie?
Informal question: Wie heißt du? Woher kommst du? Wo wohnst du?

Lesson 07

Getting to Know Someone — Jemanden kennenlernen

After introducing yourself, the conversation continues with questions about languages, interests, and background. Learn the key question words and the verb "sprechen".

Grammar Rules
1. German question words (W-Fragen): Wer (who), Was (what), Wo (where), Woher (from where), Wohin (to where), Wie (how), Wann (when), Warum (why), Welche/r/s (which).
2. "Sprechen" means to speak. Conjugation: ich spreche, du sprichst, er/sie/es spricht, wir sprechen, ihr sprecht, sie/Sie sprechen. Note the stem change: e→i in du/er/sie.
3. When asking which languages someone speaks: "Welche Sprachen sprechen Sie?" / "Welche Sprachen sprichst du?"
4. To express partial ability: "ein bisschen" (a little) + language. Example: Ich spreche ein bisschen Deutsch. (I speak a little German.)
Formula
Question: [W-word] + [verb] + [subject] + [rest]? → Woher kommst du?
Language ability: Ich spreche [language] / ein bisschen [language] / kein [language].

Lesson 08

How Are You? — Wie geht's?

"Wie geht's?" is one of the most common phrases in daily German life. Learn how to ask and answer naturally, and understand the grammar of "mir geht es".

Grammar Rules
1. "Wie geht es Ihnen?" (formal) / "Wie geht es dir?" / "Wie geht's?" (informal contractions). All mean "How are you?"
2. The answer uses dative: "Mir geht es gut" (It goes well to me = I am doing well). "mir" = to me (dative of ich); "dir" = to you (dative of du); "Ihnen" = to you (formal dative).
3. Common responses: sehr gut (very good), gut (good), es geht (so-so), nicht so gut (not so good), schlecht (bad), müde (tired), super (great).
4. After answering, return the question: "Und Ihnen?" (formal) / "Und dir?" (informal). This is standard German politeness.
5. "auch" means "also/too" — "Mir geht es auch gut." (I'm also doing well.)
Formula
Question: Wie geht es [Ihnen / dir]? → Wie geht es Ihnen? (formal)
Answer: Mir geht es [adjective]. Danke. Und [Ihnen / dir]?
Short answer: [adjective] + danke. → Gut, danke! / Super, danke!

Lesson 09

Sentence Structure Part 1 — Satzbau Teil 1

German sentences follow a specific word order. The most important rule: the verb is always in second position in a main clause. Learn this and you will avoid the most common beginner mistakes.

Grammar Rules
1. In a standard German sentence, the verb is always in POSITION 2 (the second element). The subject is usually in position 1, but can move.
2. Subject–Verb–Object (SVO) is the default word order: Ich trinke Kaffee. (I drink coffee.)
3. When another element starts the sentence (time, place, etc.), the verb stays in position 2 and the subject moves to position 3: Heute trinke ich Kaffee. (Today I drink coffee.)
4. The object (what is affected by the verb) goes after the verb in simple sentences. At A1, focus on building correct basic SVO sentences.
Formula
Standard: [Subject] + [Verb (pos.2)] + [Object/rest] → Ich kaufe das Buch.
Inverted: [Time/place] + [Verb (pos.2)] + [Subject] + [Object] → Heute kaufe ich das Buch.

Lesson 10

Sentence Structure Part 2 — Satzbau Teil 2

Build on basic SVO structure by adding time expressions, frequency words, and place adverbs. These expand your sentences naturally.

Grammar Rules
1. Time adverbs typically go at the start of a sentence (triggering inversion) or right after the verb: Ich gehe heute ins Kino. / Heute gehe ich ins Kino.
2. Frequency adverbs (oft, immer, manchmal, nie) go after the verb (and after the subject if inverted): Ich arbeite oft am Wochenende. / Er spielt manchmal Tennis.
3. TeKaMoLo rule: Time (Temporal) > Cause (Kausal) > Manner (Modal) > Place (Lokal). When multiple adverbials appear, this is the preferred order.
4. "Am Wochenende" = on the weekend (am = an + dem). "Am" is always used with days and parts of day: am Montag, am Morgen.
Formula
Time first: [Time] + [Verb] + [Subject] + [frequency/manner] + [place] → Heute spiele ich oft im Park.
Standard with time: [Subject] + [Verb] + [Time] + [frequency] + [object/place] → Ich arbeite oft am Wochenende.
