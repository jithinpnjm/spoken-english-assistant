Overview — The 3 Time Groups

English tenses organise time into three groups. Master these and you can express almost anything.

PAST

Actions finished or happening in the past.

  • Past Simple
  • Past Continuous
  • Present Perfect
PRESENT

Habits, facts, what is happening now, or just finished.

  • Present Simple
  • Present Continuous
  • Present Perfect
FUTURE

Plans, predictions, spontaneous decisions.

  • will
  • going to
  • Present Continuous
Why tenses matter for fluency Using the wrong tense changes your meaning entirely. "I eat lunch" (habit) vs "I am eating lunch" (right now) vs "I have eaten lunch" (done, so don't bring food) — three different messages. Native speakers use tense shifts automatically; learners need to make them conscious first.
Memory anchor Think of tenses as three cameras: the past camera looks back, the present camera looks at now, the future camera looks forward. Each has a zoom setting (simple, continuous, perfect).

Most common mistake — the missing -s
✗ He work in a bank.
✓ He works in a bank.

The third-person -s is small but critical. Native speakers notice its absence immediately. Practise by writing 10 sentences about someone else every day.

Stative verbs — NEVER use with -ing

These verbs describe states, not actions. They don't "happen"; they just exist.

CategoryStative verbs
Mental statesknow, believe, think (opinion), understand, remember, forget, mean
Emotionslove, hate, like, prefer, want, wish, need, desire
Sensessee, hear, smell, taste, feel (perception)
Possessionhave, own, belong, possess, contain
Otherseem, appear, look (seem), cost, weigh, include
✗ I am knowing the answer.
know = stative, can't be continuous
✓ I know the answer.

Some verbs change meaning: I think (opinion) vs I am thinking (considering) · I have a car (possession) vs I am having lunch (experience).

Present Perfect

have / has + past participle

Form

SubjectPositiveNegativeQuestion
I / You / We / Theyhave workedhave not workedHave you worked?
He / She / Ithas workedhas not workedHas she worked?

Uses

Life experience (no specific time)
I have visited Paris.
Sometime in my life — when? doesn't matter
Result of a past action
She has broken her leg.
Result visible now — she can't walk
Recent past (just/already/yet)
I have just finished the report.
Very recent, relevant to now
Unfinished period (since/for)
He has lived here for 10 years.
Started in past, still true now

Memory trick — Present Perfect = past action with present relevance Ask yourself: "Does this matter NOW?" If yes → Present Perfect. If it's just a historical record → Past Simple.
I have lost my keys. (I can't open the door now — present relevance)
I lost my keys last Tuesday. (just reporting a past event)

Rule: Past Simple needs a time marker or context
I ate breakfast at 8. (clear time → Past Simple)
I have eaten breakfast. (no time → Present Perfect, result is I'm not hungry now)
Regular verb spelling traps
  • Short vowel + consonant → double: stop → stopped, plan → planned
  • Consonant + -e → just add -d: love → loved, arrive → arrived
  • Consonant + -y → change to ied: study → studied, cry → cried
  • Vowel + -y → add -ed: play → played, enjoy → enjoyed

Past Continuous

was / were + verb+ing

Form

SubjectPositiveNegativeQuestion
I / He / She / Itwas workingwas not workingWas he working?
You / We / Theywere workingwere not workingWere they working?

Uses

  • Background action in progress when another event happened
  • Two simultaneous actions in the past
  • Setting the scene in a story

The classic pattern: background + interruption

Past Continuous (background) + when + Past Simple (interruption)
I was cooking dinner when the phone rang.
Cooking = background. Rang = interruption.
She was walking home when it started raining.
Walking = background. Started = interruption.
while vs when — the key distinction
WordUseExample
whilejoins two continuous (ongoing) actionsI was reading while she was cooking.
whenintroduces a short interrupting eventI was reading when she called.
✓ While I was sleeping, he arrived. (while + continuous ✓)
✓ When I was sleeping, he arrived. (when + continuous is also fine for background)
✗ While I slept, he arrived. (while needs continuous for background)
Memory trick Past Continuous = the movie was already playing. Past Simple = someone walked in. The camera was rolling (was + -ing) when the door opened (past simple).

Future — will / going to / Present Continuous

English has three main ways to express the future. Choosing the right one depends on whether the plan existed before and how certain you are.

will

Spontaneous decisions, predictions, promises, offers.

I'll carry that for you.
Offer, decided right now
It will rain tomorrow.
Prediction (no evidence)
going to

Prior plans, intentions, predictions based on evidence.

We're going to visit Rome in June.
Plan already made
Look at those clouds — it's going to rain.
Prediction based on visible evidence
Present Continuous

Arranged events (time & place confirmed, in the diary).

I'm meeting the dentist at 10.
Appointment already booked
They're flying to Dubai on Friday.
Tickets bought, confirmed

Decision tree — which future to use?

Did you decide just now, spontaneously?
YES → use will — "The phone is ringing." "I'll get it."
↓ NO
Is it a confirmed arrangement (date/time/place fixed)?
YES → use Present Continuous — "I'm seeing the doctor at 3."
↓ NO
Is there evidence right now, or is it a prior intention/plan?
YES → use going to — "She's going to study medicine." / "Look out — it's going to fall!"
↓ NONE of the above
General prediction or neutral future statement?
→ use will — "Technology will change everything."

Forms

PositiveNegativeQuestion
willI will go / I'll goI will not go / I won't goWill you go?
going toI am going to goI am not going to goAre you going to go?
Pres. Cont.I am goingI am not goingAre you going?
Common mistake
✗ I will to call you later. (no "to" after will)
✓ I will call you later.
✗ She is go to study medicine.
✓ She is going to study medicine.

Conditionals

Conditionals talk about conditions and their results. The "if" clause states the condition; the main clause states the result.

Zero Conditional — General truth / fact

If + present simple, [main clause] present simple
If you heat water to 100°C, it boils.
Always true — scientific fact
If I don't sleep enough, I feel terrible.
Always true for me — personal habit
Use: Facts, laws of nature, things that are always true. "If" can be replaced with "when" without changing meaning.

First Conditional — Real / possible situation

If + present simple, will + base verb
If it rains tomorrow, we will cancel the picnic.
Realistic possibility in the future
If you study hard, you will pass the exam.
Real possibility, likely to happen
Use: Real conditions — the speaker believes it could genuinely happen.

Second Conditional — Unreal / hypothetical situation

If + past simple, would + base verb
If I had a million dollars, I would travel the world.
I don't have a million dollars — imaginary
If she were the president, she would change the law.
She isn't the president — hypothetical
Note on "were" In formal/written English, use were for all subjects in Second Conditional: "If I were you…" (not "was"). In everyday speech, "was" is common but "were" is more correct.

✗ If it will rain, we will cancel the picnic.

Active vs Passive Voice

In active sentences, the subject does the action. In passive sentences, the subject receives the action.

Passive = be (in any tense) + past participle

Tense comparison: active vs passive

TenseActivePassive
Present SimpleThey make cars here.Cars are made here.
Past SimpleSomeone stole my wallet.My wallet was stolen.
Present PerfectThey have built a bridge.A bridge has been built.
Future (will)They will announce the results.The results will be announced.
Present Cont.They are repairing the road.The road is being repaired.

When to use passive

Use passive when…
  • The doer is unknown: My bike was stolen.
  • The doer is unimportant: The road is being repaired.
  • The action/result is the focus: The report was submitted on time.
  • Formal/academic/news writing (impersonal tone).
Avoid passive when…
  • Active is clearer and more direct.
  • You want to show who is responsible.
  • The sentence becomes awkward or very long.

Common passive sentences to memorise

English is spoken by over a billion people.
The report was written by the marketing team.
This bridge was built in 1902.
Mistakes were made.
The meeting has been postponed.
You are requested to fill in this form.
Adding the agent — "by" Include the agent (doer) with "by" only when it adds useful information.
Romeo and Juliet was written by Shakespeare. (agent important — who wrote it)
The window was broken. (no "by" needed — doer unknown or irrelevant)

Common Tense Mistakes

These are the most frequent errors made by Indian and international English learners, with the correct forms and explanations.

#IncorrectCorrectRule
1 I am knowing the answer. I know the answer. "know" is stative — no continuous
2 I am understanding now. I understand now. "understand" is stative
3 I am liking this movie. I like this movie. "like" is stative — emotion verb
4 I have been here since 3 years. I have been here for 3 years. for + duration; since + point in time
5 I did not went there. I did not go there. did + base verb (not past form)
6 She don't know the answer. She doesn't know the answer. he/she/it → does not (doesn't)
7 I have met him yesterday. I met him yesterday. "yesterday" = specific time → Past Simple, not Present Perfect
8 If I will go, I will call you. If I go, I will call you. No "will" in if-clause of First Conditional
9 He is my best friend since school. He has been my best friend since school. Ongoing situation from past to now → Present Perfect
10 I am working here since 2020. I have been working here since 2020. Ongoing action from past to now → Present Perfect Continuous
since vs for — the single most confused pair
  • since = from a point in time to now: since 2020 / since Monday / since I was a child
  • for = a duration / length of time: for 3 years / for two weeks / for a long time
I have lived here for 5 years. (duration = 5 years)
I have lived here since 2019. (point in time = 2019)
Quick self-check habit Before speaking or writing, ask yourself three questions:
  1. When does this happen? (past / now / future)
  2. Is it a state or an action? (states → simple; actions → can be continuous)
  3. Is there a time word? (specific time → Past Simple; no time / relevant now → Present Perfect)
