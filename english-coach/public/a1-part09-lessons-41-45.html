Lesson 41

war / hatte — Simple Past of sein/haben — Präteritum von sein und haben

To talk about the past in spoken German, we almost always use a complex two-verb structure (the Present Perfect). However, there are two massive exceptions: 'sein' (to be) and 'haben' (to have). For these two verbs, Germans use the Simple Past even in casual conversation. Saying 'I was sick' or 'I had time' requires memorizing 'war' and 'hatte'.

Grammar Rules
'war' is the past form of 'sein' (I was). 'hatte' is the past form of 'haben' (I had).
Just like with modal verbs, the 'ich' form and the 'er/sie/es' form are completely identical (ich war / er war).
Do NOT add a '-t' to the 'er/sie/es' form. It stays exactly the same as 'ich'.
Use 'war' to describe past states or locations (I was tired, I was in Berlin).
Use 'hatte' to describe past possessions or physical feelings (I had a car, I had a headache).
Formula
Past State: Subject + war(en) + Adjective/Place. (Ich war müde.)
Past Possession: Subject + hatte(n) + Noun. (Ich hatte Zeit.)

Model Sentences
Gestern war ich krank.
Yesterday I was sick.
Gestern = Yesterday (position 1) | war = was (position 2) | ich = I (position 3) | krank = sick → Inversion rule still applies with 'war'.
Letzte Woche hatte ich viel Stress.
Last week I had a lot of stress.
Letzte Woche = Last week (position 1, time block) | hatte = had (position 2) | ich = I (position 3) | viel Stress = a lot of stress → Time block in position 1 forces subject to position 3.
Wo warst du am Wochenende?
Where were you on the weekend?
Wo = Where | warst = were (past of 'sein' for 'du') | du = you | am Wochenende = on the weekend → 'du' form adds '-st': war + st = warst.
Früher hatten wir kein Geld, aber wir waren glücklich.
In the past we had no money, but we were happy.
Früher = In the past | hatten = had (wir form) | wir = we | kein Geld = no money | aber = but | wir = we | waren = were (wir form) | glücklich = happy → 'wir' form adds '-en' to both.
Mein Vater hatte ein altes Auto. Es war sehr laut.
My father had an old car. It was very loud.
Mein Vater = My father | hatte = had ('er' form — identical to 'ich' form, no extra '-t') | ein altes Auto = an old car | Es = It | war = was | sehr laut = very loud → 'er' form never gets a '-t'.
Hattet ihr einen guten Flug?
Did you all have a good flight?
Hattet = Had (verb first for yes/no question, 'ihr' form) | ihr = you all | einen guten Flug = a good flight → 'ihr' form of 'hatte' is 'hattet'.

Common Mistakes
❌ Letztes Jahr ich war in München.
✓ Letztes Jahr war ich in München.
Inversion rule still applies! 'Letztes Jahr' takes Position 1, so the verb 'war' MUST stay in Position 2, pushing 'ich' to Position 3.
❌ Mein Bruder wart hier.
✓ Mein Bruder war hier.
Adding a '-t' to the 3rd person singular. The 'er/sie/es' form of 'war' is just 'war' — no '-t' is ever added.
❌ Ich war Hunger.
✓ Ich hatte Hunger.
Translating 'I was hungry' literally. In German, you 'have' hunger. Therefore, in the past tense, you 'had' hunger (Ich hatte Hunger).
Lesson 42

Non-Separable Verbs — Untrennbare Verben

In Lesson 37, you learned about verbs that split apart (like 'aufstehen'). However, there is a distinct family of prefixes in German that are permanently glued to their base verbs. They never separate, no matter what. Memorizing these specific 'superglue' prefixes will save you from breaking words that are meant to stay whole.

Grammar Rules
There are 8 prefixes that NEVER separate from the verb: be-, emp-, ent-, er-, ge-, miss-, ver-, zer-.
A common mnemonic to remember them is: 'Be-emp-ent-er-ge-miss-ver-zer'.
Because they do not separate, you treat them exactly like completely normal verbs. The whole word takes Position 2.
Example: 'verstehen' (to understand). You say 'Ich verstehe das' (I understand that). You NEVER say 'Ich stehe das ver'.
Formula
Subject (1) + Non-Separable Verb (2) + Object/Rest.
Ich (1) + besuche (2) + meinen Vater (3).

Model Sentences
Ich verstehe dieses Wort nicht.
I do not understand this word.
Ich = I | verstehe = understand (the 'ver-' prefix stays glued — it never moves to the end) | dieses Wort = this word | nicht = not → No splitting. The whole verb stays in position 2.
Besuchst du am Wochenende deine Eltern?
Are you visiting your parents on the weekend?
Besuchst = Visit (the 'be-' prefix stays glued) | du = you | am Wochenende = on the weekend | deine Eltern = your parents → Conjugate the full word, nothing separates.
Wir bekommen heute Besuch.
We are getting visitors today.
Wir = We | bekommen = get/receive (the 'be-' prefix stays glued) | heute = today | Besuch = visit (noun) → Watch out: 'bekommen' means 'receive', not 'become'!
Der Mann verkauft sein altes Fahrrad.
The man is selling his old bicycle.
Der Mann = The man | verkauft = sells (the 'ver-' prefix stays glued) | sein altes Fahrrad = his old bicycle → Whole verb in position 2 as normal.
Erzählen Sie mir von Ihrem Beruf.
Tell me about your profession. (Formal command)
Erzählen Sie = Tell (formal command with 'er-' prefix, stays glued) | mir = to me | von Ihrem Beruf = about your profession → Even in commands, the prefix never separates.
Ich vergesse immer meinen Schlüssel.
I always forget my key.
Ich = I | vergesse = forget (the 'ver-' prefix stays glued) | immer = always | meinen Schlüssel = my key (masculine direct object) → Nothing moves to the end.

Answer: Er verkauft sein Auto.
Common Mistakes
❌ Ich suche meine Oma be.
✓ Ich besuche meine Oma.
Treating a 'be-' verb as separable. The prefix 'be-' is permanently glued. It never goes to the end of the sentence.
❌ Ich bekomme nach Berlin.
✓ Ich komme nach Berlin.
False friend! 'Bekommen' sounds like 'become' in English, but it actually means 'to receive/get'. If you want to say you are coming to Berlin, just use 'kommen'.
❌ Wir zahlen die Rechnung be.
✓ Wir bezahlen die Rechnung.
Splitting an inseparable verb. 'Bezahlen' (to pay) stays whole in Position 2.
Lesson 43

Talking About Your Health — Über Gesundheit sprechen

If you need to call in sick to work or visit a doctor in Germany, you must know how to describe where you hurt. Germans use two very specific structures to express pain: the noun-based 'I have...-pain' (Ich habe Kopfschmerzen) and the verb-based 'My... hurts me' (Mir tut der Kopf weh). Mastering these ensures you get the right help quickly.

Grammar Rules
Structure 1: Combine the body part + 'schmerzen' (pains). Use the verb 'haben'. (Ich habe Kopfschmerzen = I have head-pains).
Structure 2: The separable verb 'wehtun' (to hurt).
In Structure 2, the body part is the subject (doing the hurting), and you are the receiver (use 'mir').
Therefore: [Body part] + tut + mir + weh. (Der Kopf tut mir weh).
If plural body parts hurt (like teeth or eyes), use the plural verb 'tun': [Plural body parts] + tun + mir + weh.
Formula
Structure 1: Ich habe + [Body Part]schmerzen.
Structure 2 (Singular): Mein [Body Part] + tut + mir + weh.
Structure 2 (Plural): Meine [Plural Body Parts] + tun + mir + weh.
Body PartStructure 1 (haben)Structure 2 (wehtun)
der Kopf (head)Ich habe Kopfschmerzen.Der Kopf tut mir weh.
der Bauch (stomach)Ich habe Bauchschmerzen.Mein Bauch tut mir weh.
der Zahn (tooth)Ich habe Zahnschmerzen.Mein Zahn tut mir weh.
die Augen (eyes - plural)Ich habe Augenschmerzen.Meine Augen tun mir weh.

Model Sentences
Ich bin krank. Ich habe Kopfschmerzen und Fieber.
I am sick. I have a headache and a fever.
Ich bin krank = I am sick | Ich habe = I have | Kopfschmerzen = head-pains (body part + schmerzen combined into one word) | und = and | Fieber = fever → German builds compound words for pain.
Mein Rücken tut mir sehr weh.
My back hurts me a lot.
Mein Rücken = My back (the subject — it is doing the hurting) | tut = does (verb in position 2) | mir = to me (the receiver) | sehr = very | weh = hurt (the prefix, sent to the end) → 'wehtun' splits: 'tut' stays in position 2, 'weh' goes to the end.
Meine Augen tun mir weh. Ich brauche eine Brille.
My eyes hurt. I need glasses.
Meine Augen = My eyes (plural subject) | tun = do (plural verb — note 'tun' not 'tut') | mir = to me | weh = hurt (at the end) | Ich brauche = I need | eine Brille = glasses → Plural body parts use 'tun' instead of 'tut'.
Wo tut es Ihnen weh, Herr Müller?
Where does it hurt you, Mr. Müller? (Formal)
Wo = Where | tut = does | es = it (subject) | Ihnen = to you (formal) | weh = hurt (at the end) | Herr Müller = address → Doctor's standard question to a formal patient.
Gute Besserung!
Get well soon!
Gute = Good | Besserung = bettering/recovery → A fixed phrase. Just memorize it as one whole expression.

Answer: tut (one tooth = singular)
Meine Ohren (ears) ___ mir weh. (tut/tun)
Answer: tun (multiple ears = plural)
Translate using 'haben' + Schmerzen: I have a sore throat. (der Hals)
Answer: Ich habe Halsschmerzen.
Translate: Do you have a headache? (der Kopf)

Common Mistakes
❌ Ich bin Kopfschmerzen.
✓ Ich habe Kopfschmerzen.
You cannot BE a headache. In German, pain is something you possess (haben). Always use 'Ich habe'.
❌ Mein Kopf wehtut.
✓ Mein Kopf tut weh.
Forgetting that 'wehtun' is a separable verb. 'tut' is the conjugated part that must sit in Position 2. 'weh' is the prefix that flies to the end.
❌ Mein Bein tut mich weh.
✓ Mein Bein tut mir weh.
Using 'mich' (direct object form) instead of 'mir' (indirect object form). The pain is happening TO you, so it requires 'mir'.
Lesson 44

Past Perfect 1: Sentence Structure — Das Perfekt: Satzbau

In English, we have the Simple Past ('I played') and the Present Perfect ('I have played'). In spoken German, the Simple Past is almost entirely dead (except for 'war/hatte'). For 90% of daily conversations about the past, Germans use the 'Perfekt'. It requires a helper verb in Position 2 and a special past-tense word (Partizip II) at the absolute end of the sentence.

Grammar Rules
The Perfekt consists of two parts: a helper verb (haben or sein) AND the Partizip II (the past participle, e.g., 'gemacht').
The helper verb goes in Position 2 and is conjugated normally to match the subject (ich habe, du hast).
The Partizip II NEVER changes its spelling. It flies to the VERY END of the sentence.
This creates the famous 'Sentence Bracket'. Everything else (time, object, place) gets squeezed between the helper verb and the Partizip II.
Formula
Subject (1) + helper verb [haben/sein] (2) + [Time/Object/Rest] + Partizip II (End).
Example: Ich (1) + habe (2) + gestern Pizza + gegessen (End).

Model Sentences
Ich habe gestern ein Buch gekauft.
I bought a book yesterday.
Ich = I | habe = have (helper verb, position 2) | gestern = yesterday | ein Buch = a book (everything in the middle) | gekauft = bought (Partizip II sent to the very end) → The bracket: helper verb on the left, Partizip II on the right.
Was hast du am Wochenende gemacht?
What did you do on the weekend?
Was = What (W-word, position 1) | hast = have (helper verb, position 2) | du = you | am Wochenende = on the weekend (middle) | gemacht = done (Partizip II at the very end) → W-question uses same bracket structure.
Wir haben schon Pizza bestellt.
We have already ordered pizza.
Wir = We | haben = have (helper verb) | schon = already | Pizza = pizza (middle) | bestellt = ordered (Partizip II at the end) → Note: 'bestellen' has a permanently glued 'be-' prefix, so the Partizip II has no 'ge-'.
Er hat mir eine Geschichte erzählt.
He told me a story.
Er = He | hat = has (helper verb) | mir = to me | eine Geschichte = a story (middle) | erzählt = told (Partizip II at the end, no 'ge-' because 'er-' prefix is permanently glued) → Non-separable verbs don't get a 'ge-' prefix.
Heute Morgen habe ich Deutsch gelernt.
This morning I studied German.
Heute Morgen = This morning (position 1) | habe = have (helper verb, position 2) | ich = I (position 3) | Deutsch = German | gelernt = learned (Partizip II at the very end) → Inversion applies: helper verb stays in position 2.

Common Mistakes
❌ Ich habe gemacht meine Hausaufgaben.
✓ Ich habe meine Hausaufgaben gemacht.
A classic English-speaker mistake. You cannot place the main action verb right after the helper verb. 'gemacht' MUST go to the very end.
❌ Wir haben gekauftet ein Brot.
✓ Wir haben ein Brot gekauft.
Do not add present tense endings to the Partizip II! 'gekauft' is the final, frozen form. It never changes.
❌ Gestern ich habe gearbeitet.
✓ Gestern habe ich gearbeitet.
Forgetting the Inversion rule. If 'gestern' is in Position 1, the helper verb 'habe' stays in Position 2, pushing 'ich' to Position 3.
Lesson 45

Past Perfect 2: haben or sein as helper — Das Perfekt: haben oder sein als Hilfsverb

In the last lesson, we used 'haben' as the helper verb for the past tense. But wait—some verbs refuse to use 'haben'! For a very specific group of verbs, you must use 'sein' (to be) as your helper verb. This sounds strange to English ears ('I am gone' instead of 'I have gone'), but the logic behind which verbs use 'sein' is actually very strict and easy to learn.

Grammar Rules
Use 'sein' (bin, bist, ist, sind, seid) as the helper verb for verbs that express MOTION from point A to point B (e.g., gehen, fahren, fliegen, kommen).
Use 'sein' for verbs that express a CHANGE OF STATE (e.g., aufwachen - to wake up, sterben - to die).
Use 'sein' for two major exceptions: 'bleiben' (to stay - geblieben) and 'passieren' (to happen - passiert).
For ALL OTHER VERBS (90% of verbs, including verbs that take a direct object), use 'haben'.
Formula
Motion/Change of state: Subject + sein (conjugated) + ... + Partizip II (End).
Example: Ich (1) + bin (2) + nach Berlin + gefahren (End).
Helper VerbWhen to useExamples (Partizip II)

sein (bin/ist...)Change of state / Exceptionsaufgewacht (woken up), geblieben (stayed), passiert (happened)
haben (habe/hat...)No motion / Has a direct objectgemacht (done), gekauft (bought), gelesen (read), geschlafen (slept)

Model Sentences
Ich bin gestern nach Berlin gefahren.
I drove to Berlin yesterday.
Ich = I | bin = am (helper 'sein' for motion from A to B) | gestern = yesterday | nach Berlin = to Berlin | gefahren = driven (Partizip II at the end) → Driving yourself from one place to another = 'sein'.
Wann bist du nach Hause gekommen?
When did you come home?
Wann = When | bist = are (helper 'sein' for 'du') | du = you | nach Hause = home | gekommen = come (Partizip II at the end) → Coming home is motion A to B = 'sein'.
Wir sind am Wochenende zu Hause geblieben.
We stayed at home on the weekend.
Wir = We | sind = are (helper 'sein' — 'bleiben' is a fixed exception) | am Wochenende = on the weekend | zu Hause = at home | geblieben = stayed (Partizip II at the end) → 'bleiben' always uses 'sein', even though it means staying still.
Er hat das Auto gefahren.
He drove the car.
Er = He | hat = has (helper 'haben' — there is a direct object 'das Auto') | das Auto = the car | gefahren = driven (Partizip II at the end) → Driving an object = 'haben'. Traveling yourself = 'sein'.
Wir haben viel getanzt und dann sind wir gegangen.
We danced a lot and then we went/left.
Wir = We | haben = have (helper — dancing is not A-to-B motion) | viel = a lot | getanzt = danced | und = and | dann = then | sind = are (helper — going/leaving is A-to-B motion) | wir = we | gegangen = gone (Partizip II) → Two helper verbs in one sentence.
Practice Exercises

Answer: sind (Flying is motion A to B.)

Translate: I went to the supermarket. (gehen)
Answer: Ich bin in den Supermarkt gegangen.
Translate: What happened? (passieren)
Answer: Was ist passiert?
Common Mistakes
❌ Ich habe nach München gefahren.
✓ Ich bin nach München gefahren.
Driving yourself to Munich is motion from A to B. It MUST use 'sein' as the helper verb (bin).
❌ Wir sind geschlafen.
✓ Wir haben geschlafen.
Overcorrecting! Sleeping is not a motion or a change of state. It uses the normal 'haben' helper verb.
❌ Ich habe zu Hause geblieben.
✓ Ich bin zu Hause geblieben.
'Bleiben' (to stay) means staying in one place, so logic suggests 'haben'. But German grammar dictates 'bleiben' is a strict exception that always takes 'sein'. Just memorize: bleiben = sein.
