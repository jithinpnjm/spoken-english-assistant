ss01 — Word Order Overview

English is an SVO language — the Subject comes first, the Verb second, and the Object third. This fixed order is what gives English sentences their meaning.

Why word order matters so much in English Unlike German (which uses case endings) or Latin (which uses inflections), English relies almost entirely on position to show who is doing what to whom. Change the order and you change — or destroy — the meaning.

Same words — completely different events. Word order is the only clue.

Memory anchor Think of English as a train: engine (Subject) → carriages (Verb) → cargo (Object). Swap engine and cargo and the train crashes.

ss02 — Basic Sentence Patterns

English has five core sentence patterns. Every English sentence fits one of them.

Pattern 1 — S + V (Subject + Verb)

S + V

No object needed — the verb is intransitive.

Examples She smiled.  |  Birds fly.  |  The baby cried.  |  He arrived.

Pattern 2 — S + V + O (Subject + Verb + Object)

S + V + O

The most common pattern. The verb is transitive and needs an object.

Examples She reads books.  |  I love coffee.  |  They built a house.

Pattern 3 — S + V + C (Subject + Linking Verb + Complement)

S + Vlink + C

Linking verbs: be, seem, appear, become, feel, look, smell, sound, taste, remain, stay. The complement describes the subject.

Examples She is a doctor.  |  He seems tired.  |  The soup tastes delicious.  |  They became friends.

Pattern 4 — S + V + O + O (Ditransitive — two objects)

S + V + IO + DO

One verb, two objects: an indirect object (recipient) and a direct object (the thing).

Examples She gave him a book.  |  I sent her an email.  |  They told us the truth.
Flip test You can reorder with "to/for": "She gave a book to him." Both orders are correct.

Pattern 5 — S + V + O + C (Object Complement)

S + V + O + C

The complement describes the object, not the subject.

Examples They elected him president.  |  She painted the wall blue.  |  He called me a genius.  |  I found it useful.

Quick comparison table

PatternFormulaExample

ss03 — Questions

Yes/No Questions — Auxiliary Inversion

Move the auxiliary verb before the subject. If there is no auxiliary, use do/does/did.

Auxiliary + Subject + Main Verb + …?
StatementYes/No Question
She is happy.Is she happy?
They can swim.Can they swim?
He plays guitar.Does he play guitar?
She called you.Did she call you?
You have eaten.Have you eaten?

Wh- Questions

The Wh- word moves to the front, then auxiliary inversion follows.

Wh- + Auxiliary + Subject + Verb + …?
Wh- WordAsks aboutExample
WhoPerson (subject)Who called you?
WhomPerson (object, formal)Whom did you call?
WhatThing / actionWhat did she say?
WherePlaceWhere do you live?
WhenTimeWhen does it start?
WhyReasonWhy are you late?
HowManner / degreeHow did you do it?
WhichChoice from a setWhich bag is yours?
WhosePossessionWhose keys are these?
Who vs Whom trick Replace the answer: if it would be "he/she/they" → use who. If it would be "him/her/them" → use whom. "Who called?" → "He called." ✓  |  "Whom did you call?" → "I called him." ✓

Question Tags

A short question added to the end of a statement to invite confirmation. If the statement is positive, the tag is negative — and vice versa.

Positive statement, negative tag?  /  Negative statement, positive tag?
StatementTagFull sentence
She is a teacherisn't she?She is a teacher, isn't she?
They don't knowdo they?They don't know, do they?
You can drivecan't you?You can drive, can't you?
He never liesdoes he?He never lies, does he?
I am latearen't I?I am late, aren't I?
Let's goshall we?Let's go, shall we?
Special case: "I am" The tag for "I am" is aren't I? (not "amn't I?") — this is the accepted informal form.

Indirect Questions

When a question is embedded inside a polite request, the word order reverts to normal statement order (no inversion).

DIRECT (inversion)
Where is the station?
INDIRECT (no inversion)
Could you tell me where the station is?
DIRECT
What time does it open?
INDIRECT
Do you know what time it opens?
More indirect question examples
  • I wonder whether she is coming.
  • Can you explain why you were late?
  • Please tell me how much it costs.
  • I'd like to know if the meeting is cancelled.

ss04 — Negation

Negation in English always requires an auxiliary verb. Different verb types use different auxiliaries.

do / does / did + not

Used with simple present and simple past of main verbs (not "be" and not modals).

PositiveNegativeContracted
I work here.I do not work here.I don't work here.
She likes tea.She does not like tea.She doesn't like tea.
They called me.They did not call me.They didn't call me.
Key rule With do/does/did, the main verb returns to its base form. Never: "She doesn't likes" — always "She doesn't like."

be + not

PositiveNegativeContracted
I am tired.I am not tired.I'm not tired.
She is here.She is not here.She isn't here.
They were late.They were not late.They weren't late.

have + not (perfect tenses)

PositiveNegativeContracted
I have finished.I have not finished.I haven't finished.
She has left.She has not left.She hasn't left.
They had eaten.They had not eaten.They hadn't eaten.

Modal + not

ModalNegativeContracted
cancannotcan't
willwill notwon't
wouldwould notwouldn't
shouldshould notshouldn't
mustmust notmustn't
couldcould notcouldn't
mightmight notmightn't (rare)

The Double Negative Mistake

English uses single negation. Two negative words cancel each other out and create an unintentional positive in standard English.

WRONG — double negative
I don't know nothing.
CORRECT
I don't know anything.
/ I know nothing.
WRONG
She didn't go nowhere.
CORRECT
She didn't go anywhere.
/ She went nowhere.
WRONG
Nobody said nothing.
CORRECT
Nobody said anything.
/ No one said anything.
Rule of thumb After a negative auxiliary (don't, doesn't, didn't, can't, won't…), use positive indefinites: any-, some-. After a positive verb with a negative indefinite, stop at one: "I know nothing."

ss06 — Adverb Placement

Frequency Adverbs

Words like always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, seldom, never, hardly ever.

Placement rules
  • Before the main verb: "She always arrives on time."
  • After be: "He is never late."
  • After the first auxiliary: "They have always been kind."
Sentence typeAdverb positionExample
Main verb sentenceBefore main verbI usually walk to work.
"be" verb sentenceAfter "be"She is always cheerful.
With auxiliaryAfter 1st auxiliaryHe has never been abroad.
Modal + main verbAfter modalYou should always check twice.
WRONG
He goes always to the gym.
CORRECT
He always goes to the gym.
WRONG
She is never late — wait, this IS correct.
CORRECT
"He is always late." (after be)

Manner, Place, and Time (MPT)

When you have multiple adverbials at the end of a clause, the natural order is Manner → Place → Time.

Manner + Place + Time
Examples
  • He drove carefully (manner) to work (place) yesterday (time).
  • She sang beautifully at the concert last night.
  • They worked hard in the office all day.
  • The children played loudly in the garden this afternoon.

Adverbs that go in the middle

Degree adverbs (almost, nearly, quite, rather, really, very, too, enough) go directly before the word they modify.

Examples
  • It is quite difficult.
  • She speaks really fast.
  • He is almost ready.
  • That is too expensive.  /  It isn't good enough.
Time adverbs at the front Time adverbs (yesterday, last week, soon, now) can also move to the front of a sentence for emphasis — this is perfectly natural: "Yesterday, I saw Maria." "Soon, everything will be different."

ss07 — Connecting Ideas

Coordinating Conjunctions — FANBOYS

The seven coordinating conjunctions:
F A N B O Y S
For  ·  And  ·  Nor  ·  But  ·  Or  ·  Yet  ·  So
WordMeaningExample
forreason / becauseHe left early, for he was tired.
andadditionI like coffee and tea.
nornegative additionShe doesn't smoke, nor does she drink.
butcontrastI tried, but I failed.
oralternativeDo you want tea or coffee?
yetcontrast (= however)She is small, yet she is strong.
soresultIt was raining, so I stayed inside.

When FANBOYS join two independent clauses, put a comma before the conjunction.

Subordinating Conjunctions

These introduce a dependent clause. The clause cannot stand alone — it needs the main clause.

CategoryConjunctionsExample
Reasonbecause, since, asI stayed in because it was cold.
Contrastalthough, even though, while, whereasAlthough it rained, we enjoyed it.
Timewhen, while, before, after, until, as soon asCall me when you arrive.
Conditionif, unless, provided that, as long asUnless you hurry, we'll be late.
Purposeso that, in order thatI left early so that I wouldn't miss the train.
Resultso…that, such…thatIt was so hot that we couldn't sleep.
Comma rule for subordinate clauses
  • Subordinate clause first → use a comma: "Although it was raining, we went out."
  • Main clause first → no comma usually: "We went out although it was raining."

ss08 — Relative Clauses

Defining vs Non-Defining

Defining (Restrictive)
  • No commas
  • Identifies which person/thing
  • Cannot be removed without losing meaning
  • Use that, which, who, whom, whose
  • "that" is preferred in informal English
Non-Defining (Non-Restrictive)
  • Always use commas
  • Adds extra information
  • Can be removed — main meaning survives
  • Use which, who, whom, whose
  • Never use "that"

Relative pronouns at a glance

PronounRefers toDefiningNon-Defining
whopeople (subject)✓✓
whompeople (object, formal)✓✓
whichthings / animals✓✓
thatpeople or things✓✗ never
whosepossession✓✓
whereplace✓✓
whentime✓✓
Defining examples
  • The man who called you is my brother. (which man? the one who called)
  • The book that I lent you is out of print.
  • The house where I grew up has been demolished.
  • The day when we met was a Friday.
Non-defining examples
  • My brother, who lives in Dublin, is visiting next week.
  • The Eiffel Tower, which was built in 1889, attracts millions of visitors.
  • Her mother, whom everyone admires, won a national award.
  • Oxford, where I studied, is a beautiful city.

Reducing Relative Clauses

When the relative pronoun is the subject and is followed by a verb, you can reduce the clause to a participle phrase.

Full clauseReduced
The man who is standing by the door…The man standing by the door…
The report that was submitted late…The report submitted late…
Anyone who wants to join…Anyone wanting to join…

Common mistakes

WRONG — "that" in non-defining
My car, that I bought last year, is already broken.
CORRECT
My car, which I bought last year, is already broken.
WRONG — extra pronoun
The woman who she helped me was kind.
CORRECT
The woman who helped me was kind.
WRONG — missing comma
My sister who is a nurse lives in Cork.
CORRECT (if only one sister)
My sister, who is a nurse, lives in Cork.

ss09 — Common Word Order Mistakes

Fifteen frequent errors made by learners — with corrections and explanations.

#WrongCorrectRule
1 I like very much English. I like English very much. "Very much" goes at the end, after the object.
2 She is a very nice girl very tall. She is a very nice, tall girl. All adjectives go before the noun.
3 He goes always to the gym. He always goes to the gym. Frequency adverbs go before the main verb.
4 I speak English good. I speak English well. Adverb (well), not adjective (good), to modify a verb.
5 Yesterday I have seen him. Yesterday I saw him. Simple past, not present perfect, with specific past time.
6 What you are doing? What are you doing? Question inversion: auxiliary before subject.
7 Do you know where is the station? Do you know where the station is? Indirect questions use statement word order.
8 The car red is mine. The red car is mine. Adjectives come before the noun in English.
9 She told to me the truth. She told me the truth. "Tell" takes an indirect object without "to".
10 I don't know nothing about it. I don't know anything about it. No double negatives in standard English.
11 He is a man very intelligent. He is a very intelligent man. Adjectives precede nouns; "very" modifies the adjective.
12 I have seen yesterday a film. I saw a film yesterday. Time adverbs go at the end (or start), not in the middle.
13 She quickly is typing. She is typing quickly. Manner adverbs go after the verb phrase, not between auxiliary and main verb.
14 I am knowing her for years. I have known her for years. Stative verbs (know, love, own) don't use continuous tenses.
15 Despite of the rain, we went out. Despite the rain, we went out. "Despite" is a preposition — no "of". Use "in spite of" if you want "of".
Top 3 to memorise first
  1. Adjectives before nouns: "the red car" not "the car red".
  2. Frequency adverbs before main verbs: "I always eat breakfast".
  3. Question inversion: "Are you ready?" not "You are ready?"
