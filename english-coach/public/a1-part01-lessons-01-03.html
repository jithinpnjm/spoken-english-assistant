Lesson 01

Greetings — Begrüßungen

How Germans greet each other changes depending on the time of day, the region, and how well you know the person. Mastering greetings is your first step to sounding natural.

Grammar Rules
1. Use formal greetings (Guten Morgen, Guten Tag, Auf Wiedersehen) with strangers, elders, and in professional settings.
2. Use informal greetings (Hallo, Hi, Tschüss) with friends, family, and peers of the same age.
3. Time-based greetings: Guten Morgen (morning), Guten Tag (daytime), Guten Abend (evening), Gute Nacht (bedtime/night farewell).
4. For goodbyes: Auf Wiedersehen is formal; Tschüss and bis bald are informal. Bis bald means "see you soon" and implies you expect to meet again shortly.
Formula
Formal Greeting: Guten [Morgen / Tag / Abend] + (name or title)
Informal Greeting: Hallo / Hi + (first name)
Formal Goodbye: Auf Wiedersehen / Auf Wiederhören (on phone)
Informal Goodbye: Tschüss / bis bald / bis später / bis morgen

Common Mistakes
Wrong: Gute Tag! (missing -n)
Right: Guten Tag!
"Guten" is the accusative form of "gut" after the implied article. The -en ending is always required here.
Wrong: Using "Tschüss" with your boss or a stranger
Right: Auf Wiedersehen / Auf Wiederhören (phone)
Tschüss is casual and can sound rude in formal or professional contexts.
Wrong: Gute Nacht as an evening greeting when arriving
Right: Guten Abend (when arriving in the evening)
Gute Nacht is strictly a farewell at the end of the night/before sleep, not an arrival greeting.
Cultural Note

Germany has strong regional greeting variations. In Bavaria and Austria you will often hear "Grüß Gott" (literally "greet God") as a formal hello. In northern Germany, especially Hamburg, "Moin" (or "Moin Moin") is used at any time of day. When entering a small shop, bakery, or doctor's waiting room in Germany, it is polite to greet everyone present with a brief "Hallo" or "Guten Tag" — even strangers. Leaving without saying "Tschüss" or "Auf Wiedersehen" can seem abrupt.

Lesson 02

Common Phrases — Häufige Redemittel

These are the essential polite expressions you need every day — please, thank you, sorry, yes, no. They keep conversations smooth and show good manners.

Grammar Rules
1. "Bitte" has multiple uses: as "please" in a request, as "you're welcome" in response to "Danke", and as "pardon?" when you didn't hear something.
2. "Danke" means thank you; "Danke schön" and "Vielen Dank" are more emphatic. "Bitte schön" or "Bitte sehr" are matching emphatic responses.
3. "Entschuldigung" is used for excuse me (to get attention) and sorry (for a minor mistake). For a sincere apology, use "Es tut mir leid."
4. "Genau" (exactly/precisely) and "klar" (of course/clear) are filler words native speakers use constantly to show agreement without repeating ja.
Formula
Request: [verb] + ... + bitte → Sprechen Sie bitte langsamer. (Please speak more slowly.)
Apology: Es tut mir leid + [reason/blank] → Es tut mir leid, ich verstehe nicht.
Agreement: Ja / Genau / Klar / Natürlich → used to confirm or agree

Common Mistakes
Wrong: Saying "Danke" and expecting no reply
Right: Reply with "Bitte" or "Bitte schön"
In German, it is polite to always respond to Danke with Bitte. Staying silent feels rude.
Wrong: Using "Entschuldigung" for a deep apology (e.g., a serious mistake)
Right: Es tut mir leid (for sincere apologies)
Entschuldigung is for minor interruptions. Es tut mir leid shows genuine remorse.
Cultural Note

Germans have a reputation for directness. Saying "nein, danke" clearly and firmly is considered polite and honest, not rude. Unlike some cultures where declining politely involves long hedging, Germans appreciate a clear no. Similarly, "bitte" is used constantly — in shops, restaurants, and offices — and its absence can make a request sound abrupt or demanding.

Lesson 03

Numbers 1–20 — Zahlen Teil 1

Numbers are everywhere — phone numbers, prices, ages, addresses. Learn 0–20 solid first; the rest of the system builds on these foundations.

Grammar Rules
1. Numbers 1–12 are unique and must be memorised individually. From 13 onward, most numbers follow a pattern: einzehn (11), zwölf (12) are irregular; 13 = dreizehn (drei+zehn), 14 = vierzehn, etc.
2. "Eins" is used when counting alone (eins, zwei, drei…). When a noun follows, it becomes "ein" for masculine/neuter or "eine" for feminine: ein Mann, eine Frau.
3. Phone numbers in German are often read in pairs: 06 21 → null-sechs, zwei-eins. Single digits can also be read individually.
Formula
Pattern 13–19: [units digit root] + zehn → drei+zehn=dreizehn, vier+zehn=vierzehn
Exception: sechzehn (not sechszehn), siebzehn (not siebenzehn)
Age: Ich bin [number] Jahre alt. → Ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt. (I am twenty years old.)

Right: siebzehn
