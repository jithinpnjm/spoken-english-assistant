# 8.9 German Everyday Culture & Etiquette (Alltagskultur)

## 🎯 What you will learn
* I understand the cultural importance of **Pünktlichkeit** (punctuality) in Germany.
* I know the eye-contact rule when toasting with drinks (**Prost!** and **Zum Wohl!**).
* I understand the German domestic ritual of **das Stoßlüften** (airing out rooms).
* I understand pedestrian red-light etiquette (**bei Rot stehen**) and Sunday rest (**die Sonntagsruhe**).
* I know everyday politeness codes: *Herr/Frau* + surname, handshakes, shoes at home, and how direct feedback sounds.
* I can describe differences between everyday life in Germany and my home country in simple German.

## 1. Pünktlichkeit: The German Concept of Time

In Germany, time is treated with great seriousness:
> *„Fünf Minuten vor der Zeit ist des Deutschen Pünktlichkeit.“*  
> *(Five minutes before the time is German punctuality.)*

* If you have an appointment with a doctor, landlord, or at work at 14:00, **arrive at 13:55**.
* Arriving 10 minutes late without a message is often seen as impolite, especially for appointments and at work (among friends it depends on the group).
* If your train is delayed or you are caught in traffic, send a quick message:
  * *„Es tut mir leid, mein Zug hat Verspätung. Ich komme etwa zehn Minuten später.“* (I am sorry, my train is delayed. I will arrive about 10 minutes late.)

## 2. 🍻 Toasting: "Prost!" & The Eye-Contact Rule

When having drinks with friends or colleagues in Germany, many people follow this ritual:

1. Clink glasses with each person individually:
   * With beer: **„Prost!“**
   * With wine or at formal dinners: **„Zum Wohl!“**
2. **Look the other person in the eye while clinking glasses.**
   * Many people notice if you look away – a popular joke says it brings bad luck. It's a friendly custom, not a rule.
3. Take a sip before putting the glass down on the table.

## 3. 🪟 The Art of "Stoßlüften" (Airing Out Rooms)

If you rent an apartment in Germany, your lease contract will likely mention: **regelmäßiges Lüften** (regular ventilation).
* German windows have two opening modes:
  * **Kippen:** Tilted slightly open at the top.
  * **Stoßlüften:** Opening all windows wide for 5 to 10 minutes twice a day to completely exchange the humid indoor air for dry outdoor air.
* This helps to prevent mould (*Schimmel*), which is why landlords often ask for it.

## 4. 🚦 The Red Pedestrian Light (Die rote Ampel)

In many countries, pedestrians cross whenever no cars are coming. 
**In Germany, many adults wait at the pedestrian crossing until the light turns green (*bei Grün gehen*):**
* Crossing on red is socially frowned upon, especially if children are present (*„Vorbild für Kinder!“* — be a role model for children!).
* Crossing on red is also an offence that can be fined (a small amount, but it happens).

---

## 5. 🛑 Sonntagsruhe: Sundays Are Different

* Most **shops and supermarkets are closed on Sundays** and public holidays (the rules come from state shop-opening laws).
  Exceptions: many bakeries open for a few hours in the morning, shops in big train stations and airports, petrol
  stations, and a few *verkaufsoffene Sonntage* (special shopping Sundays) a year.
* **Tip:** do your weekend shopping on Saturday – supermarkets can be very full on Saturday afternoons.
* Sunday is also a quiet day in many buildings: no drilling or lawnmowing (see [4.4 Hausmeister & Neighbours](../vol4-home/hausmeister-neighbors.mdx)).

## 6. 🤝 Small Politeness Codes

| Situation | Common in Germany | Useful German |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Addressing people | *Herr / Frau* + surname with *Sie* until someone offers *du* | *Guten Tag, Frau Schmidt.* |
| Meeting someone formally | a short handshake and eye contact | *Freut mich.* / *Angenehm.* |
| Entering a home | many people take their shoes off – ask | *Soll ich die Schuhe ausziehen?* |
| Giving feedback | fairly direct; it is usually not meant as rude | *Das finde ich nicht so gut.* |
| Being invited | bring a small gift, arrive on time (for parties, a little later is fine) | *Danke für die Einladung!* |
| Entering/leaving a small shop or waiting room | a general greeting to everyone | *Guten Tag!* / *Tschüss!* |

These are tendencies – people and regions differ. When in doubt, watch what others do, or ask.
