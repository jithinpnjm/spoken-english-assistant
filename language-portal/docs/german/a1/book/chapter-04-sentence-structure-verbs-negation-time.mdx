# Chapter 4: Basic Sentence Structure, Verbs in Present Tense, Negation (`kein` vs. `nicht`), Clock Time & Ordering

Welcome to Chapter 4! Up to now, you have learned how to greet people, introduce yourself, count in German, and use nouns and pronouns (*der, die, das* and *ich, du, er, sie, es*). Now it is time to connect those building blocks into real, living German sentences!

Many beginners worry that German grammar is complicated. Here is the good news: building a normal German sentence actually comes down to **just two simple rules**. Once you see how those two rules work, and how action words (verbs) change their endings to match the person speaking, you can talk about your daily life, say what you do and do not have, tell the time on any clock, and walk into a German café to order coffee and cake with confidence.

---

## 🎯 What you will learn

* I know the **2 Golden Rules of German Sentence Structure**: the Verb is always in **Position 2**, and the Subject stands either **directly before** or **directly after** the verb.
* I understand why **"2nd Position" does NOT mean "2nd word"** (*Ein neues Auto | kaufe | ich morgen*).
* I understand what **Verb Conjugation** means in plain English and can use the two most important German verbs by heart: **`sein`** (to be) and **`haben`** (to have).
* I can find the **Verb Stem** of any regular verb (*spielen ➔ spiel-*) and add the 6 present-tense endings (**`-e, -st, -t, -en, -t, -en`**) using the classroom memory hook *"I requ**est** you to write it **ten ten** times"*.
* I know the **2 easy spelling exceptions** for regular verbs (adding a helper **`-e-`** after `-d/-t/-n` like *du arbeitest*, and adding only **`-t`** after `-s/-ß/-z` like *du heißt*).
* I can conjugate common **Irregular (Strong) Verbs** where the vowel changes **only for `du` and `er/sie/es`** (*sprechen ➔ du sprichst, lesen ➔ du liest, fahren ➔ du fährst*).
* I know exactly when to say "no / not" using **`kein / keine`** (for nouns with *ein/eine* or no article) versus **`nicht`** (for verbs, adjectives, names, and *der/die/das* nouns), plus how to use **`nicht/kein … sondern …`** ("not X, but rather Y").
* I can ask for and tell the **Clock Time (`Die Uhrzeit`)** both officially (*15:15 = fünfzehn Uhr fünfzehn*) and in everyday conversation (*Viertel nach drei, halb vier*), and I can **order and pay in a restaurant or café**.

## 1. Basic German Sentence Structure — Only 2 Rules to Remember!

### The Mental Model: What Makes a Sentence?

Before we look at German, let us look at how a simple sentence works in English:
* **"He plays football today."**

In this sentence, we have four pieces of information (we can call them **building blocks** or **chunks**):
1. **Who** is doing the action? (The **Subject**): *He* (*Er*)
2. **What action** is happening? (The **Verb**): *plays* (*spielt*)
3. **When** is it happening? (The **Time**): *today* (*heute*)
4. **What** is he playing? (The **Object**): *football* (*Fußball*)

In English, you are almost always forced to start with the person (*He plays...*). If you try to move the words around in English, it quickly sounds broken: you cannot say *"Football plays he today"* or *"Today plays he football."*

German is much more flexible! In German, you can start your sentence with **who** (*Er*), **when** (*Heute*), or even **what** (*Fußball*)—as long as you follow **2 Golden Rules**.

---

### Golden Rule 1: The Verb is ALWAYS at the 2nd Position!

In every normal German statement (a sentence ending with a period `.`), the action word (**the conjugated verb**) has a permanent, reserved seat: **Position 2**. It never moves to Position 1, and it never slides to Position 3.

Let us take the German sentence **`Er spielt heute Fußball`** (*He plays football today*) and move its building blocks around. Watch where the verb **`spielt`** (*plays*) sits in every single version:

| Position 1 (Starting Chunk) | **Position 2 (VERB — Fixed!)** | Position 3 | Position 4 | English Meaning & Emphasis |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Er** | **spielt** | heute | Fußball. | *He plays football today.* (Standard focus on "He") |
| **Heute** | **spielt** | er | Fußball. | *Today he plays football.* (Emphasis on "Today") |
| **Fußball** | **spielt** | er | heute. | *Football is what he plays today.* (Emphasis on "Football") |
| **Er** | **spielt** | Fußball | heute. | *He plays football today.* (Possible in spoken German, though Time usually comes before Object/Place) |

Look closely at the second column: **`spielt`** is in **Position 2** every single time!
* What is **WRONG**?
  * ❌ *Heute er **spielt** Fußball.* (Wrong! Here *Heute* is Position 1, *er* is Position 2, and *spielt* got pushed to Position 3!)
  * ❌ *Er heute **spielt** Fußball.* (Wrong! Again, *spielt* got pushed to Position 3!)

Now let us test the exact same rule with a second classroom example:
* **`Ich kaufe morgen ein neues Auto.`** (*I am buying / will buy a new car tomorrow.*)
  * `Ich` = I (Subject)
  * `kaufe` = buy / am buying (Verb)
  * `morgen` = tomorrow (Time)
  * `ein neues Auto` = a new car (Object)

Let us rearrange the building blocks of this sentence:

| Position 1 (Starting Chunk) | **Position 2 (VERB — Fixed!)** | Position 3 | Position 4 | English Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ich** | **kaufe** | morgen | ein neues Auto. | *I will buy a new car tomorrow.* |
| **Morgen** | **kaufe** | ich | ein neues Auto. | *Tomorrow I will buy a new car.* |
| **Ein neues Auto** | **kaufe** | ich | morgen. | *A new car is what I will buy tomorrow.* |

---

### Crucial Question: "Wait! In `Ein neues Auto kaufe ich morgen`, isn't `kaufe` the 4th word?!"

This is the single most important question every smart beginner asks in class! Let us count the individual words in:
* *(1) Ein (2) neues (3) Auto (4) **kaufe** (5) ich (6) morgen.*

Why do we say **`kaufe`** is in **Position 2** when it is the **4th word**?

Because **Position 2 does NOT mean "the 2nd word"!**
**Position 2 means the 2nd Grammatical Idea (or Chunk / Block) of the sentence!**

Think of a train with train cars:
* **Train Car 1 (Position 1)** holds **one complete idea**.
* Can you chop `"ein neues Auto"` (*a new car*) in half and put `"ein"` in the front of the sentence and `"neues Auto"` at the back? No! *"A new car"* is **one single thing**. All three words (`Ein neues Auto`) travel together inside **Position 1**.
* And what comes immediately after Position 1? **Position 2 (`kaufe`)**!

Let us look at two more examples from our class notes to make this 100% crystal clear:

```text
Example A: Two people as ONE subject chunk in Position 1
[ Position 1: Ich und Thomas ]   [ Position 2: kaufen ]   [ Position 3: morgen ]   [ Position 4: ein neues Auto. ]
       (3 words = 1 idea)            (VERB = Pos 2!)
```
* **Word-by-Word Breakdown:** `Ich und Thomas` (*I and Thomas* — together they are the "who" of the sentence, so they share Position 1) + `kaufen` (*buy* — Position 2!) + `morgen` (*tomorrow*) + `ein neues Auto` (*a new car*).

```text
Example B: Two time words as ONE time chunk in Position 1
[ Position 1: Heute und morgen ]   [ Position 2: lese ]   [ Position 3: ich ]   [ Position 4: ein Buch. ]
       (3 words = 1 time idea)       (VERB = Pos 2!)
```
* **Word-by-Word Breakdown:** `Heute und morgen` (*Today and tomorrow* — one combined time block in Position 1) + `lese` (*read* — Position 2!) + `ich` (*I* — Position 3) + `ein Buch` (*a book* — Position 4).

```text
Example C: A longer time phrase in Position 1
[ Position 1: Jeden Tag um acht Uhr ]   [ Position 2: trinke ]   [ Position 3: ich ]   [ Position 4: Kaffee. ]
      (5 words = 1 time idea)               (VERB = Pos 2!)
```
* **Word-by-Word Breakdown:** `Jeden Tag um acht Uhr` (*Every day at eight o'clock* — all five words answer the single question "When?", so they sit together in Position 1) + `trinke` (*drink* — Position 2!) + `ich` (*I* — Position 3) + `Kaffee` (*coffee* — Position 4).

---

### Golden Rule 2: The Subject Always Comes Directly BEFORE or Directly AFTER the Verb!

Now you know that the Verb is glued to **Position 2**. Where does the **Subject** (the person or thing doing the action, like *ich, du, er, wir, Thomas*) go?

**Rule 2:** The Subject and the Verb are best friends. They must always touch each other!
* Either the Subject stands in **Position 1** (directly **before** the verb):
  * **`Ich` | `kaufe`** | `morgen` | `ein neues Auto.`
  * **`Er` | `spielt`** | `heute` | `Fußball.`
* Or, if something else (like `Morgen` or `Heute` or `Ein neues Auto`) takes Position 1, the Subject immediately steps to **Position 3** (directly **after** the verb):
  * `Morgen` | **`kaufe` | `ich`** | `ein neues Auto.`
  * `Heute` | **`spielt` | `er`** | `Fußball.`
  * `Ein neues Auto` | **`kaufe` | `ich`** | `morgen.`

Notice how in every valid sentence, the subject (`ich`, `er`) is right next to the verb (`kaufe`, `spielt`):
* `Ich kaufe...` or `...kaufe ich...`
* `Er spielt...` or `...spielt er...`

### Bonus Tip: Time Before Place (`Zeit vor Ort`)
When both a **time** (*when*) and a **place** (*where*) appear after the verb, German naturally puts **Time before Place**:
* **`Ich fahre morgen (Time) nach Berlin (Place).`** (*I am going to Berlin tomorrow.*)
* **`Wir lernen heute (Time) in der Schule (Place).`** (*We are studying at school today.*)

## 2. What is Verb Conjugation? The Two Bedrock Verbs: `haben` (to have) & `sein` (to be)

### Plain-English Explanation: What Does "Conjugation" Mean?

Teachers and grammar books love the big word **Conjugation** (*die Konjugation*). What does it actually mean in everyday life?

It simply means **changing the form of an action word (a verb) so that it matches the person doing the action!**

You already do this every day in English without thinking about it. Look at the English verb **"to be"**:
* Do we say *"I be happy, you be happy, he be happy"*?
* No! We change the verb to match the person:
  * *I **am** happy.*
  * *You **are** happy.*
  * *He **is** happy.*
* Look at the English verb **"to have"**:
  * *I **have** a car.*
  * *He **has** a car.*

Changing *be* into *am / are / is*, or changing *have* into *have / has*, is called **conjugation**.
In English, verbs only change a tiny bit. In German, the verb puts on a clear, helpful ending for each pronoun (*ich, du, er/sie/es, wir, ihr, sie/Sie*) so you always know exactly who is doing the action!

Before we learn normal regular verbs, we must master the **two most important verbs in the entire German language**:
1. **`sein`** = **to be** (*I am, you are, he is...*)
2. **`haben`** = **to have** (*I have, you have, he has...*)

Just like *"am, are, is"* in English, `sein` and `haben` are special and very old words, so we learn their forms directly by heart.

---

### Bedrock Verb 1: `sein` (to be)

We use **`sein`** to say **who** we are, **where** we are, **what job** we have, **how old** we are, and **how** something is (*tired, happy, good, expensive*).

| Personal Pronoun | Meaning | Conjugated Form of `sein` | English Meaning | Everyday German Example | English Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`ich`** | I | **`bin`** | am | *Ich **bin** Thomas.* | I am Thomas. |
| **`du`** | you (1 friend/family) | **`bist`** | are | *Du **bist** sehr nett.* | You are very nice. |
| **`er`** | he / it (masc.) | **`ist`** | is | *Er **ist** Lehrer.* | He is a teacher. |
| **`sie`** | she / it (fem.) | **`ist`** | is | *Sie **ist** Ärztin.* | She is a doctor. |
| **`es`** | it (neut.) | **`ist`** | is | *Das Wetter **ist** gut.* | The weather is good. |
| **`wir`** | we | **`sind`** | are | *Wir **sind** in Berlin.* | We are in Berlin. |
| **`ihr`** | you all (2+ friends) | **`seid`** | are | *Ihr **seid** pünktlich!* | You all are on time! |
| **`sie`** | they | **`sind`** | are | *Sie **sind** aus Indien.* | They are from India. |
| **`Sie`** | You (formal) | **`sind`** | are | *Woher **sind** Sie?* | Where are You from? |

#### Everyday Sentences with `sein` (with Word-by-Word Breakdowns):
1. **`Ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt.`**
   * *Word-by-word:* `Ich` (I) + `bin` (am) + `zwanzig` (twenty) + `Jahre` (years) + `alt` (old).
   * *Meaning:* I am 20 years old.
2. **`Heute bin ich zu Hause.`**
   * *Word-by-word:* `Heute` (Today — Pos. 1) + `bin` (am — Verb in Pos. 2!) + `ich` (I — Subject in Pos. 3!) + `zu Hause` (at home).
   * *Meaning:* Today I am at home.
3. **`Mein Bruder ist Student, und meine Eltern sind in Kochi.`**
   * *Word-by-word:* `Mein Bruder` (My brother = *er*) + `ist` (is) + `Student` (a university student), `und` (and) + `meine Eltern` (my parents = *sie* plural, "they") + `sind` (are) + `in Kochi` (in Kochi).

---

### Bedrock Verb 2: `haben` (to have)

We use **`haben`** to talk about things we own (*a car, a house, money*), family members (*children, siblings*), time (*free time, an appointment*), and feelings like hunger or thirst (*Ich habe Hunger = I have hunger / I am hungry*).

| Personal Pronoun | Meaning | Conjugated Form of `haben` | English Meaning | Everyday German Example | English Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`ich`** | I | **`habe`** | have | *Ich **habe** ein Auto.* | I have a car. |
| **`du`** | you (informal) | **`hast`** | have | *Du **hast** eine Frage.* | You have a question. |
| **`er / sie / es`** | he / she / it | **`hat`** | has | *Er **hat** viel Zeit.* | He has a lot of time. |
| **`wir`** | we | **`haben`** | have | *Wir **haben** Unterricht.* | We have class. |
| **`ihr`** | you all (informal) | **`habt`** | have | *Ihr **habt** Glück!* | You all have luck (are lucky)! |
| **`sie / Sie`** | they / You (formal) | **`haben`** | have | *Sie **haben** zwei Kinder.* | They / You have two children. |

:::tip Watch Out for `du hast` and `er/sie/es hat`!
In **`du hast`** and **`er/sie/es hat`**, the letter **`-b-`** from *haben* disappears!
* ❌ Do NOT say *du habst* or *er habt*!
* ✅ Always say **`du hast`** (*you have*) and **`er/sie/es hat`** (*he/she/it has*)!
* For **`ihr`** (you all), the **`-b-`** stays: **`ihr habt`**!
:::

#### Everyday Sentences with `haben`:
1. **`Am Montag habe ich Deutschunterricht.`**
   * *Word-by-word:* `Am Montag` (On Monday — Pos. 1) + `habe` (have — Pos. 2) + `ich` (I — Pos. 3) + `Deutschunterricht` (German class).
2. **`Thomas hat einen Hund, und wir haben eine Katze.`**
   * *Word-by-word:* `Thomas` (He) + `hat` (has) + `einen Hund` (a dog), `und` (and) + `wir` (we) + `haben` (have) + `eine Katze` (a cat).

## 3. Regular Verbs in the Present Tense (`Regelmäßige / Schwache Verben`)

### Plain-English Explanation: What is a "Regular Verb" and a "Verb Stem"?

In German, about 85% of verbs in the Present Tense (`Präsens`) follow one simple, predictable pattern. Because they follow the normal rules without any surprises, we call them **Regular Verbs** (*regelmäßige Verben*, also called "weak verbs" / *schwache Verben*).

When you look up a German verb in a dictionary, you see its base form (called the **Infinitive** / *Infinitiv*), which means *"to do something"*:
* **`spielen`** = to play
* **`machen`** = to do / to make
* **`kommen`** = to come
* **`wohnen`** = to live / reside
* **`lernen`** = to learn / study

Notice how almost every German dictionary verb ends in **`-en`** (or occasionally just **`-n`**).

To use a verb in a sentence, we follow a **2-Step Formula**:

#### Step 1: Chop off the `-en` to find the **Verb Stem** (`Verbstamm`)
The **Verb Stem** is simply the core part of the verb that is left after you remove **`-en`**:
* `spielen` (to play) minus `-en` ➔ Stem: **`spiel-`**
* `machen` (to make/do) minus `-en` ➔ Stem: **`mach-`**
* `kommen` (to come) minus `-en` ➔ Stem: **`komm-`**
* `wohnen` (to live) minus `-en` ➔ Stem: **`wohn-`**
* `lernen` (to learn) minus `-en` ➔ Stem: **`lern-`**
* `kaufen` (to buy) minus `-en` ➔ Stem: **`kauf-`**
* `trinken` (to drink) minus `-en` ➔ Stem: **`trink-`**
* `gehen` (to go) minus `-en` ➔ Stem: **`geh-`**

#### Step 2: Glue the 6 Personal Endings onto the Stem!
Once you have the stem (for example, **`spiel-`**), you simply add the ending that matches the person doing the action:

| Pronoun | Meaning | **The Ending** | Stem + Ending (`spielen`) | Complete Form | English Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`ich`** | I | **`-e`** | `spiel` + `e` | **`ich spiele`** | I play / I am playing |
| **`du`** | you (informal) | **`-st`** | `spiel` + `st` | **`du spielst`** | you play / you are playing |
| **`er / sie / es`** | he / she / it | **`-t`** | `spiel` + `t` | **`er/sie/es spielt`** | he/she/it plays / is playing |
| **`wir`** | we | **`-en`** | `spiel` + `en` | **`wir spielen`** | we play / we are playing |
| **`ihr`** | you all | **`-t`** | `spiel` + `t` | **`ihr spielt`** | you all play / are playing |
| **`sie / Sie`** | they / You (formal) | **`-en`** | `spiel` + `en` | **`sie/Sie spielen`** | they/You play / are playing |

---

### The Famous Classroom Memory Hook: `"e - st - t - en - t - en"`

How can you remember the 6 endings (**`-e, -st, -t, -en, -t, -en`**) in the exact order of `ich, du, er/sie/es, wir, ihr, sie/Sie` so you never forget them in an exam?

Use our classroom sentence:
> **"I requ`est` you to write it `ten` `ten` times!"**

Look at the bold letters inside that English sentence:
1. **`est`** ➔ splits into **`-e`** (`ich`) and **`-st`** (`du`)
2. **`ten`** ➔ splits into **`-t`** (`er/sie/es`) and **`-en`** (`wir`)
3. **`ten`** ➔ splits into **`-t`** (`ihr`) and **`-en`** (`sie/Sie`)

**`e - st - t - en - t - en`**! Say *"est - ten - ten"* out loud three times, and you have memorized the entire regular present-tense system of German!

### The 2 Easy Spelling Exceptions (Why Do We Say `du arbeitest` and `du heißt`?)

Look at `arbeiten`, `zeichnen`, and `heißen` in Table B above. They are regular verbs, so why do they have a tiny spelling adjustment? Only to make them **easy for your mouth to pronounce**!

#### Spelling Exception 1: Verb Stem Ends in `-d`, `-t`, or Consonant + `-n`/`-m` ➔ Add a Helper `-e-`!
Try to say `arbeit` + `t` (*arbeitt*) or `arbeit` + `st` (*arbeitst*) out loud. Your tongue gets stuck because there are too many hard consonants piled together!
The same thing happens with `zeichn-` (*to draw*), `öffn-` (*to open*), `regn-` (*to rain*), `antwort-` (*to answer*), `find-` (*to find*), and `wart-` (*to wait*).

**The Fix:** Whenever a verb stem ends in **`-d`**, **`-t`**, or a consonant + **`-n` / `-m`** (like `zeichn-`, `öffn-`, `regn-`, `atm-`), German inserts a friendly helper **`-e-`** before **`-st`** and **`-t`** (that is, for **`du`**, **`er/sie/es`**, and **`ihr`**):

| Pronoun | `arbeiten` (to work) | `antworten` (to answer) | `finden` (to find) | `öffnen` (to open) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`ich`** | ich arbeite | ich antworte | ich finde | ich öffne |
| **`du`** | du arbeit**est** | du antwort**est** | du find**est** | du öffn**est** |
| **`er / sie / es`** | er arbeit**et** | er antwort**et** | er find**et** | er öffn**et** |
| **`wir`** | wir arbeiten | wir antworten | wir finden | wir öffnen |
| **`ihr`** | ihr arbeit**et** | ihr antwort**et** | ihr find**et** | ihr öffn**et** |
| **`sie / Sie`** | sie arbeiten | sie antworten | sie finden | sie öffnen |

*(Note: If a stem ends in a vowel + `n` like `wohn-` or `l/r` + `n` like `lern-`, it is already easy to say `du wohnst` and `du lernst`, so no helper `-e-` is needed!)*

#### Spelling Exception 2: Verb Stem Ends in an "S-Sound" (`-s`, `-ß`, `-ss`, `-z`, `-tz`) ➔ `du` Only Adds `-t`!
Look at the verb **`heißen`** (*to be called*). Its stem is **`heiß-`**.Remember that `ß` is pronounced `"ss"`.
If we added `-st` for `du`, we would get *`du heißst`* (three S-sounds in a row: *heiss-st*!).
The same thing would happen with **`reisen`** (*to travel*, stem `reis-`), **`tanzen`** (*to dance*, stem `tanz-`), and **`sitzen`** (*to sit*, stem `sitz-`).

**The Fix:** Because the stem already ends in an S-sound (`-s, -ß, -z`), we drop the `s` of `-st` for **`du`** and simply add **`-t`**!
* **`heißen`** ➔ `ich heiße`, **`du heißt`**, **`er/sie/es heißt`**
* **`reisen`** ➔ `ich reise`, **`du reist`**, **`er/sie/es reist`**
* **`tanzen`** ➔ `ich tanze`, **`du tanzt`**, **`er/sie/es tanzt`**
* **`sitzen`** ➔ `ich sitze`, **`du sitzt`**, **`er/sie/es sitzt`**

Notice the cool result: for these verbs, the **`du`** form and the **`er/sie/es`** form look **100% identical** (`du heißt` = `er heißt`, `du tanzt` = `sie tanzt`)!

---

### Why German Present Tense is 3x Easier Than English!

In English, you have to choose between three different ways to talk about the present and near future:
1. **Simple Present:** *"I play football."*
2. **Present Continuous ("am/is/are + -ing"):** *"I am playing football right now."*
3. **Future with "will / going to":** *"I will play / am going to play football tomorrow."*

**In German, there is NO `"am/is/are + -ing"` form!**
One single German Present Tense form covers all three!

| What You Want to Say in English | How You Say It in German | Why It Works |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *I play football (every Saturday).* | **`Ich spiele (jeden Samstag) Fußball.`** | Normal present tense (`ich spiele`). |
| *I am playing football (right now).* | **`Ich spiele (jetzt) Fußball.`** | Still `ich spiele`! (**NEVER** say ❌ *Ich bin spiele*!) |
| *I will play / am going to play football tomorrow.* | **`Ich spiele morgen Fußball.`** *(or: **`Morgen spiele ich Fußball.`**)* | Present tense + a future time word (`morgen`) = Future meaning! |
| *I will buy a new car tomorrow.* | **`Ich kaufe morgen ein neues Auto.`** | Present tense `kaufe` + `morgen` = *I will buy tomorrow*! |

:::danger Never Translate English "I am + verb-ing" Word for Word!
The #1 mistake English speakers make is saying:
* ❌ *Ich bin spiele Fußball.* ("I am play football")
* ❌ *Er ist arbeitet heute.* ("He is works today")
In German, **`Ich spiele`** already means BOTH *"I play"* AND *"I am playing"*. You NEVER put `bin / bist / ist` in front of another action verb!
:::

## 4. Irregular Verbs in the Present Tense (`Unregelmäßige / Starke Verben`)

### Plain-English Explanation: What Makes a Verb "Irregular" (or "Strong")?

We just saw that regular verbs keep their stem 100% unchanged (`spiel-` stays `spiel-` for every single person).

However, a small group of very common everyday verbs—like *speaking, eating, reading, seeing, driving, sleeping, helping, taking,* and *giving*—are called **Irregular Verbs** (*unregelmäßige Verben* or **Strong Verbs** / *starke Verben*).

Why? Because **the vowel inside the verb stem changes** in the Present Tense!

### The Best News Ever: The Vowel ONLY Changes for `du` and `er / sie / es`!

Here is the secret that makes irregular verbs easy to master:
1. The **endings** are still the exact same regular endings (**`-e, -st, -t, -en, -t, -en`**)!
2. The vowel inside the stem **ONLY changes for `du` (2nd person singular) and `er / sie / es` (3rd person singular)**!
3. For **`ich`**, **`wir`**, **`ihr`**, and **`sie / Sie`**, the verb is **100% normal** with no vowel change at all!

There are **3 Vowel-Change Groups** in the A1 Present Tense:
* **Group 1: `e ➔ i`** (*sprechen, essen, nehmen, geben, helfen, treffen*)
* **Group 2: `e ➔ ie`** (*lesen, sehen*)
* **Group 3: `a ➔ ä`** (*fahren, schlafen, tragen, waschen, gefallen, laufen*)

Let us look at the complete conjugation tables for every single one of these verbs from our course notes!

---

### Group 1: Stem Vowel Changes from `e ➔ i` (ONLY in `du` and `er/sie/es`)

#### 1. `sprechen` (to speak), `geben` (to give), `helfen` (to help)

| **`wir`** *(Normal!)* | wir sprechen | wir geben | wir helfen |

| **`sie / Sie`** *(Normal!)* | sie/Sie sprechen | sie/Sie geben | sie/Sie helfen |

#### 2. `essen` (to eat), `nehmen` (to take), `treffen` (to meet)

:::info Two Important Notes on `essen` and `nehmen`:
1. Look at **`essen`** (*to eat*): The stem `ess-` already ends in `-ss`! So when `e` changes to `i` (`iss-`), **`du`** only adds `-t` (Spelling Exception 2!). That means **`du isst`** (*you eat*) and **`er/sie/es isst`** (*he/she/it eats*) are spelled identically!
2. Look at **`nehmen`** (*to take*): For `du` and `er/sie/es`, `eh` becomes short **`imm`** with a double `m`: **`du nimmst`**, **`er/sie/es nimmt`**! (For *ich, wir, ihr, sie*, it stays `ich nehme, wir nehmen, ihr nehmt, sie nehmen`).
:::

Look at the two ways Germans use **`werden`**:
1. **Meaning "to become / to get" (Everyday A1):**
   * *Mein Sohn will Arzt **werden**.* (My son wants to **become** a doctor.)
   * *Es **wird** kalt / dunkel.* (It is **getting** cold / dark.)
2. **Recognizing the Future Tense (`werden` + Infinitive at the end):**
   At A1, you can always talk about the future using the normal Present Tense + a time word (*Morgen **regnet** es · Ich **lerne** Deutsch*). However, in weather forecasts and announcements, you will also hear **`werden`** + an infinitive at the end for *"will ..."*:
   * *Ich **werde** Deutsch **lernen**.* (I **will learn** German.)
   * *Morgen **wird** es **regnen**.* (Tomorrow it **will rain**.)

## 5. Negation in German: How to Say "No" and "Not" (`kein` vs. `nicht` & `sondern`)

### Plain-English Explanation: What is "Negation"?

**Negation** (*die Verneinung*) simply means making a positive statement negative—turning *"Yes, I have a car"* into *"No, I do **not** have a car"*, or turning *"The weather is good"* into *"The weather is **not** good."*

First, when someone asks you a question and you want to answer **"No"** at the very beginning of your reply, you always use **`Nein`**:
* *Kommst du heute?* (Are you coming today?) ➔ **`Nein`**! (No!)

Second, when you want to say **"not"** or **"no / not a"** *inside* a sentence, German uses two words:
1. **`kein` / `keine`** (means *"no / not a / not any"* in front of a noun)
2. **`nicht`** (means *"not"* for everything else!)

Beginners often ask: *"How do I know whether to choose `kein` or `nicht`?"* Let us make this super simple!

---

### Rule A: When to Use `kein` or `keine` ("no / not a / not any")

Think of **`kein`** as the negative twin brother of **`ein`** (*a / an*)!
You simply put **`k`** in front of **`ein`** ➔ **`kein`**, and **`k`** in front of **`eine`** ➔ **`keine`**!

Use **`kein` / `keine`** in ONLY two situations—both are **directly in front of a Noun** (a person or thing written with a capital letter):

#### 1. When the positive sentence has `ein` or `eine` ("a / an") before the noun:
* Look at the gender of the noun (`der / das` vs. `die`):
  * Masculine (`der`) or Neutral (`das`): **`ein` ➔ `kein`** (and in Accusative masculine after *haben/kaufen*: **`einen` ➔ `keinen`**)
  * Feminine (`die`): **`eine` ➔ `keine`**

| Positive Sentence (`ein / eine`) | Negative Sentence (`kein / keine`) | English Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *Das ist **ein** Tisch.* (`der Tisch`) | *Das ist **kein** Tisch.* | That is **not a** table. |
| *Das ist **ein** Buch.* (`das Buch`) | *Das ist **kein** Buch.* | That is **not a** book. |
| *Ich habe **ein** Auto.* (`das Auto`) | *Ich habe **kein** Auto.* | I have **no** car / I do **not** have **a** car. |
| *Das ist **eine** Lampe.* (`die Lampe`) | *Das ist **keine** Lampe.* | That is **not a** lamp. |
| *Ich habe **eine** Katze.* (`die Katze`) | *Ich habe **keine** Katze.* | I do **not** have **a** cat. |
| *Er hat **einen** Bruder.* (`der Bruder`, Acc.) | *Er hat **keinen** Bruder.* | He does **not** have **a** brother. |

#### 2. When the positive sentence has a Noun with NO article at all (Plural nouns or uncountable nouns like *Geld, Zeit, Hunger, Kaffee*):
In English, we say *"I have children"* (no article) ➔ *"I have **no** children / I don't have **any** children."*
Or *"I have money / time"* ➔ *"I have **no** money / **no** time."*
In German, whenever a noun has no article in the positive sentence, you negate it with **`kein` / `keine`**:

| Positive Sentence (Noun with No Article) | Negative Sentence (`kein / keine`) | Why `kein` or `keine`? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *Ich habe **Geld**.* (`das Geld` = money) | *Ich habe **kein** Geld.* | `das Geld` is neutral ➔ **`kein`** (*I have no money.*) |
| *Er hat **Hunger**.* (`der Hunger` = hunger) | *Er hat **keinen** Hunger.* | `der Hunger` is masculine ➔ **`keinen`** (*He isn't hungry.*) |
| *Ich habe **Zeit**.* (`die Zeit` = time) | *Ich habe **keine** Zeit.* | `die Zeit` is feminine ➔ **`keine`** (*I have no time.*) |
| *Wir haben **Kinder**.* (`die Kinder` = plural) | *Wir haben **keine** Kinder.* | Plural nouns always take `die` ➔ **`keine`** (*We have no children.*) |
| *Sie kauft **Äpfel**.* (`die Äpfel` = plural) | *Sie kauft **keine** Äpfel.* | Plural noun ➔ **`keine`** (*She isn't buying any apples.*) |

---

### Rule B: When to Use `nicht` ("not")

If you are **NOT** negating an `ein/eine` noun or a zero-article noun, you use **`nicht`**!
Here are the **4 clear situations** where you always use **`nicht`**:

#### 1. To Negate an Action / Verb ("do not do something"):
When you want to say someone does *not* work, does *not* drink, does *not* sleep, or does *not* come, put **`nicht`** after the verb (or at the end of the simple clause):
* *Ich trinke.* (I drink.) ➔ **`Ich trinke nicht.`** (*I do not drink.*)
* *Ich arbeite heute.* (I work today.) ➔ **`Ich arbeite heute nicht.`** / **`Heute arbeite ich nicht.`** (*I am not working today.*)
* *Er schläft.* (He sleeps.) ➔ **`Er schläft nicht.`** (*He is not sleeping.*)
* *Wir spielen heute nicht.* (*We are not playing today.*)

#### 2. To Negate an Adjective or Adverb ("not good, not expensive, not fast"):
Put **`nicht`** **directly in front of** the describing word (adjective/adverb) you want to negate:
* *Das Wetter ist gut.* (The weather is good.) ➔ **`Das Wetter ist nicht gut.`** (*The weather is not good.*)
* *Das Auto ist teuer.* (The car is expensive.) ➔ **`Das Auto ist nicht teuer.`** (*The car is not expensive.*)
* *Die Aufgabe ist schwer.* (The task is difficult.) ➔ **`Die Aufgabe ist nicht schwer.`** (*The task is not difficult.*)

#### 3. To Negate Proper Names, Cities, or Specific People (`der/die/das` or `mein/dein` nouns):
You cannot say *"no Thomas"* or *"no my car"*. When a noun already has a specific label—like a person's name (`Thomas`), a definite article (`der, die, das`), or a possessive word (`mein, dein, sein`)—put **`nicht`** in front of it:
* *Das ist Thomas.* ➔ **`Das ist nicht Thomas.`** (*That is not Thomas.*)
* *Das ist mein Auto.* ➔ **`Das ist nicht mein Auto.`** (*That is not my car.*)
* *Das ist der Lehrer.* ➔ **`Das ist nicht der Lehrer.`** (*That is not the teacher.*)

#### 4. To Negate a Place or Time Phrase with a Preposition (`in Berlin`, `am Montag`, `zu Hause`):
Put **`nicht`** right in front of the place or time phrase:
* *Ich wohne in Berlin.* ➔ **`Ich wohne nicht in Berlin.`** (*I do not live in Berlin.*)
* *Er ist zu Hause.* ➔ **`Er ist nicht zu Hause.`** (*He is not at home.*)
* *Wir kommen am Montag.* ➔ **`Wir kommen nicht am Montag.`** (*We are not coming on Monday.*)

---

### Bonus Pattern from Your Class Notebook: `nicht / kein … sondern …` ("not X, but rather Y!")

In English, the word **"but"** has two slightly different jobs:
1. Normal contrast: *"I would like to come, **but** (`aber`) I have no time."*
2. Correcting a wrong idea right after `nicht` or `kein`: *"That is **not** a blackboard, **but rather** (`sondern`) a TV!"*

In your class notebook (Batch 02, Page 18), you learned the special connector **`sondern`** (*but rather / instead*).
**Golden Rule for `sondern`:** Whenever you first say what something is **NOT** (using `nicht` or `kein/keine`), and then immediately replace it with the **correct** fact, use **`sondern`**!

Let us look at the exact examples from your notebook and everyday life:
1. **`Ist das eine Tafel? — Nein, das ist keine Tafel, sondern (das ist) ein Fernseher!`**
   * *Translation:* Is that a blackboard? — No, that is **not a** blackboard, **but rather** a television!
2. **`Das ist kein Stift, sondern ein Kuli.`**
   * *Translation:* That is **not a** generic pen, **but rather** a ballpoint pen.
3. **`Ich komme nicht aus Deutschland, sondern aus Indien.`**
   * *Translation:* I do **not** come from Germany, **but rather** from India.
4. **`Er wohnt nicht in München, sondern in Berlin.`**
   * *Translation:* He does **not** live in Munich, **but rather** in Berlin.
5. **`Heute trinke ich keinen Kaffee, sondern Tee.`**
   * *Translation:* Today I am **not** drinking coffee, **but rather** tea.

## 6. How to Tell the Time (`Die Uhrzeit`) & Ordering and Paying (`Bestellen und Bezahlen`)

### Part A: Telling Clock Time (`Die Uhrzeit`) — Step by Step

Knowing how to tell the time is essential for train stations, appointments, German classes, and the telc / Goethe A1 listening exam!

#### 1. How to Ask for the Time
There are two polite, everyday ways to ask *"What time is it?"* in German. Both mean the exact same thing:
* <GermanAudio text="Wie spät ist es?" /> — **`Wie spät ist es?`** *(Literally: "How late is it?" = What time is it?)*
* <GermanAudio text="Wie viel Uhr ist es?" /> — **`Wie viel Uhr ist es?`** *(Literally: "How much o'clock is it?" = What time is it?)*

And every answer starts with:
* **`Es ist ...`** (*It is ...*)

When you want to ask **"At what time...?"** something happens, use **`Um wie viel Uhr ...?`** and answer with **`Um ... Uhr`** (*At ... o'clock*):
* **`Um wie viel Uhr beginnt der Deutschkurs? — Um 9 Uhr!`** (*At what time does the German course start? — At 9 o'clock!*)

---

#### 2. The Two Ways to Tell Time in German: Official (`Offiziell`) vs. Informal (`Inoffiziell / Privat`)

Just like in English (where you can say *"fifteen thirty"* at the airport or *"half past three"* with friends), German has two ways to read the clock:

##### Way 1: Official Time (`Offiziell` — Radio, TV, Train Stations, Doctor's Appointments)
Official German time is the easiest system in the world!
* It uses the **24-hour clock** (`0` to `24` hours) so you never confuse morning and evening.
* **Formula:** **`[Hour] + Uhr + [Minutes]`**
* Simply read the hour number, say the word **`Uhr`**, and read the minute number!

| Digital Clock | Official German (`[Hour] + Uhr + [Minutes]`) | English Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **08:00** | *Es ist **acht Uhr**.* | It is 08:00 (8:00 AM). |
| **09:15** | *Es ist **neun Uhr fünfzehn**.* | It is 09:15 (9:15 AM). |
| **12:30** | *Es ist **zwölf Uhr dreißig**.* | It is 12:30 (12:30 PM). |
| **14:00** | *Es ist **vierzehn Uhr**.* | It is 14:00 (2:00 PM). |
| **15:15** | *Es ist **fünfzehn Uhr fünfzehn**.* | It is 15:15 (3:15 PM). |
| **15:30** | *Es ist **fünfzehn Uhr dreißig**.* | It is 15:30 (3:30 PM). |
| **15:45** | *Es ist **fünfzehn Uhr fünfundvierzig**.* | It is 15:45 (3:45 PM). |
| **20:20** | *Es ist **zwanzig Uhr zwanzig**.* | It is 20:20 (8:20 PM). |

---

##### Way 2: Informal / Everyday Spoken Time (`Inoffiziell / Privat` — Friends, Family, Daily Life)
In everyday conversation, Germans use the **12-hour clock** (`1` to `12`) and four key helper words:
* **`nach`** = **after / past** (*fünf **nach** drei* = 5 past 3)
* **`vor`** = **before / to** (*fünf **vor** vier* = 5 to 4)
* **`Viertel`** = **quarter (15 minutes)** (*Viertel **nach** drei* = quarter past 3; *Viertel **vor** vier* = quarter to 4)
* **`halb`** = **halfway TO the NEXT hour!**
* **`kurz`** = **shortly / just (1 to 3 minutes)** (*kurz **nach** drei* = just after 3; *kurz **vor** vier* = almost 4)

:::danger THE #1 CLOCK RULE IN GERMAN: `halb vier` means **3:30** (NOT 4:30)!
In English, *"half past three"* looks **backward** at the hour 3 (`3:30`).
In German, **`halb`** looks **FORWARD** to the **next hour** that is half full!
* Think of a glass of water: **`halb vier`** means *"halfway to four o'clock"* = **3:30**!
* **`halb fünf`** = *"halfway to five"* = **4:30**!
* **`halb eins`** = *"halfway to one"* = **12:30**!
* **`halb zehn`** = *"halfway to ten"* = **9:30**!
**Quick Formula:** Whenever you see `:30`, **add +1 to the hour**! (`3:30` ➔ `3 + 1 = 4` ➔ **`halb vier`**!)
:::

Also look closely at **`:25`** and **`:35`** around the half hour (`halb`):
* **3:25** is 5 minutes **before** 3:30 (`halb vier`) ➔ **`fünf vor halb vier`**!
* **3:35** is 5 minutes **after** 3:30 (`halb vier`) ➔ **`fünf nach halb vier`**!
* For **3:20** and **3:40**, you can either count from the full hour (*zwanzig nach drei* / *zwanzig vor vier*) OR count 10 minutes from the half hour (*zehn vor halb vier* / *zehn nach halb vier*)!

  *Why:* `Ein neues Auto` ("a new car") is ONE grammatical chunk in Position 1. The verb **`kaufe`** sits in Position 2, followed immediately by the subject **`ich`** in Position 3!
</details>

<details>

  **Answer:** **Wrong!** Correct sentence: **`Morgen arbeite ich in Berlin.`**
  *Why:* In *Morgen ich arbeite*, the verb *arbeite* was pushed to Position 3. In German, the verb must stay in Position 2!
</details>

</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>18. helfen (er) & treffen (sie - she):</strong> „Thomas ________ seinem Freund, und am Abend ________ Maria ihre Freundin.“</summary>

  **Answer:** **`hilft`** / **`trifft`**
  *Why:* Both `helfen` and `treffen` belong to Group 1 (`e ➔ i`): `er hilft`, `sie trifft`.
</details>

### Step 4: Negation with `kein / keine`, `nicht`, and `sondern` (Questions 19–24)

Choose **`kein` (`keine / keinen`)**, **`nicht`**, or **`sondern`** to complete each negative sentence.

<details>
  <summary><strong>19. Negate the noun:</strong> <em>Das ist ein Tisch (`der Tisch`).</em> ➔ „Nein, das ist ________ Tisch.“</summary>

  **Answer:** **`kein`** (*„Nein, das ist **kein** Tisch.“*)
  *Why:* The positive sentence has `ein Tisch` (a noun with an indefinite article). To negate `ein`, we use **`kein`**.
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>20. Negate the noun:</strong> <em>Ich habe eine Schwester (`die Schwester`) und Kinder (`die Kinder`).</em> ➔ „Ich habe ________ Schwester und ________ Kinder.“</summary>

  **Answer:** **`keine`** / **`keine`** (*„Ich habe **keine** Schwester und **keine** Kinder.“*)
  *Why:* Feminine nouns (`eine Schwester`) and plural nouns with no article (`Kinder`) both negate with **`keine`**.
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>21. Negate the verb & adjective:</strong> „Heute arbeite ich ________, denn das Wetter ist ________ gut.“</summary>

  **Answer:** **`nicht`** / **`nicht`** (*„Heute arbeite ich **nicht**, denn das Wetter ist **nicht** gut.“*)
  *Why:* To negate an action verb (`arbeite`) or an adjective (`gut`), we always use **`nicht`**!
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>22. Negate a name & a place:</strong> „Das ist ________ Thomas, und er wohnt ________ in München.“</summary>

  **Answer:** **`nicht`** / **`nicht`** (*„Das ist **nicht** Thomas, und er wohnt **nicht** in München.“*)
  *Why:* Proper names (`Thomas`) and prepositional place phrases (`in München`) always negate with **`nicht`**.
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>23. Negate an uncountable noun:</strong> <em>Ich habe Geld (`das Geld`) und Zeit (`die Zeit`).</em> ➔ „Leider habe ich heute ________ Geld und ________ Zeit.“</summary>

  **Answer:** **`kein`** / **`keine`** (*„Leider habe ich heute **kein** Geld und **keine** Zeit.“*)
  *Why:* Uncountable nouns without an article take `kein/keine` matching their gender: `das Geld` ➔ **`kein Geld`**, `die Zeit` ➔ **`keine Zeit`**.
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>24. Correcting a negative statement ("not X, but rather Y"):</strong> „Das ist keine Tafel, ________ ein Fernseher!“</summary>

  **Answer:** **`sondern`** (*„Das ist keine Tafel, **sondern** ein Fernseher!“*)
  *Why:* Whenever we correct a negative word (`nicht` or `kein/keine`) with the real answer ("not a blackboard, **but rather** a TV"), we use **`sondern`**!
</details>

### Step 5: Clock Time (`Die Uhrzeit`) & Ordering in a Café (Questions 25–30)

Write the German clock time or café expression!

<details>
  <summary><strong>25. Clock Time (3:15 and 3:45 informal):</strong> How do you say <strong>3:15</strong> and <strong>3:45</strong> in everyday spoken German?</summary>

  **Answer:**
  * **3:15** = **`Es ist Viertel nach drei.`** (quarter past three)
  * **3:45** = **`Es ist Viertel vor vier.`** (quarter to four)
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>26. The Famous Half-Hour Trap (3:30 and 7:30 informal):</strong> What does <em>„Es ist halb vier“</em> mean? And how do you say <strong>7:30</strong> in informal German?</summary>

  **Answer:**
  * *„Es ist halb vier“* means **3:30** ("halfway to four"!).
  * **7:30** in informal German is **`Es ist halb acht`** ("halfway to eight"!).
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>27. Around the Half Hour (3:25 and 3:35 informal):</strong> How do you say <strong>3:25</strong> and <strong>3:35</strong> in everyday German?</summary>

  **Answer:**
  * **3:25** = **`Es ist fünf vor halb vier.`** (5 minutes before 3:30)
  * **3:35** = **`Es ist fünf nach halb vier.`** (5 minutes after 3:30)
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>28. Official 24-Hour Time (14:15 and 18:30):</strong> How do they announce <strong>14:15</strong> and <strong>18:30</strong> at a German train station?</summary>

  **Answer:**
  * **14:15** = **`Es ist vierzehn Uhr fünfzehn.`**
  * **18:30** = **`Es ist achtzehn Uhr dreißig.`**
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>29. Ordering in a Café:</strong> How do you politely tell the waiter: <em>"I would like a coffee, please. The bill, please!"</em>?</summary>

  **Answer:** **`Ich möchte einen Kaffee, bitte. Die Rechnung, bitte!`**
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>30. Paying & Tipping:</strong> The waiter asks: <em>„Zahlen Sie zusammen oder getrennt? Bar oder mit Karte?“</em> and says the bill is 6,50 €. How do you say: <em>"Together, please. In cash. 7 euros — keep the change!"</em>?</summary>

  **Answer:** **`Zusammen, bitte. Ich zahle bar. Sieben Euro — stimmt so!`**
</details>
