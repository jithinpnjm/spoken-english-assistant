# Chapter 1: Basics — Alphabet, Sounds, Greetings, Basic Phrases & Introducing Yourself

Welcome to your very first German lesson! Whether you are preparing to move to Germany, planning to take the **telc Deutsch A1** or **Goethe-Zertifikat A1** exam, or starting from absolute zero for work and study, you are in the right place.

In this chapter, we start completely from scratch. We assume you do not know a single word of German yet. Step by step, in plain everyday English, you will learn how to read and pronounce German letters, how to greet people politely at different times of the day, how to use essential classroom and survival phrases, and how to answer all 23 classic self-introduction questions from your class notebook.

---

## 🎯 What you will learn

* I can say all **30 characters** of the German alphabet (**26 standard letters + 4 special characters**: **ä, ö, ü, ß**) with their friendly German letter names.
* I can avoid the classic phone-spelling traps for English speakers (**A / E / I**, **J / G**, **V / W**, and **Z**).
* I can tell the difference between **long vowel sounds** and **short vowel sounds** just by looking at the consonants that follow them.
* I can pronounce every common German letter combination (**ie** vs. **ei**, **eu / äu**, **au**, **st / sp**, **-er**, **ch**, **sch**, **tsch**, **kn**, **pf**, **ph**, **qu**, **th**, and **-ig**).
* I understand when to use formal **Sie** (with strangers, officials, and teachers) and informal **du** (with friends, family, and children).
* I can greet people and say goodbye politely at any time of the day, and use the four everyday meanings of **bitte**.
* I can ask **"How are you?"** both formally (*Wie geht es Ihnen?*) and informally (*Wie geht es dir? / Wie geht's?*) and answer naturally with *Mir geht es gut, danke*.
* I can ask and answer **all 23 self-introduction questions** about my name, first name, last name, origin, city, workplace, job, mother tongue, languages, age, marital status, hobbies, phone number, area code, and postal code.

## 1. The German Alphabet: 26 Letters + 4 Special Characters (30 Characters Total)

### Why German Pronunciation is Much Friendlier Than English

If you already speak English, you have actually survived one of the most unpredictable spelling systems in the world! Think about how the letters **o-u-g-h** sound completely different in the English words *th**ou**gh*, *thr**ou**gh*, *t**ou**gh*, and *th**ou**ght*. Or think about how the letter **a** sounds different in *c**a**t*, *c**a**r*, *c**a**ke*, and *w**a**ter*.

**German is much kinder to beginners.** Once you learn the sound of a German letter or letter combination, it makes that exact same sound almost every single time! There are no random silent letters at the end of words (like the silent *e* in English *name* or *make*), and you can read almost any German street sign, train station name, or form out loud on your very first day.

### The Mental Model: 26 Familiar Letters + 4 Bonus Characters

The German alphabet uses the exact same **26 Latin letters (A to Z)** that you know from English, plus **4 special characters** that sit at the end of the alphabet chart:
1. **Ä / ä** (A with two dots)
2. **Ö / ö** (O with two dots)
3. **Ü / ü** (U with two dots)
4. **ß** (called *Eszett* or *scharfes S* — a sharp double-S sound)

That gives you **30 characters in total**.

### The Phone Spelling Traps for English Speakers

When English speakers spell their name out loud in German for the first time, four groups of letters cause almost 90% of all mistakes. Why? Because the English name of one letter often sounds just like the German name of a *completely different* letter!

Let's put these traps side by side so you never fall for them:

#### Trap 1: The Vowel Triangle (A — E — I)
In English, we say **A = "ay"**, **E = "ee"**, **I = "eye"**.  
In German, every single one of those shifts by one step!
* **A** is **"ah"** (If you say English *"ay"*, a German listener will write down the letter **E**!)
* **E** is **"ay"** (If you say English *"ee"*, a German listener will write down the letter **I**!)
* **I** is **"ee"** (If you say English *"eye"*, a German listener will write down the two letters **E-I**!)

**Easy Memory Trick for A – E – I:** Repeat the rhythm **"Ah — Ay — Ee"** three times out loud before you spell any name containing A, E, or I.

#### Trap 2: The J and G Swap
* In German, **G** is called **"gay"** (with a hard *g* like *gate*). Because English speakers call the letter **J** *"jay"*, people frequently mix up **G** and **J**.
* In German, **J** has a completely unique name: **"yott"** (rhymes with *hot* or *lot*). Whenever your name has a **J** (like *Jithin, Joseph, Julia,* or *Jürgen*), say **"yott"** loud and clear!

#### Trap 3: The V and W Twins
* **V** is called **"faow"** (rhymes with *how* and *cow*) and sounds like an English **F**.
* **W** is called **"vay"** (rhymes with *day*) and sounds like an English **V**.
* Watch out: If you want to spell **W** and you accidentally say *"vee"*, a German speaker will get confused. Remember: **V = faow, W = vay**!

#### Trap 4: The Crisp Z ("tsett")
* Never call **Z** *"zee"* or *"zed"*. In German, *"zee"* sounds close to **C ("tsay")** or the word *Sie*.
* Always call **Z** **"tsett"**!

## 2. Sounds & Pronunciations Made Simple

Now let's look at how letters team up inside German words to create clear, predictable sounds.

### A. Long vs. Short Vowel Sounds

First, what is a **vowel** and what is a **consonant**?
* **Vowels** are the open, musical sounds you make without blocking the air in your mouth: **a, e, i, o, u** (plus **ä, ö, ü**).
* **Consonants** are all the other letters (**b, c, d, f, g, k, l, m, n, p, r, s, t, v, w, z**, and so on) where your lips, teeth, or tongue tap or block the air.

In German, every vowel can be pronounced either **long** (held out smoothly for an extra beat) or **short** (clipped and quick). And here is the best part: **German spelling tells you right away whether a vowel is long or short!**

#### Rule 1: When is a German Vowel LONG?
A vowel is pronounced **long and smooth** when:
1. It is followed by only **one single consonant**:
   * **der Name** (the name) — long *ah* before single *m*
   * **kam** (came) — long *ah* before single *m*
   * **das Sofa** (the sofa) — long *oh* before single *f*
   * **der Tag** (the day) — long *ah* before single *g*
   * **gut** (good) — long *oo* before single *t*
2. It is **doubled** (*aa, ee, oo*):
   * **der Tee** (the tea), **der Kaffee** (the coffee), **das Haar** (the hair), **das Boot** (the boat)
3. It is followed by a **silent h** (*ah, eh, ih, oh, uh, äh, öh, üh*):
   * **wohnen** (to live — long *oh*), **gehen** (to go — long *ay*), **die Uhr** (the clock — long *oo*), **das Jahr** (the year — long *ah*)
4. For the vowel **i**, it is written as **ie**:
   * **sieben** (seven), **vier** (four), **lieben** (to love)

#### Rule 2: When is a German Vowel SHORT?
A vowel is pronounced **short, crisp, and quick** when it is followed by **two or more consonants** (especially double consonants like *mm, nn, tt, ll, ss, ff, ck, tz*):
* **der Kamm** (the comb) — short *a* before double *mm* (compare long *kam* vs. short *Kamm*!)
* **die Stadt** (the city) — short *a* before *dt*
* **das Bett** (the bed) — short *e* before double *tt*
* **die Sonne** (the sun) — short *o* before double *nn*
* **kommen** (to come) — short *o* before double *mm*
* **das Wasser** (the water) — short *a* before double *ss*
* **die Mutter** (the mother) — short *u* before double *tt*

| Vowel | Long Vowel Example (1 Consonant / Silent h / Double Vowel) | Short Vowel Example (2+ Consonants / Double Consonant) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A / a** | **der Name** (name), **kam** (came), **der Tag** (day) | **der Kamm** (comb), **die Stadt** (city), **der Mann** (man) |
| **E / e** | **der Weg** (way), **gehen** (to go), **der Tee** (tea) | **das Bett** (bed), **nett** (nice), **essen** (to eat) |
| **I / i** | **wir** (we), **vier** (four), **ihr** (you all / her) | **in** (in), **mit** (with), **immer** (always), **bitte** (please) |
| **O / o** | **das Sofa** (sofa), **wohnen** (to live), **rot** (red) | **die Sonne** (sun), **kommen** (to come), **offen** (open) |
| **U / u** | **gut** (good), **die Schule** (school), **die Uhr** (clock) | **die Mutter** (mother), **der Hund** (dog), **und** (and) |

### B. The Special Consonants Explained Simply (S, V, W, J, Z)

Let's zoom in on the five single consonants from your course notes that behave differently from English:

1. **S / s** — When **s** stands at the beginning of a word or syllable right before a vowel, it makes a soft, buzzing English **Z** sound (like the *z* in *zoo* or *zero*):
   * **die Sonne** (the sun) ➔ sounds like *"zon-nuh"*
   * **sieben** (seven) ➔ sounds like *"zee-ben"*
   * **das Sofa** (the sofa) ➔ sounds like *"zoh-fah"*
   * **singen** (to sing) ➔ sounds like *"zing-en"*
   * *(At the very end of a word, like **das Haus** or **das Eis**, it makes a normal soft English **s** sound.)*

2. **V / v (faow)** — In native German words, **v** sounds exactly like an English **F**!
   * **der Vater** (the father) ➔ sounds like *"fah-ter"*
   * **vier** (four) ➔ sounds like *"feer"*
   * **verstehen** (to understand) ➔ starts with *"fer-"*
   * **viel** (much / a lot) ➔ sounds like *"feel"*
   * **von** (from / of) ➔ sounds like *"fon"*
   * *(Only in a few imported foreign words like **die Vase** or **das Visum** does it sound like an English v.)*

3. **W / w (vay)** — In German, **w** ALWAYS sounds like an English **V** (with your top teeth gently touching your bottom lip, never rounding your lips like an English *w*!):
   * **das Wasser** (the water) ➔ sounds like *"vah-ser"*
   * **wir** (we) ➔ sounds like *"veer"*
   * **wo** (where) ➔ sounds like *"voh"*
   * **wohnen** (to live) ➔ sounds like *"voh-nen"*
   * **wie** (how) ➔ sounds like *"vee"*

4. **J / j (yott)** — In German, **j** ALWAYS sounds like an English **Y** (as in *yes, yellow, you*):
   * **ja** (yes) ➔ sounds like *"yah"*
   * **das Jahr** (the year) ➔ sounds like *"yahr"*
   * **jung** (young) ➔ sounds like *"yoong"*
   * **die Jacke** (the jacket) ➔ sounds like *"yah-kuh"*
   * **der Januar** (January) ➔ sounds like *"yah-noo-ahr"*

5. **Z / z (tsett)** — In German, **z** ALWAYS makes a sharp, crisp **TS** sound (like the *ts* at the end of *cats*, *hats*, or the middle of *pizza*):
   * **zehn** (ten) ➔ sounds like *"tsayn"*
   * **das Zimmer** (the room) ➔ sounds like *"tsim-mer"*
   * **der Zug** (the train) ➔ sounds like *"tsook"*
   * **zwei** (two) ➔ sounds like *"tsvai"*
   * **zahlen** (to pay) ➔ sounds like *"tsah-len"*

### C. The 3 Umlauts (Ä, Ö, Ü) and ß (Eszett)

What is an **Umlaut**? *Umlaut* simply means "changed sound." When two dots are placed over **a, o, u** to create **ä, ö, ü**, the sound moves forward toward the front of your mouth.

Never ignore the two dots! In German, the dots can change the entire meaning of a word:
* **schon** (without dots) = *already*
* **schön** (with two dots) = *beautiful / nice*
* **die Mutter** (without dots) = *the mother (1 mother)*
* **die Mütter** (with two dots) = *the mothers (plural)*

Let's learn how to make each of the four special characters effortlessly:

| Character | Letter Name | Simple Mouth Trick | Everyday Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ä / ä** | **ah-umlaut** (*"ay"*) | Sounds just like a clear **"ay"** (as in *air* or *bear*) when long, or exactly like the short **"e"** in *bed* when short! | **der Bär** (the bear), **spät** (late), **das Mädchen** (the girl), **die Äpfel** (the apples), **März** (March) |
| **Ö / ö** | **oh-umlaut** (*"eyo"*) | Round your lips into a tight circle as if you are going to say **"oh"**, hold your lips frozen in that circle, and say **"ay"** (like *bed/day*)! | **schön** (beautiful), **öffnen** (to open), **zwölf** (twelve), **hören** (to hear), **Österreich** (Austria), **Köln** (Cologne) |
| **Ü / ü** | **oo-umlaut** (*"eyu"*) | Push your lips forward tightly as if you are going to **whistle** (or say *"oo"*), hold your lips frozen there, and say a clear **"ee"** (like *see*)! | **fünf** (five), **die Tür** (the door), **über** (over / about), **für** (for), **grün** (green), **München** (Munich), **Tschüss** (bye) |
| **ß** | **Eszett / scharfes S** | Looks a bit like a capital B, but it is actually a **sharp double-S sound** (like the *ss* in *kiss* or *bus*, never a buzzing *z*). | **heißen** (to be called), **die Straße** (the street), **groß** (big / tall), **dreißig** (thirty), **weiß** (white / knows) |

#### When Does German Write `ß` vs. `ss`?
Both **ß** and **ss** make the exact same unvoiced *"ss"* sound! The difference is all about the vowel right before them:
* Write **`ß`** after a **long vowel** or a **two-vowel team** (*ei, au, eu*): **die Straße** (long *ah*), **groß** (long *oh*), **heißen** (after *ei*), **dreißig** (after *ei*).
* Write **`ss`** after a **short vowel**: **essen** (short *e*), **das Wasser** (short *a*), **müssen** (short *ü*), **wissen** (short *i*).
* Also note: **ß** never appears at the beginning of a word, and in Switzerland people always write *ss* instead of *ß* (*Strasse*).

### D. Complete Guide to German Character Combinations

When two, three, or four letters stand side by side in German, they form a fixed team with a single reliable sound. Let's go through **every single combination** from your course notes step by step!

#### 1. `ie` vs. `ei` — The #1 Sound Pair to Master!
English speakers mix up **ie** and **ei** all the time until they learn this one golden trick:
> 💡 **The Second-Letter Trick:** Cover up the first letter and say the **English name of the SECOND letter**!
> * In **`i` + `e`**, the second letter is **E** ➔ say a long **"ee"** (like *see, tree, green*)!
> * In **`e` + `i`**, the second letter is **I** ➔ say **"eye / ai"** (like *my, sky, bike*)!

Let's compare them side by side:
* **`ie` = long "ee" sound:**
  * **sieben** (*zee-ben* — seven)
  * **vier** (*feer* — four)
  * **lieben** (*lee-ben* — to love)
  * **Wien** (*veen* — Vienna)
  * **Sie / sie** (*zee* — you / she / they)
  * **hier** (*heer* — here)
  * **spielen** (*shpee-len* — to play)
  * **das Papier** (*pah-peer* — the paper)
* **`ei` = "eye / ai" sound:**
  * **mein** (*mine* — my)
  * **sein** (*zine* — his / to be)
  * **eins** (*eyens* — one)
  * **zwei** (*tsvai* — two)
  * **drei** (*drai* — three)
  * **nein** (*nine* — no)
  * **heißen** (*hai-sen* — to be called)
  * **der Wein** (*vine* — the wine)
  * **arbeiten** (*ar-bai-ten* — to work)

*(Bonus note: A few words spell the "eye" sound as **`ai`**, such as **der Mai** [May] and **Frankfurt am Main** — it sounds identical to `ei`!)*

#### 2. `eu` and `äu` = "oye" (like *boy, toy, coin*)
Never pronounce **eu** like English *"yoo"*! In German, both **`eu`** and **`äu`** always sound like **"oye"**:
* **`eu` = "oye":**
  * **der Euro** (*oy-ro* — the euro)
  * **Europa** (*oy-ro-pah* — Europe)
  * **heute** (*hoy-tuh* — today)
  * **neun** (*noyn* — nine)
  * **der Freund** (*froynd* — the friend)
  * **neu** (*noy* — new)
  * **Deutsch / Deutschland** (*doytsh / doytsh-lahnt* — German / Germany)
* **`äu` = "oye" (exact same sound as `eu`!):**
  * **die Häuser** (*hoy-zer* — the houses, plural of *das Haus*)
  * **die Bäume** (*boy-muh* — the trees, plural of *der Baum*)
  * **läuft** (*loyft* — runs / walks)
  * **verkaufen ➔ der Verkäufer** (*fer-koy-fer* — the salesperson)

#### 3. `au` = "ow / ou" (like *house, mouse, cow*)
Whenever you see **`au`**, pronounce it like the **"ow"** in *how now brown cow* (never like the *"aw"* in English *August*):
* **das Haus** (*hows* — the house)
* **aus** (*ows* — from / out of)
* **das Auto** (*ow-toh* — the car)
* **kaufen** (*kow-fen* — to buy)
* **die Frau** (*frow* — the woman / Mrs.)
* **auch** (*owkh* — also / too)
* **blau** (*blow* — blue)

#### 4. `st` and `sp` at the Start of a Word = "sht" and "shp"
Whenever a German word (or main word stem) starts with **`st-`** or **`sp-`**, pretend there is an invisible *h* after the *s*!
* **`st-` at the start = "sht":**
  * **die Stadt** (*shtat* — the city)
  * **die Straße** (*shtrah-suh* — the street)
  * **die Stunde** (*shtoon-duh* — the hour / lesson)
  * **der Stuhl** (*shtool* — the chair)
  * **der Stift** (*shtift* — the pen / pencil)
  * **stehen** (*shtay-en* — to stand)
  * **buchstabieren** (*bookh-shtah-bee-ren* — to spell)
* **`sp-` at the start = "shp":**
  * **der Sport** (*shport* — the sport)
  * **spielen** (*shpee-len* — to play)
  * **sprechen** (*shpre-chen* — to speak)
  * **die Sprache** (*shprah-khuh* — the language)
  * **spät** (*shpayt* — late)
  * **der Spitzer** (*shpit-tser* — the pencil sharpener)

*(Note: If `st` comes at the **end** of a word, like **ist** [is] or **bist** [are], it is pronounced as a normal crisp **"st"**, not "sht"!)*

#### 5. `-er` at the End of a Word = Soft "uh / a"
When **`-er`** appears at the very end of a word, German speakers do **not** curl their tongue to make a heavy American *"errr"* sound. Instead, relax your mouth and pronounce **`-er`** at the end of a word like a soft, gentle **"uh" / "ah"** (like the *-a* at the end of *sofa*):
* **der Vater** (*fah-tuh* — the father)
* **die Mutter** (*moot-tuh* — the mother)
* **der Bruder** (*broo-duh* — the brother)
* **die Schwester** (*shves-tuh* — the sister)
* **das Wasser** (*vah-suh* — the water)
* **das Zimmer** (*tsim-muh* — the room)
* **der Lehrer** (*lay-ruh* — the teacher)
* **der Computer** (*kom-pyoo-tuh* — the computer)

#### 6. `ch` — The Two Friendly Sounds (Soft Whisper vs. Throat Sound)
In German, **`ch`** never sounds like the English *"ch"* in *chair* or *cheese*. Instead, it has two easy sounds depending on which vowel comes right before it:
* **1. The Soft Whisper `ch` (after *i, e, ä, ö, ü, ei, eu, äu* or consonants *l, n, r*):**
  Smile slightly, lift the middle of your tongue toward the roof of your mouth, and blow air gently—it sounds just like the beginning of the English word **h**uge or **h**uman (or a kitten hissing softly!):
  * **ich** (I), **mich** (me), **sprechen** (to speak), **die Kirche** (the church), **die Milch** (the milk), **nicht** (not), **rechts** (right), **das Mädchen** (the girl), **welche** (which)
* **2. The Soft Throat `ch` (after dark vowels *a, o, u, au*):**
  Made softly in the back of the throat, like gently clearing your throat or fogging up a pair of glasses:
  * **machen** (to make / do), **das Buch** (the book), **auch** (also), **kochen** (to cook), **die Nacht** (the night), **die Woche** (the week), **acht** (eight), **suchen** (to look for)
* **Bonus `chs` = "ks":** When `ch` is followed by `s`, it sounds like **ks** (as in *six*): **sechs** (*zeks* — six)!

#### 7. `sch` = "sh" and `tsch` = "ch"
* **`sch` (3 letters) = English "sh" (as in *shoe, ship, fish*):**
  * **die Schule** (*shoo-luh* — the school)
  * **schön** (*shern* — beautiful / nice)
  * **schreiben** (*shrai-ben* — to write)
  * **der Tisch** (*tish* — the table)
  * **der Fisch** (*fish* — the fish)
  * **Englisch** (*eng-lish* — English)
* **`tsch` (4 letters) = English "ch" (as in *church, chair, match*):**
  * **Deutsch** (*doytsh* — German)
  * **Deutschland** (*doytsh-lahnt* — Germany)
  * **Tschüss!** (*tshoos* — Bye!)
  * **Entschuldigung** (*ent-shool-dee-goong* — Excuse me / Sorry)

#### 8. More Essential Combinations from Your Notes (`kn`, `pf`, `ph`, `qu`, `th`, `-ig`)
Let's make sure you know these six important combinations from Chapter 1 of your course notes:
* **`kn` — Pronounce BOTH the K and the N!**
  In English, the *k* in *knee* and *knife* is silent. In German, **there is no silent K**! You say the **k** and the **n** right after each other (*"k-n"*):
  * **das Knie** (*k-nee* — the knee)
  * **der Knopf** (*k-nopf* — the button)
  * **der Knoblauch** (*k-nob-lowkh* — the garlic)
* **`pf` — Pronounce BOTH the P and the F together!**
  Start with your lips closed for **p** and immediately blow into an **f** (*"pf"*):
  * **der Apfel** (*ap-fel* — the apple)
  * **das Pferd** (*pfert* — the horse)
  * **der Pfeffer** (*pfef-fuh* — the pepper)
* **`ph` = "f" (just like in English):**
  * **das Alphabet** (*al-fah-bayt* — the alphabet)
  * **die Phase** (*fah-zuh* — the phase)
  * *(Note: Modern German spells most everyday words directly with **F**, like **das Foto** and **das Telefon**!)*
* **`qu` = "kv" (not English "kw"):**
  * **das Quiz** (*kviz* — the quiz)
  * **die Quittung** (*kvit-toong* — the receipt)
  * **die Qualität** (*kvah-lee-tayt* — the quality)
* **`th` = crisp "t" (NEVER put your tongue between your teeth like English *think* or *this*!):**
  In German, `th` sounds 100% identical to a normal crisp **`t`**:
  * **das Theater** (*tay-ah-tuh* — the theater)
  * **das Thema** (*tay-mah* — the topic / theme)
  * **Thomas** (*toh-mas* — Thomas)
  * **die Apotheke** (*ah-poh-tay-kuh* — the pharmacy)
* **`-ig` at the end of a word = soft "-ich" (or "-ik" in Southern Germany):**
  When a word ends in **`-ig`** (especially numbers and adjectives), standard German pronounces it just like the soft **`-ich`** in *ich*:
  * **zwanzig** (*tsvan-tsich* — twenty)
  * **dreißig** (*drai-sich* — thirty)
  * **ledig** (*lay-dich* — single / unmarried)
  * **richtig** (*rich-tich* — correct / right)
  * **wichtig** (*vich-tich* — important)
  * **entschuldigen** (verb) vs. **billig** (*bil-lich* — cheap)

## 3. Greetings & Farewells in Daily Life (Begrüßung und Abschied)

Now that you can read and pronounce German words with confidence, let's start speaking! The very first words you will use every day in Germany are **greetings** (*die Begrüßung*) and **farewells** (*der Abschied*).

### The Mental Model: Formal (`Sie`) vs. Informal (`du`)

Before you greet someone or ask their name, German asks you to make one simple, polite choice: **What is my relationship with this person?**

In English, we use the single word **"you"** for everyone—whether we are talking to our best friend, a little child, a police officer, or a bank manager.  
In German, there are two ways to say "you", and each one has its own matching style of greeting:

| Feature | Formal (**Sie** — *Polite / Official*) | Informal (**du** — *Friendly / Casual*) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Who do you use it with?** | Strangers, shopkeepers, doctors, government officials, teachers, bosses, older adults you don't know well, and **examiners in your telc A1 exam** | Family members, close friends, children, teenagers, university students, and pets |
| **How do you address them?** | **Herr** (Mr.) or **Frau** (Ms./Mrs.) + **Last Name** (*Guten Tag, Herr Müller! / Guten Tag, Frau Weber!*) | **First Name** (*Hallo, Alex! / Morgen, Lea!*) |
| **Hello Greeting** | **Guten Morgen / Guten Tag / Guten Abend** | **Hallo! / Hi! / Morgen!** |
| **Goodbye** | **Auf Wiedersehen!** (in person) / **Auf Wiederhören!** (on the phone) | **Tschüss! / Ciao! / Bis bald!** |

> 💡 **Beginner Golden Rule:** When you meet an adult in Germany for the first time (at a shop, office, hotel, or exam), **always default to the formal style (`Guten Tag`, `Auf Wiedersehen`, and `Sie`)**. Using *Sie* shows warmth and respect!

### Time-of-Day Greetings (Begrüßung)

Let's look at how Germans greet each other from early morning until night:

| German Greeting | Pronunciation Guide | Literal / English Meaning | When to Use It |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Guten Morgen!** | *goo-ten mor-gen* | Good morning! | From waking up until around **11:00 AM / 12:00 PM (noon)** |
| **Guten Tag!** | *goo-ten tahk* | Good day! (Hello!) | From around **12:00 PM (noon) until 6:00 PM** (also works all day as a polite standard greeting) |
| **Guten Abend!** | *goo-ten ah-bent* | Good evening! | From around **6:00 PM (18:00)** onwards |
| **Hallo!** | *hah-loh* | Hello! / Hi! | Anytime of day! Friendly, warm, and used everywhere with friends and in casual shops |
| **Grüß Gott!** | *groos got* | Good day! (lit. "Greet God") | Very common in **Southern Germany (Bavaria / Munich)** and **Austria** |
| **Moin! / Moin Moin!** | *moyn* | Hello! / Good day! | Used **all day long** (even at night!) in **Northern Germany (Hamburg, Bremen, Kiel)** |
| **Grüezi!** | *groo-et-see* | Hello! (polite) | Used in **Switzerland** |
| **Herzlich willkommen!** | *herts-lich vil-kom-men* | Warm welcome! / Welcome! | Used when welcoming a guest to a course, home, hotel, or city |

#### ⚠️ Watch Out: Why *Gute Nacht!* is a Farewell, Not a "Hello"!
In English, if you meet a colleague at 9:00 PM in a restaurant, you say *"Good evening!"*, and when you go to bed, you say *"Good night!"*.  
German works the exact same way:
* When you **arrive** or greet someone at 8:00 PM or 9:00 PM, always say **„Guten Abend!“** (Good evening!).
* Only say **„Gute Nacht!“** (Good night!) right before someone goes to sleep in bed!

### Saying Goodbye (Abschied)

| German Farewell | Pronunciation Guide | English Meaning | When to Use It |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Auf Wiedersehen!** | *owf vee-der-zay-en* | Goodbye! (lit. "Until seeing again") | **Formal** goodbye when leaving in person (shops, offices, doctors, exams) |
| **Auf Wiederhören!** | *owf vee-der-her-en* | Goodbye on the phone! (lit. "Until hearing again") | **Formal** goodbye at the end of a **phone call** (because you *hear* each other on the phone, you don't *see* each other!) |
| **Tschüss!** | *tshoos* | Bye! | **Informal** and friendly — used with friends, classmates, and in everyday shops |
| **Bis bald!** | *bis balt* | See you soon! (lit. "Until soon") | When you will see the person again soon |
| **Bis später!** | *bis shpay-tuh* | See you later! (lit. "Until later") | When you will see the person again later the **same day** |
| **Bis morgen!** | *bis mor-gen* | See you tomorrow! | When you will see the person tomorrow (for example, at your German class!) |
| **Bis gleich!** | *bis glaich* | See you in a moment! | When you are stepping away for just a few minutes |
| **Bis Montag!** | *bis mohn-tahk* | See you on Monday! | When you will see them on Monday |
| **Schönen Tag noch!** | *sher-nen tahk nokh* | Have a nice day (still)! | Polite wish when leaving a shop or conversation during the day |
| **Schönen Abend noch!** | *sher-nen ah-bent nokh* | Have a nice evening! | Polite wish when leaving in the evening |
| **Ein schönes Wochenende!** | *ain sher-nes vokh-en-en-duh* | Have a nice weekend! | Said on Friday or Saturday when saying goodbye |
| **Danke, gleichfalls! / Ebenso!** | *dahn-kuh, glaich-fahls / ay-ben-zoh* | Thanks, you too! (Likewise!) | The magic reply whenever someone wishes you a nice day, evening, or weekend! |

## 4. Basic Everyday & Classroom Phrases

### A. Polite Magic Words: Ja, Nein, Danke, Entschuldigung & The 4 Meanings of *Bitte*

In German, politeness is built around five short words that you will use dozens of times a day:

1. **Ja** (*yah*) = **Yes**
2. **Nein** (*nine*) = **No**
3. **Danke!** / **Danke schön!** / **Vielen Dank!** = **Thank you! / Thank you very much! / Many thanks!**
4. **Entschuldigung!** (*ent-shool-dee-goong*) = **Excuse me! / I'm sorry!** (Used both to get someone's attention politely on the street and to apologize if you bump into someone.)
5. **Bitte!** (*bit-tuh*) — The Swiss-Army Knife of German Politeness!

In English, we have different words for *"Please"*, *"You're welcome"*, *"Here you go"*, and *"Pardon?"*. In German, the single word **bitte** does all four jobs! Let's see how:

| Situation | How You Use **Bitte** | English Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Asking for something politely** | *Ein Wasser, **bitte**.* / *Buchstabieren Sie das, **bitte**.* | **Please** (*A water, please. / Spell that, please.*) |
| **2. Replying after someone says "Danke!"** | Person A: *Vielen Dank!* ➔ Person B: ***Bitte! / Bitte schön! / Gern geschehen!*** | **You're welcome!** / **My pleasure!** |
| **3. Handing something to someone or offering a seat** | Waiter hands you a coffee: ***Bitte schön!*** | **Here you go! / Help yourself!** |
| **4. Asking someone to repeat what they just said** | ***Wie bitte?*** (*vee bit-tuh?*) | **Pardon? / Excuse me, what did you say?** |

### B. Asking "How Are You?" (Questions 18 & 19 from Your Notebook)

In your class notebook (`Batch 01`, Page 6), Questions 18 and 19 show the exact difference between asking a friend *"How are you?"* and asking an adult/stranger formally. Let's break them down word by word!

#### 1. Informal (With a Friend, Classmate, or Family Member):
* **Question:** **Wie geht es dir?** (or shortened in everyday speech: **Wie geht's?**)
  * *Word-by-word breakdown:* **Wie** (How) + **geht** (goes) + **es** (it) + **dir** (to you — informal) = *"How is it going for you?"*
* **Answer:** **Mir geht es gut, danke. Und dir?** (or simply: **Danke, gut! Und dir?**)
  * *Word-by-word breakdown:* **Mir** (To me) + **geht** (goes) + **es** (it) + **gut** (good/well), **danke** (thanks). **Und dir?** (And to you?)
* **Reply:** **Auch gut, danke!** (*Also good, thanks!*)

#### 2. Formal (With a Teacher, Stranger, Official, or Examiner):
* **Question:** **Wie geht es Ihnen?**
  * *Word-by-word breakdown:* **Wie** (How) + **geht** (goes) + **es** (it) + **Ihnen** (to you — formal, always capitalized!) = *"How are you doing?"*
* **Answer:** **Mir geht es gut, danke. Und Ihnen?**
  * *Word-by-word breakdown:* **Mir** (To me) + **geht es gut** (it goes well), **danke** (thank you). **Und Ihnen?** (And you — formal?)
* **Reply:** **Auch gut, danke!** (*Also good, thank you!*)

#### How to Answer Depending on Your Mood
* 😊 **Super! / Sehr gut!** = Super! / Very good!
* 🙂 **Gut, danke!** = Good, thanks!
* 😐 **Es geht.** = So-so / It's okay.
* 😔 **Nicht so gut.** = Not so good.
* 😴 **Ich bin müde.** = I am tired.

> ⚠️ **Important Beginner Warning: Never Say *"Ich bin gut"*!**  
> In English, we say *"I am good"*. If you translate that word-for-word as *„Ich bin gut“*, a German speaker hears *"I am skilled / talented / a morally good person"*!  
> To say how you are feeling, always say: **„Mir geht es gut!“** (or simply **„Danke, gut!“**).

## 8. Common Beginner Mistakes (❌ Wrong ➔ ✅ Right ➔ Why)

Check this table carefully—fixing these 10 habits today will put you ahead of 90% of A1 learners:

| ❌ Common Beginner Mistake | ✅ Correct German | 💡 Why It Happens & How to Remember |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ❌ *Ich bin gut, danke.* | ✅ **Mir geht es gut, danke.** | *Ich bin gut* means "I am skilled/talented." For your health or mood, always say **Mir geht es gut** (or **Danke, gut**). |
| ❌ *Ich komme in Indien.* | ✅ **Ich komme aus Indien.** | **Woher kommen Sie?** always takes **aus** (from your origin). **Wo wohnen Sie?** takes **in** (in your current city). |
| ❌ *Ich arbeite in der Ernakulam.* | ✅ **Ich arbeite in Ernakulam.** | Never put an article (*der, die, das*) before city names! Just say **in Ernakulam**, **in Kaloor**, **in Berlin**. |
| ❌ *Was ist Ihr Name?* | ✅ **Wie ist Ihr Name?** | German asks *"How is your name?"* (**Wie** ist Ihr Name?), never *Was*. |
| ❌ *Mein Muttersprache ist Malayalam.* | ✅ **Meine Muttersprache ist Malayalam.** | *Die Muttersprache* is feminine, so "my" adds an **-e**: **Meine Muttersprache**. |
| ❌ *Mein Hobbys ist Lesen und Reisen.* | ✅ **Meine Hobbys sind Lesen und Reisen.** | For plural hobbies (2 or more), use **Meine Hobbys sind …**; for 1 hobby, use **Mein Hobby ist …**. |
| ❌ Pronouncing **ei** in *mein* as *"meen"* | ✅ Pronounce **ei** as **"eye"** (*mine*) | Remember the Second-Letter Trick: **ei** ends in **i** (*"eye"*), while **ie** ends in **e** (*"ee"* as in *sieben*). |
| ❌ Pronouncing **w** in *Wasser* like English *w* | ✅ Pronounce **w** like English **V** (*vah-ser*) | In German, **W = English V** (*Wasser, wir, wohnen*), while **V = English F** (*Vater, vier*). |
| ❌ Saying *Gute Nacht!* when arriving at 8 PM | ✅ Say **Guten Abend!** when arriving | **Guten Abend** = Good evening (hello). **Gute Nacht** is only said right before going to bed to sleep! |
| ❌ Saying *Auf Wiedersehen!* at the end of a phone call | ✅ Say **Auf Wiederhören!** on the phone | On the phone you *hear* (*hören*) each other, so formal phone calls end with **Auf Wiederhören!** (or informal **Tschüss!**). |

  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  **Ess – Tay – Air – Ah – Eszett (or scharfes S) – Ay.**
</details>

5. How do you spell the surname **Müller** out loud using **Doppel-**?
<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  **Emm – Ü (U mit Punkten / Umlaut-U) – Doppel-Ell – Ay – Air.**
</details>

<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

### Step 2: Sounds, Vowels & Letter Combinations (Questions 7–12)

7. Look at the pair **kam** (came) and **der Kamm** (the comb). Which one has a **long** "ah" vowel and which one has a **short** "a" vowel? Why?
<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  **kam** has a **long** "ah" sound because it is followed by only **one consonant** (*m*). **der Kamm** has a **short** "a" sound because it is followed by **double consonants** (*mm*).
</details>

8. Do these words have the **"ee"** sound (like *see*) or the **"eye"** sound (like *my*)?  
   a) **vier** · b) **drei** · c) **heißen** · d) **sieben** · e) **mein** · f) **spielen**
<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  * **a) vier** = **"ee"** (*ie* ends in *e*)
  * **b) drei** = **"eye"** (*ei* ends in *i*)
  * **c) heißen** = **"eye"** (*ei* ends in *i*)
  * **d) sieben** = **"ee"** (*ie* ends in *e*)
  * **e) mein** = **"eye"** (*ei* ends in *i*)
  * **f) spielen** = **"ee"** (*ie* ends in *e*)
</details>

9. How do you pronounce the starting letters of **die Stadt**, **sprechen**, and **die Schule**?
<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  * **die Stadt** starts with **"sht"** (*shtat*) because `st-` is at the start of the word.
  * **sprechen** starts with **"shp"** (*shpre-chen*) because `sp-` is at the start of the word.
  * **die Schule** starts with **"sh"** (*shoo-luh*) because `sch` always sounds like English *sh*.
</details>

10. What sound do **`eu`** and **`äu`** make in **heute**, **der Euro**, **neun**, and **die Häuser**?
<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  They all make the exact same **"oye"** sound (like in English *boy* or *coin*): *hoy-tuh, oy-ro, noyn, hoy-zer*.
</details>

11. How do you pronounce the `k` in **das Knie** (knee) and **der Knopf** (button), and the `th` in **das Thema** (topic) and **das Theater** (theater)?
<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  * In **`kn`** (*Knie, Knopf*), the **k is NOT silent** — you pronounce both the **k** and the **n** (*k-nee, k-nopf*)!
  * In **`th`** (*Thema, Theater*), you pronounce a crisp **"t"** (*tay-mah, tay-ah-tuh*), never an English *th*.
</details>

12. Why do we write **heißen** and **die Straße** with **`ß`**, but **essen** and **das Wasser** with **`ss`**?
<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  We write **`ß`** after a **long vowel** (*die Straße*) or a **two-vowel combination** (*heißen*). We write **`ss`** after a **short vowel** (*essen, das Wasser*).
</details>

13. It is 9:30 AM and you walk into your doctor's office in Frankfurt. What do you say to the receptionist?

<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

    b) *Vielen Dank! — Bitte schön!*

    d) The examiner speaks too quietly and you ask: *Wie bitte?*
<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  * **a)** Please
  * **b)** You're welcome!
  * **c)** Here you go!
  * **d)** Pardon? / What did you say?
</details>

17. Your teacher asks you formally: **„Wie geht es Ihnen?“** Write a complete polite reply and ask the teacher back!
<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  **„Mir geht es gut, danke. Und Ihnen?“** (Never *Ich bin gut*!)
</details>

18. Your classmate Lea asks you informally: **„Hallo! Wie geht es dir?“** Write a complete friendly reply and ask her back!
<details>
  <summary>Show answer</summary>

  **„Mir geht es gut, danke. Und dir?“** (or **„Danke, gut! Und dir?“**).
</details>
