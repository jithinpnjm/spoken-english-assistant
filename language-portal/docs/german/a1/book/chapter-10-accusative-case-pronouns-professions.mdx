## 1. Finding the Subject (Nominativ) & Direct Object (Akkusativ) — Explained from Zero

### Why Does German Have "Cases" (*Kasus*)?

Up to now, whenever you learned a German noun in your vocabulary list, you learned it in its basic dictionary form:
* **der Tisch** (the table) — **ein Tisch** (a table)
* **die Tasche** (the bag) — **eine Tasche** (a bag)
* **das Buch** (the book) — **ein Buch** (a book)

That basic form is called the **Nominative Case (*der Nominativ*)**.
Whenever a person or thing is simply **being** something (*Das ist **ein Tisch***) or **doing** the action in a sentence (**Der Mann** schläft · **Das Kind** spielt), it stands in the **Nominativ**.

Now, what happens when a sentence has **two participants**—one person who **does** the action, and another thing (or person) that **receives** the action directly?

Look at this simple English sentence from our class notes:
* ***"Peter buys a table."*** (*Peter kauft einen Tisch.*)

In this sentence, we have two nouns: **Peter** and **a table**.
Do they have the same job in the sentence? **No!**
* **Peter** is the active buyer. He is **doing** the buying.
* **The table** is not buying anything! The table is sitting in the shop **being bought** by Peter.

In German, every noun wears a "hat" (its article: *der, die, das, ein, eine*) that shows what **job** that noun is doing in the sentence:
1. The person or thing **doing** the action is the **Subject**, and it wears the **Nominativ** hat (*der / ein*).
2. The person or thing that the action is **directly done to** is the **Direct Object**, and it puts on the **Accusative (*Akkusativ*)** hat (*den / einen*)!

---

### The 3-Step Formula to Find Nominativ vs. Akkusativ

Whenever you look at a sentence like *"Peter buys a table"* (*Peter kauft einen Tisch*), you never need to guess which word is **Nominativ** and which word is **Akkusativ**. Just follow this exact **3-Step Detective Formula** from our course notes:

```text
Sentence:   "Peter"          "kauft"          "einen Tisch."
            (Peter)          (buys)           (a table.)
               │                │                   │
            STEP 2           STEP 1              STEP 3
         Who buys? (Wer?)   The Verb!     What does he buy? (Was?)
               ▼                                    ▼
      SUBJECT (Nominativ)               DIRECT OBJECT (Akkusativ)
```

Let us walk through the 3 steps one by one:

* **Step 1: Find the Action Verb first!**
  Look at the sentence: *"Peter buys a table."* What is the action? **buys (*kauft*)**.
* **Step 2: Ask *"Who is doing the action?"* (*Wer?* or *Was?*)**
  Ask: *"Who is doing the buying?"*
  Answer: **Peter!**
  Therefore, **Peter** is the **Subject (*das Subjekt*)**, and the Subject is ALWAYS in the **Nominativ** case!
* **Step 3: Ask *"What (or whom) is he buying?"* (*Was?* for things / *Wen?* for people)**
  Ask: *"What is Peter buying?"*
  Answer: **A table (*einen Tisch*)!**
  Therefore, **a table** is the **Direct Object**, and the Direct Object is ALWAYS in the **Akkusativ** case!

Let us test this 3-Step Formula on four more everyday sentences:

| German Sentence | Step 1: The Verb | Step 2: Who does it? ➔ **Subject (Nominativ)** | Step 3: What / Whom? ➔ **Direct Object (Akkusativ)** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ***Der Mann trinkt einen Kaffee.*** *(The man drinks a coffee.)* | *trinkt* (drinks) | *Wer trinkt?* ➔ **Der Mann** (Nominativ) | *Was trinkt er?* ➔ **einen Kaffee** (Akkusativ) |
| ***Die Frau liest ein Buch.*** *(The woman reads a book.)* | *liest* (reads) | *Wer liest?* ➔ **Die Frau** (Nominativ) | *Was liest sie?* ➔ **ein Buch** (Akkusativ) |
| ***Das Kind isst eine Pizza.*** *(The child eats a pizza.)* | *isst* (eats) | *Wer isst?* ➔ **Das Kind** (Nominativ) | *Was isst es?* ➔ **eine Pizza** (Akkusativ) |
| ***Ich besuche meinen Bruder.*** *(I visit my brother.)* | *besuche* (visit) | *Wer besucht?* ➔ **Ich** (Nominativ) | *Wen besuche ich?* ➔ **meinen Bruder** (Akkusativ) |

### The Question Words for Nominativ vs. Akkusativ
Notice the question words we used in Step 2 and Step 3:
* To ask for the **Subject (Nominativ)**:
  * **Wer?** (Who? — for people): ***Wer** kauft den Tisch? — **Peter**!*
  * **Was?** (What? — for things): ***Was** ist kalt? — **Das Wasser** ist kalt!*
* To ask for the **Direct Object (Akkusativ)**:
  * **Wen?** (Whom? — for people): ***Wen** besuchst du? — Ich besuche **den Arzt** / **meinen Bruder**.* (Notice how **We-n** ends in **`-n`**, just like **de-n** and **eine-n**!)
  * **Was?** (What? — for things): ***Was** kaufst du? — Ich kaufe **den Tisch** / **einen Apfel**.*

### Important Reminder (Batch 02, Page 28): After the Verb `sein`, Always Use Nominativ!

Before we look at the article tables, there is one golden rule from your handwritten notebook (Batch 02, Page 28) that saves beginners from the #1 exam trap:

:::warning The `sein` Rule: `sein` = Equals Sign (`=`), Always Nominativ!
Look at the verb **`sein`** (*ich bin, du bist, er/sie/es **ist**, wir sind, ihr seid, sie/Sie **sind***, and past tense ***war / waren***).
When you say *"Das **ist** ein Tisch"* (That **is** a table), is anybody doing an action to the table? Is anybody buying, eating, kicking, or carrying the table?
**No!** You are just pointing at it and naming it (*Das = ein Tisch*).

Therefore, **after the verb `sein` (*ist, sind, war, waren*), NEVER use the Accusative!** Always keep the **Nominativ** (*der / ein / kein*):
* ✅ *Das **ist ein** Tisch.* (Nominativ — just naming it!)
* ➔ *Ich **kaufe einen** Tisch.* (Akkusativ — action done to the table!)
* ✅ ***Der** Wein **ist** gut.* (Nominativ — describing the wine!)
* ➔ *Ich **trinke einen** Wein.* (Akkusativ — drinking the wine!)
* ✅ *Das **ist kein** Problem.* (Nominativ!)
* ➔ *Ich **habe kein** Problem.* (Akkusativ!)
:::

## 2. The Great Relief: In Accusative, ONLY Masculine Changes! (`den / einen / keinen`)

Many students worry that learning the Accusative case means memorizing 12 brand-new articles.
Here is the best news in the entire German language:

**In the Accusative case, Feminine (`die`), Neutral (`das`), and Plural (`die`) nouns DO NOT CHANGE AT ALL! They stay 100% identical to the Nominativ!**

Only **Masculine (`der`)** nouns change in the Accusative case—and they all change with the exact same sound at the end: **`-en`**!
* **`der`** changes to **`den`**
* **`ein`** changes to **`einen`**
* **`kein`** changes to **`keinen`**

Let us look at the complete comparison tables from your notebook (Batch 02, Page 28 & Course Notes P.1251–1265):

### Table 1: Definite Articles ("the" — *Bestimmter Artikel*)

| Gender | Nominativ (Subject / after *sein*) | Akkusativ (Direct Object) | Did It Change? | Example in Akkusativ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Masculine** | **der** Tisch | **den** Tisch | **YES! (`der ➔ den`)** | *Ich kaufe **den** Tisch.* |
| **Feminine** | **die** Tasche | **die** Tasche | No (100% unchanged!) | *Ich kaufe **die** Tasche.* |
| **Neutral** | **das** Buch | **das** Buch | No (100% unchanged!) | *Ich kaufe **das** Buch.* |
| **Plural** | **die** Bücher | **die** Bücher | No (100% unchanged!) | *Ich kaufe **die** Bücher.* |

### Table 2: Indefinite Articles ("a / an" — *Unbestimmter Artikel*)

| Gender | Nominativ (Subject / after *sein*) | Akkusativ (Direct Object) | Did It Change? | Example in Akkusativ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Masculine** | **ein** Tisch | **einen** Tisch | **YES! (`ein ➔ einen`)** | *Peter kauft **einen** Tisch.* |
| **Feminine** | **eine** Tasche | **eine** Tasche | No (100% unchanged!) | *Peter kauft **eine** Tasche.* |
| **Neutral** | **ein** Buch | **ein** Buch | No (100% unchanged!) | *Peter kauft **ein** Buch.* |
| **Plural** | *(no article)* Bücher | *(no article)* Bücher | No (100% unchanged!) | *Peter kauft Bücher.* |

### Table 3: Negative Articles ("no / not a" — *Negativartikel*)

| Gender | Nominativ (Subject / after *sein*) | Akkusativ (Direct Object) | Did It Change? | Example in Akkusativ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Masculine** | **kein** Tisch | **keinen** Tisch | **YES! (`kein ➔ keinen`)** | *Ich brauche **keinen** Tisch.* |
| **Feminine** | **keine** Tasche | **keine** Tasche | No (100% unchanged!) | *Ich brauche **keine** Tasche.* |
| **Neutral** | **kein** Buch | **kein** Buch | No (100% unchanged!) | *Ich brauche **kein** Buch.* |
| **Plural** | **keine** Bücher | **keine** Bücher | No (100% unchanged!) | *Ich brauche **keine** Bücher.* |

:::tip The 3-Second Memory Trick: "Only Masculine Gets the `-en`!"
Look at all three tables above together:
* **der ➔ den**
* **ein ➔ einen**
* **kein ➔ keinen**
And later in Chapter 11, you will see that possessive articles do the exact same thing:
* **mein ➔ meinen**, **dein ➔ deinen**, **sein ➔ seinen**, **ihr ➔ ihren**, **unser ➔ unseren**, **Ihr ➔ Ihren**!
Every single masculine word in the Accusative simply puts on the **`-en`** ending! Feminine, Neutral, and Plural never change a single letter!
:::

### Walkthrough of the Examples from Your Notebook (Batch 02, Pages 28–29)

Let us walk through the exact dialogues and sentence patterns from your class notes (Batch 02, Pages 28–29) so you can see how `einen / eine / ein` and `keinen / keine / kein` work in real-life conversation:

#### Pattern 1: *Was essen Sie? / Was trinken Sie?* (Ordering & Eating)
When you eat (**essen**), drink (**trinken**), order (**bestellen**), or would like (**möchten**) food or drinks, the food or drink is the **Direct Object (Akkusativ)**!
So all you need to check is: **Is the food or drink masculine (`der`)?**
* If it is **`der`** ➔ use **`den / einen / keinen`**.
* If it is **`die`** ➔ use **`die / eine / keine`**.
* If it is **`das`** ➔ use **`das / ein / kein`**.

Look at your notebook examples:
1. <GermanAudio text="Was trinken Sie? — Ich trinke einen Wein." />
   * **Word-by-word:** *Was* (What) + *trinken* (drink) + *Sie* (you - Nominativ)? — *Ich* (I - Nominativ) + *trinke* (drink) + ***einen** Wein* (a wine - Akkusativ, because it is **der Wein**!).
   * Negation: *Ich trinke **keinen** Wein.* (I do not drink wine.)
2. <GermanAudio text="Ich trinke eine Limonade." />
   * **Why `eine`?** Because lemonade is feminine (**die Limonade**), and feminine stays **eine** in the Accusative!
   * Negation: *Ich trinke **keine** Limonade.*
3. <GermanAudio text="Ich trinke ein Wasser." />
   * **Why `ein`?** Because water is neutral (**das Wasser**), and neutral stays **ein** in the Accusative!
   * Negation: *Ich trinke **kein** Wasser.*
4. <GermanAudio text="Was essen Sie? — Ich esse einen Apfel und einen Salat." />
   * **Why `einen`?** Both **der Apfel** (apple) and **der Salat** (salad) are masculine (`der`), so both become **einen** (*einen Apfel, einen Salat*)!
5. <GermanAudio text="Ich esse eine Banane und eine Suppe." />
   * **Why `eine`?** Both **die Banane** and **die Suppe** are feminine (`die`), so they stay **eine**!
6. <GermanAudio text="Ich esse ein Brot und ein Ei." />
   * **Why `ein`?** Both **das Brot** (bread) and **das Ei** (egg) are neutral (`das`), so they stay **ein**!

#### Pattern 2: *Haben Sie Geschwister?* (Using `haben` + Accusative)
The verb **`haben`** (to have) is one of the most frequent Accusative verbs in German! Whatever you **have** (a brother, a sister, a car, a dog, time, hunger) is in the **Accusative case**:
* <GermanAudio text="Haben Sie Geschwister? — Ja, ich habe einen Bruder." />
  * *(Do you have siblings? — Yes, I have a brother.)*
  * **Why `einen Bruder`?** Because brother is masculine (**der Bruder**)! After *ich habe*, *ein Bruder* MUST become **einen Bruder**!
* <GermanAudio text="Ja, ich habe eine Schwester." />
  * *(Yes, I have a sister.)* — **die Schwester** (feminine) stays **eine Schwester**.
* <GermanAudio text="Nein, ich habe keinen Bruder und keine Schwester. Ich bin Einzelkind." />
  * *(No, I have no brother and no sister. I am an only child.)* — **keinen Bruder** (masculine) vs. **keine Schwester** (feminine).

Both verbs take the **Accusative case**!
:::

### Special Spotlight: `es gibt` + Accusative ("There is / There are")

One of the most useful expressions in all of A1 German is **`es gibt`** (*there is* / *there are*).
Why does **`es gibt`** always take the **Accusative case**?
Because literally, *es gibt* means *"it gives..."*! Since *"es"* (it) is the grammatical subject, the thing that *"it gives"* is the **Direct Object (Akkusativ)**!

Look at how **`es gibt`** works with masculine, feminine, neutral, and plural nouns:
* **Masculine (`der` ➔ `einen / keinen`):**
  * <GermanAudio text="In meiner Straße gibt es einen Supermarkt und einen Park." />
    *(In my street there is a supermarket and a park.)* — **der Supermarkt ➔ einen Supermarkt**, **der Park ➔ einen Park**!
  * <GermanAudio text="Gibt es hier einen Bahnhof? — Nein, hier gibt es keinen Bahnhof." />
    *(Is there a train station here? — No, there is no train station here.)*
* **Feminine (`die` ➔ `eine / keine`):**
  * *Gibt es hier **eine** Bank / **eine** Apotheke? — Ja, es gibt **eine** Bank.*
* **Neutral (`das` ➔ `ein / kein`):**
  * *In der Stadt gibt es **ein** Kino, **ein** Krankenhaus und **ein** Museum.*
* **Plural (`die` ➔ `viele / keine`):**
  * *Hier gibt es **viele** Restaurants, aber **keine** Hotels.*

## 3. Personal Pronouns in the Accusative (`mich, dich, ihn, sie, es, uns, euch, sie, Sie`)

### Why Do We Need Accusative Pronouns?

In English, when a person moves from the **Subject** position to the **Direct Object** position, the pronoun changes:
* You say: *"I see **him**"* (NOT *"I see **he**"*!).
* You say: *"He loves **me**"* (NOT *"He loves **I**"*!).
* You say: *"She calls **us**"* (NOT *"She calls **we**"*!).

German works the exact same way! When a personal pronoun (*ich, du, er, sie, es, wir, ihr, sie, Sie*) is the **Direct Object** of a verb (or comes after an Accusative preposition like *für* or *ohne*), it changes into its **Accusative Pronoun** form.

Even better: In German, we also use **`ihn` (him/it)**, **`sie` (her/it)**, and **`es` (it)** to replace **objects and things** according to their grammatical gender (`der`, `die`, `das`)!
* A masculine object (**der / den Tisch**) is replaced by **`ihn`**!
* A feminine object (**die Tasche**) is replaced by **`sie`**!
* A neutral object (**das Buch**) is replaced by **`es`**!

| Person / Gender | Nominativ (Subject: *Who does it?*) | Akkusativ (Direct Object: *Whom / What?*) | Did It Change? | Everyday Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1st Singular** (I ➔ me) | **ich** | **mich** | **YES!** (`ich ➔ mich`) | *Verstehst du **mich**?* (Do you understand me?) |
| **2nd Singular** (you ➔ you) | **du** | **dich** | **YES!** (`du ➔ dich`) | *Ich liebe **dich**!* (I love you!) |
| **3rd Sing. Masc.** (he / *der* ➔ him / it) | **er** *(der)* | **ihn** *(den)* | **YES!** (`er ➔ ihn`) | *Kennst du Peter? — Ja, ich kenne **ihn**.* |
| **3rd Sing. Fem.** (she / *die* ➔ her / it) | **sie** *(die)* | **sie** *(die)* | No! (stays **sie**) | *Wo ist die Tasche? — Ich habe **sie**.* |
| **3rd Sing. Neut.** (it / *das* ➔ it) | **es** *(das)* | **es** *(das)* | No! (stays **es**) | *Das Buch ist gut. Ich kaufe **es**.* |
| **1st Plural** (we ➔ us) | **wir** | **uns** | **YES!** (`wir ➔ uns`) | *Besuchst du **uns** am Sonntag?* (Are you visiting us on Sunday?) |
| **2nd Plural** (you all ➔ you all) | **ihr** | **euch** | **YES!** (`ihr ➔ euch`) | *Ich lade **euch** zur Party ein!* (I invite you all to the party!) |

Look at how closely the 3rd-person Accusative pronouns match the Accusative articles **`den / die / das`**:
* Masculine: **de`n`** Tisch ➔ **ih`n`** (Both end in **`-n`**!)
* Feminine: **d`ie`** Tasche ➔ **s`ie`** (Both stay **`-ie`**!)
* Neutral: **da`s`** Buch ➔ **e`s`** (Both end in **`-s`**!)
* Plural: **d`ie`** Bücher ➔ **s`ie`** (Both stay **`-ie`**!)
And look at the 4 pronouns that never change at all between Nominativ and Akkusativ: **`sie`** (she/her), **`es`** (it), **`sie`** (they/them), and **`Sie`** (You formal)!
:::

### Walkthrough of the Exact Examples from Your Notebook (Batch 03, Page 32)

Let us practice the two major notebook drills from Batch 03, Page 32, where we use **`finden`** (to think of / have an opinion about) and **`kaufen`** (to buy) with Accusative pronouns:

#### Drill A: *Wie finden Sie ...?* ("What do you think of ...?")
When someone asks *"Wie finden Sie ...?"* (Literally: *"How do you find ...?"* = *"What do you think of ...?"*), the person or thing you are judging is the **Direct Object (Akkusativ)**!
Let us look at the exact sentences from Batch 03, Page 32:

1. **Talking about a male person (`er ➔ ihn`):**
   * <GermanAudio text="Wie finden Sie Sulf? — Ich finde ihn freundlich." />
   * *Question:* **Wie finden Sie Sulf?** (What do you think of Sulf?)
   * *Answer:* **Ich finde ihn freundlich.** (I find him friendly / I think he is friendly.)
   * **Why `ihn`?** Sulf is a man (*er*). In the answer, *"Ich"* is the Subject (Nominativ), and Sulf is the Direct Object of *finde*, so **er** changes to **ihn**!

2. **Talking about a feminine thing (`die ➔ sie`):**
   * <GermanAudio text="Wie finden Sie die Klimaanlage? — Ich finde sie kalt." />
   * *Question:* **Wie finden Sie die Klimaanlage?** (What do you think of the air conditioner?)
   * *Answer:* **Ich finde sie kalt.** (I find it cold.)
   * **Why `sie`?** In English we call an air conditioner *"it"*, but in German **die Klimaanlage** is feminine (`die`)! So in the Accusative, **die Klimaanlage** becomes **sie**!

3. **Talking about a neutral thing (`das ➔ es`):**
   * <GermanAudio text="Wie finden Sie das Zentrum? — Ich finde es sauber." />
   * *Question:* **Wie finden Sie das Zentrum?** (What do you think of the city center?)
   * *Answer:* **Ich finde es sauber.** (I find it clean.)
   * **Why `es`?** Because **das Zentrum** is neutral (`das`), so we replace it with **es**!

4. **Talking about a masculine thing (`der / den ➔ ihn`):**
   * <GermanAudio text="Wie finden Sie den Film? — Ich finde ihn sehr spannend!" />
   * *Question:* **Wie finden Sie den Film?** (What do you think of the movie?)
   * *Answer:* **Ich finde ihn sehr spannend!** (I find it very exciting!)
   * **Why `ihn`?** Because **der Film** is masculine (`der` ➔ Accusative `den Film`), so "it" is **ihn**!

#### Drill B: Replacing Shopping Objects (`den Tisch ➔ ihn`, `die Tasche ➔ sie`, `das Buch ➔ es`)
Look at the second set of examples from Batch 03, Page 32:
* **den Tisch** (*der Tisch* — masculine):
  * *Der Tisch ist schön. Ich kaufe **den Tisch**.* ➔ <GermanAudio text="Ich kaufe ihn." /> *(I buy it.)*
* **die Tasche** (*die Tasche* — feminine):
  * *Die Tasche ist günstig. Ich kaufe **die Tasche**.* ➔ <GermanAudio text="Ich kaufe sie." /> *(I buy it.)*
* **das Buch** (*das Buch* — neutral):
  * *Das Buch ist interessant. Ich kaufe **das Buch**.* ➔ <GermanAudio text="Ich kaufe es." /> *(I buy it.)*
* **die Schuhe** (*die Schuhe* — plural):
  * *Die Schuhe sind bequem. Ich kaufe **die Schuhe**.* ➔ <GermanAudio text="Ich kaufe sie." /> *(I buy them.)*

## 4. Accusative Prepositions — The "GODFU" Shortcut! (`gegen, ohne, durch, für, um`)

### What is an Accusative Preposition?

So far, we saw that **verbs** like *kaufen, haben, brauchen, trinken* put the noun after them into the Accusative case.
Now here is the second trigger for the Accusative case: **Prepositions** (small connecting words like *for, without, through, against, around*).

In your class notebook (Batch 03, Page 38), your teacher taught the famous **"GODFU" Shortcut** (also sometimes remembered as **FUDGO**).
Whenever one of these **5 prepositions** comes before a noun or a pronoun, it functions like a magnet: **the noun or pronoun immediately after it MUST be in the Accusative case (`den / einen / keinen` or `mich / dich / ihn / uns / euch`) — 100% of the time, with zero exceptions!**

```text
   G  ➔  gegen   (against / around an approximate clock time)
   O  ➔  ohne    (without)
   D  ➔  durch   (through)
   F  ➔  für     (for)
   U  ➔  um      (around a place / at an exact clock time)
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   ALL 5 ALWAYS TRIGGER THE ACCUSATIVE CASE!
```

Let us examine each of the 5 **GODFU** prepositions in detail using every single example from your notebook (Batch 03, Page 38 & Course Notes P.1290–1312):

---

### 1. `G` = `gegen` (against / into / around a clock time)

**`gegen`** has three common everyday uses at A1:
1. **In sports or competitions ("against / versus"):**
   * <GermanAudio text="Am Wochenende spielt Indien gegen Pakistan." />
     *(On the weekend, India plays against Pakistan.)*
   * *Heute Abend spielt Deutschland **gegen** Spanien.* *(Tonight Germany plays against Spain.)*
2. **Opposing someone/something or bumping into something ("against / into"):**
   * <GermanAudio text="Am Wochenende haben die Menschen gegen den Politiker demonstriert." />
     *(On the weekend, the people demonstrated against the politician.)* — Notice: **der Politiker** ➔ **gegen den Politiker** (Accusative)!
   * *Das Auto fährt **gegen einen** Baum.* *(The car drives into/against a tree — **der Baum ➔ gegen einen Baum**.)*
   * *Hast du ein Medikament **gegen den** Husten?* *(Do you have a medicine against the cough — **der Husten ➔ gegen den Husten**?)*
3. **Approximate time ("around / approximately at"):**
   * *Ich komme **gegen** 8 Uhr nach Hause.* *(I am coming home around 8 o'clock.)*

---

### 2. `O` = `ohne` (without)

**`ohne`** means **"without"** and is always followed by the Accusative case:
* <GermanAudio text="Meine Großmutter kann ohne ihre Brille nicht sehen." />
  *(My grandmother cannot see without her glasses.)* — **die Brille ➔ ohne ihre Brille**.
* <GermanAudio text="Peter geht ohne seine Frau ins Theater." />
  *(Peter goes to the theater without his wife.)* — **die Frau ➔ ohne seine Frau**.
* <GermanAudio text="Am Abend gehen wir ohne die Kinder ins Kino." />
  *(In the evening we go to the cinema without the children.)* — **die Kinder ➔ ohne die Kinder**.
* <GermanAudio text="Ich trinke meinen Kaffee ohne Zucker und ohne Milch." />
  *(I drink my coffee without sugar and without milk.)*
* *Ich gehe nicht **ohne meinen** Regenschirm (*der Regenschirm*) und nicht **ohne dich** (*du ➔ dich*)!*
  *(I am not going without my umbrella and not without you!)*

---

### 3. `D` = `durch` (through)

**`durch`** means **"through"** (walking, driving, running, or looking through a space):
* <GermanAudio text="Es läuft über 200 Kilometer durch die Wüste." />
  *(It runs over 200 kilometers through the desert.)* — **die Wüste ➔ durch die Wüste**.
* <GermanAudio text="Man kann durch die Stadt fahren." />
  *(One can drive through the city.)* — **die Stadt ➔ durch die Stadt**.
* <GermanAudio text="Manchmal gehen wir durch den Wald spazieren." />
  *(Sometimes we go for a walk through the forest.)* — Notice: **der Wald** (the forest, masculine) ➔ **durch den Wald** (Accusative)!
* *Wir machen einen Spaziergang **durch den** Park.* *(We take a walk through the park — **der Park ➔ durch den Park**.)*

---

### 4. `F` = `für` (for)

**`für`** means **"for"** and is the single most common Accusative preposition in everyday German and on the A1 exam:
* <GermanAudio text="Für wen ist das teure Geschenk?" />
  *(For whom is the expensive gift?)* — Notice the question word: **Für wen?** (*für* + *wen* in the Accusative!).
* <GermanAudio text="Die Mutter hat viel Zeit für ihre Kinder." />
  *(The mother has a lot of time for her children.)* — **die Kinder ➔ für ihre Kinder**.
* <GermanAudio text="Ich kaufe Blumen für meine Mutter und ein Buch für meinen Vater." />
  *(I buy flowers for my mother and a book for my father.)* — Notice: **für meine Mutter** (feminine) vs. **für meinen Vater** (masculine **der Vater ➔ für meinen Vater**)!
* *Hier ist ein Brief **für den** Chef (*der Chef*) / **für dich** (*du ➔ dich*).*

---

### 5. `U` = `um` (around a place / at an exact clock time)

**`um`** has two very important meanings in A1 German:
1. **Place ("around / right around"):**
   * <GermanAudio text="Sie finden einen Supermarkt gleich hier um die Ecke." />
     *(You will find a supermarket right here around the corner.)* — **die Ecke ➔ um die Ecke**.
   * <GermanAudio text="Sie finden eine Bank gleich hier um die Ecke." />
     *(You will find a bank right here around the corner.)*
   * <GermanAudio text="Die Katze rennt um das Haus herum." />
     *(The cat runs around the house.)* — **das Haus ➔ um das Haus (herum)**.
   * *Wir sitzen **um den** Tisch.* *(We are sitting around the table — **der Tisch ➔ um den Tisch**.)*
2. **Exact Clock Time ("at ..."):**
   * *Der Unterricht beginnt **um** 9 Uhr.* *(The class starts at 9 o'clock.)*

### The Secret to Masculine and Feminine Professions in German

In your course notes (Pages 1313–1344) and handwritten notebook (Batch 02, Pages 16–17), we learn how to talk about professions (**die Berufe**, singular: **der Beruf**).

Before we read the master table of 40+ professions, look at the **simple rule** that turns almost any masculine job into its feminine equivalent:

### How to Talk About Your Job (Step-by-Step Formulas!)

When someone asks you about your profession in German, there are two standard questions:
* **Formal (`Sie`):** <GermanAudio text="Was sind Sie von Beruf? / Was machen Sie beruflich?" /> *(What is your profession? / What do you do professionally?)*

And here are the **3 golden ways to answer** (Notice Rule #1: **No article `ein/eine`** right after `Ich bin` or `Ich arbeite als`!):

1. **Formula 1: `Ich bin` + Profession (NO `ein/eine`!)**
   * ✅ <GermanAudio text="Ich bin Arzt von Beruf." /> *(I am a doctor by profession — NOT ~~Ich bin ein Arzt~~!)*
   * ✅ <GermanAudio text="Meine Frau ist Lehrerin." /> *(My wife is a teacher — NOT ~~eine Lehrerin~~!)*
   * ✅ *Ich bin Student / Studentin.* *(I am a university student.)*
2. **Formula 2: `Ich arbeite als` + Profession + `bei` + Company Name**
   * ✅ <GermanAudio text="Ich arbeite als Ingenieur bei Bosch." /> *(I work as an engineer at Bosch.)*
   * ✅ <GermanAudio text="Sie arbeitet als Verkäuferin in einem Supermarkt." /> *(She works as a salesperson in a supermarket.)*
3. **Formula 3: Describing Your Job Duties (`Tätigkeit`), Salary (`Gehalt`), and Pros/Cons (`Vorteil / Nachteil`)**
   * *Ich habe **einen** guten Job. Ich verdiene gutes Geld, und das Gehalt ist gut.*
   * *Ein Vorteil ist: Ich habe nette Kollegen. Ein Nachteil ist: Ich habe manchmal Nachtschicht.*

:::warning Classic Beginner Trap: `der Urlaub` vs. `die Ferien`!

Never tell your boss *"Ich möchte Ferien nehmen"* — always say ***„Ich möchte Urlaub nehmen / beantragen.“***
:::

#### 3. Everyday Office Small Talk & The Lunchtime Greeting `"Mahlzeit!"`
* **Politely asking a colleague for a moment or help:**
  * *„**Hast du kurz Zeit?** / **Haben Sie kurz Zeit, Herr Meyer?**“* (Do you have a moment?)
  * *„**Ich habe eine kurze Frage.** Kannst du mir kurz helfen?“* (I have a quick question. Can you help me for a second?)
  * *Replying:* *„Ja, gerne! Worum geht es?“* (Yes, gladly! What is it about?) or *„Im Moment leider nicht, ich bin in einem Meeting (**in einer Besprechung**). Geht es in 20 Minuten?“*
* **Office equipment hiccups:**
  * *„Mein **Laptop / Computer** startet nicht.“* · *„Mein **Bildschirm** (monitor) ist schwarz.“*
  * *„**Der Drucker** (printer) funktioniert nicht / hat kein Papier. Ich muss **das Dokument** ausdrucken und **die E-Mail** schicken.“*
* **The German lunchtime institution — `„Mahlzeit!“`:**
  Around 12:00–13:00 in German offices and factories, colleagues passing you in the hallway or kitchen will nod and say **„Mahlzeit!“** (literally *"Mealtime!"* = *"Hello, enjoy your lunch break!"*). Simply smile and reply **„Mahlzeit!“** right back!
  * *Inviting a coworker:* *„Kommst du mit **in die Kantine** (company cafeteria)? Gehen wir zusammen Mittagessen?“*
* **At the end of the workday:**
  When leaving the office, wish your colleagues **„Schönen Feierabend!“** (*Have a nice evening after work!*), and on Fridays **„Schönes Wochenende!“**

#### 4. Calling in Sick to Work (`Die Krankmeldung & die AU`)
If you wake up sick on a workday in Germany, follow these two steps:
1. **Message or call your employer early (before your work hours begin):**
   * <GermanAudio text="Guten Morgen, ich bin heute leider krank und kann nicht zur Arbeit kommen." />
     *„**Guten Morgen, ich bin heute leider krank und kann nicht zur Arbeit kommen.** Ich gehe heute zum Arzt.“*
2. **Get the Medical Sick Note (`die Krankschreibung / die Arbeitsunfähigkeitsbescheinigung / AU`):**
   * By law, if you are sick for more than three calendar days (or from Day 1 if your *Arbeitsvertrag* requires it), you need a doctor's sick note: **die Arbeitsunfähigkeitsbescheinigung** (short: **die AU** or **die Krankschreibung**, today sent digitally as the **eAU** from the doctor's practice to your health insurance so your employer can retrieve it).

### Mini-Dialogue: Putting Everything Together! (Accusative Articles, Pronouns, GODFU & Professions)

Now that you have learned every word and rule in this chapter, read this friendly conversation between two friends, **Lukas** and **Priya**, meeting in the city center:

> **Lukas:** <GermanAudio text="Hallo Priya! Wen suchst du hier in der Stadt?" />
> *(Hello Priya! Whom are you looking for here in the city?)*
>
> **Priya:** <GermanAudio text="Hallo Lukas! Ich treffe meinen Bruder Rohan. Kennst du ihn?" />
> *(Hello Lukas! I am meeting my brother Rohan. Do you know him?)*
>
> **Lukas:** <GermanAudio text="Nein, ich kenne ihn noch nicht. Was ist er von Beruf?" />
> *(No, I don't know him yet. What is his profession?)*
>
> **Priya:** <GermanAudio text="Er ist Arzt von Beruf. Er arbeitet im Krankenhaus und hat oft Nachtdienst. Aber heute hat er frei!" />
> *(He is a doctor by profession. He works in the hospital and often has night duty. But today he is off!)*
>
> **Lukas:** <GermanAudio text="Super! Und was hast du da? Hast du eine neue Tasche?" />
> *(Great! And what do you have there? Do you have a new bag?)*
>
> **Priya:** <GermanAudio text="Nein, das ist ein Geschenk für ihn! Er hat morgen Geburtstag. Ich habe einen Pullover und eine Uhr für ihn gekauft." />
> *(No, that is a gift for him! He has his birthday tomorrow. I bought a sweater and a watch for him.)*
>
> **Lukas:** <GermanAudio text="Wie schön! Geht ihr jetzt durch den Park?" />
> *(How nice! Are you guys walking through the park now?)*
>
> **Priya:** <GermanAudio text="Ja, wir gehen durch den Park, und gleich da um die Ecke gibt es ein nettes Café. Dort trinken wir einen Kaffee — natürlich ohne Zucker! Kommst du mit? Wir laden dich ein!" />
> *(Yes, we are walking through the park, and right there around the corner there is a nice café. There we will drink a coffee — without sugar, of course! Are you coming along? We invite you!)*

Look at how naturally you just understood every single Accusative structure in that dialogue:
* **Wen** suchst du? (Asking for a person in the Accusative!)
* **meinen Bruder** (*der Bruder* ➔ Accusative after *treffe*)
* Kennst du **ihn**? (*er ➔ ihn* after *kennst*)
* Was ist er **von Beruf**? — Er ist **Arzt** (no article!)
* **für ihn** (preposition *für* + *ihn*)
* **einen Pullover** (*der Pullover* ➔ Accusative after *gekauft*)
* **durch den Park** (preposition *durch* + *der Park ➔ den Park*)
* **um die Ecke** (preposition *um* + *die Ecke*)
* **es gibt ein nettes Café** (*es gibt* + Accusative)
* **einen Kaffee** (*der Kaffee* ➔ Accusative after *trinken*)
* **ohne Zucker** (preposition *ohne*)
* Wir laden **dich** ein (*du ➔ dich* after *einladen*)!

### Common Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right | Why? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *Peter kauft **ein** Tisch.* | *Peter kauft **einen** Tisch.* | *der Tisch* is masculine! As the direct object of *kauft*, *ein* must become **einen**. |
| *Das ist **einen** Tisch.* | *Das ist **ein** Tisch.* | After **sein** (*ist, sind*), we are only naming the subject—always use **Nominativ** (*ein*)! |
| *Hier gibt es **ein** Supermarkt.* | *Hier gibt es **einen** Supermarkt.* | **es gibt** always triggers the Accusative (*der Supermarkt ➔ einen Supermarkt*). |
| *Wie finden Sie den Film? — Ich finde **er** gut.* | *Wie finden Sie den Film? — Ich finde **ihn** gut.* | *der Film* is the direct object of *finde*, so masculine *er* changes to **ihn**! |
| *Wie finden Sie die Tasche? — Ich kaufe **es**.* | *Wie finden Sie die Tasche? — Ich kaufe **sie**.* | *die Tasche* is feminine (`die`), so "it" in German is **sie** (not *es*)! |
| *Wir gehen durch **der** Wald.* | *Wir gehen durch **den** Wald.* | **durch** is a GODFU preposition (+ Accusative), so *der Wald* becomes **den Wald**. |
| *Ist der Brief für **du**?* | *Ist der Brief für **dich**?* | **für** is a GODFU preposition (+ Accusative), so *du* becomes **dich**. |
| *Ich bin **einen** Arzt von Beruf.* | *Ich bin **Arzt** von Beruf.* | When stating your profession after *sein* (or *arbeiten als*), drop the article *ein/eine* (and *sein* never takes Accusative anyway!). |
