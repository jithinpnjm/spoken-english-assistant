## 1. Basic Comparison in German — The 3 Levels (Positiv, Komparativ, Superlativ)

### The Mental Model: How Comparison Works in Plain English

Imagine you are standing in a German bakery looking at three cakes, or in a supermarket looking at three bags of apples.
In English, when we compare things, we use **three levels**:
1. **Level 1 (Basic form):** *"This cake is **good**."* / *"This bag is **cheap**."*
2. **Level 2 (Comparing two things):** *"The strawberry cake is **better** than the cheesecake."* / *"This bag is **cheaper** than that bag."*
3. **Level 3 (The #1 winner in a group):** *"The chocolate cake is **the best**!"* / *"This bag is **the cheapest**!"*

German works in the **exact same three levels**! In German grammar books, these three steps are called:
* **Step 1: Positiv** (the normal, basic word: *billig* = cheap, *schnell* = fast, *gut* = good)
* **Step 2: Komparativ** (the "more / -er" form used to compare two things: *billiger* = cheaper, *schneller* = faster, *besser* = better)
* **Step 3: Superlativ** (the "most / -est" form for the #1 top level: *am billigsten* = the cheapest, *am schnellsten* = the fastest, *am besten* = the best)

Let's learn the simple formula for regular adjectives first, and then master the 3 superstar irregular words that appear in almost every A1 exam!

---

### Step-by-Step Formula 1: Regular Adjectives (`-er` and `am ...-sten`)

For regular adjectives in German, building the 3 levels is just like English:
* **Level 1 (Positiv):** Take the normal adjective (*billig, schnell, klein, schön, langsam*).
* **Level 2 (Komparativ):** Add **`-er`** to the end of the adjective!
  * *billig* (cheap) ➔ **billiger** (cheaper)
  * *schnell* (fast) ➔ **schneller** (faster)
  * *klein* (small) ➔ **kleiner** (smaller)
  * *schön* (beautiful) ➔ **schöner** (more beautiful)
* **Level 3 (Superlativ):** Put the little word **`am`** in front and add **`-sten`** (or **`-esten`** if the word ends in *-t, -d, -s, -ß, -z*) to the end!
  * *billig* ➔ *billiger* ➔ **am billigsten** (the cheapest)
  * *schnell* ➔ *schneller* ➔ **am schnellsten** (the fastest)
  * *klein* ➔ *kleiner* ➔ **am kleinsten** (the smallest)
  * *schön* ➔ *schöner* ➔ **am schönsten** (the most beautiful)

:::info Why does German add NO "more" before long adjectives?
In English, we say *cheap ➔ cheaper*, but for longer words we say *beautiful ➔ **more** beautiful* or *interesting ➔ **more** interesting*.
In German, you **NEVER** put *mehr* in front of an adjective! Even long adjectives simply take **`-er`** and **`am ...-sten`**:
* *interessant* (interesting) ➔ **interessanter** (more interesting — NEVER ~~*mehr interessant*~~!) ➔ **am interessantesten** (the most interesting).
* *wichtig* (important) ➔ **wichtiger** (more important) ➔ **am wichtigsten** (the most important).
:::

### Step-by-Step Formula 2: The 3 Most Important Irregular Comparisons in A1 (Memorize These!)

Just like English does not say *"good ➔ gooder ➔ goodest"* (we say *good ➔ better ➔ best*), German has **three superstar irregular comparison words** that change completely from Level 1 to Level 2 and Level 3.
You will use these three sets in **almost every speaking and writing exam** at the A1 level!

| # | Level 1: Positiv | Level 2: Komparativ | Level 3: Superlativ | English Meaning & When to Use It |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **gut** | **besser** | **am besten** | **good ➔ better ➔ the best** *(Describing quality, taste, or how well someone does something)* |
| **2** | **gern** | **lieber** | **am liebsten** | **gladly (like doing) ➔ prefer doing ➔ like doing the most** *(Talking about your favorite foods, drinks, hobbies, or activities with ANY verb!)* |
| **3** | **viel** | **mehr** | **am meisten** | **much / a lot ➔ more ➔ the most** *(Talking about quantities, money, time, or how much of something you eat/do)* |

<GermanAudio text="gut, besser, am besten. gern, lieber, am liebsten. viel, mehr, am meisten." label="Listen to the 3 Superstar Irregular Comparisons" />

Let's look closely at all three of these with the exact examples from your course notes and handwritten notebook!

#### 1. `gut` ➔ `besser` ➔ `am besten` (Good ➔ Better ➔ The Best)
Look at how we compare three cakes (*der Käsekuchen* = cheesecake, *der Erdbeerkuchen* = strawberry cake, *der Schokokuchen* = chocolate cake):
* **Level 1 (Positiv):** <GermanAudio text="Käsekuchen schmeckt gut." /> **`Käsekuchen schmeckt gut.`** *(Cheesecake tastes good.)*
* **Level 2 (Komparativ):** <GermanAudio text="Erdbeerkuchen schmeckt besser als Käsekuchen." /> **`Erdbeerkuchen schmeckt besser als Käsekuchen.`** *(Strawberry cake tastes better than cheesecake.)*
* **Level 3 (Superlativ):** <GermanAudio text="Schokokuchen schmeckt am besten." /> **`Schokokuchen schmeckt am besten.`** *(Chocolate cake tastes the best!)*

#### 2. `gern` ➔ `lieber` ➔ `am liebsten` (Like ➔ Prefer ➔ Like the Most)
In German, you don't need three different verbs for *"I like eating"*, *"I prefer eating"*, and *"My favorite thing to eat is"*. Instead, you keep the normal verb (**`ich esse`** or **`ich trinke`** or **`ich spiele`**) and just upgrade the little helper word from **`gern`** to **`lieber`** to **`am liebsten`**!
* **Level 1 (`gern` = gladly / like to):**
  * <GermanAudio text="Ich esse gern Salat." /> **`Ich esse gern Salat.`** *(I like eating salad / I gladly eat salad.)*
  * <GermanAudio text="Ich trinke gern Tee." /> **`Ich trinke gern Tee.`** *(I like drinking tea.)*
* **Level 2 (`lieber` = prefer to):**
  * <GermanAudio text="Ich esse lieber Burger als Salat." /> **`Ich esse lieber Burger als Salat.`** *(I prefer eating burgers to salad.)*
  * <GermanAudio text="Ich trinke lieber Kaffee." /> **`Ich trinke lieber Kaffee.`** *(I prefer drinking coffee.)*
* **Level 3 (`am liebsten` = like the most / favorite):**
  * <GermanAudio text="Ich esse Pizza am liebsten." /> **`Ich esse Pizza am liebsten.`** (or: **`Am liebsten esse ich Pizza.`**) *(I like eating pizza the most / Pizza is my favorite thing to eat!)*
  * <GermanAudio text="Am liebsten trinke ich Wasser." /> **`Am liebsten trinke ich Wasser.`** *(Most of all, I like drinking water.)*

#### 3. `viel` ➔ `mehr` ➔ `am meisten` (A Lot ➔ More ➔ The Most)
Use **`viel ➔ mehr ➔ am meisten`** when talking about amounts, quantities, or which thing you eat/use the most:
* **Level 1 (`viel` = a lot / much):**
  * <GermanAudio text="Thomas hat viel Geld." /> **`Thomas hat viel Geld.`** *(Thomas has a lot of money.)*
* **Level 2 (`mehr` = more):**
  * <GermanAudio text="Frank hat mehr Geld als Thomas." /> **`Frank hat mehr Geld als Thomas.`** *(Frank has more money than Thomas.)*
* **Level 3 (`am meisten` = the most):**
  * <GermanAudio text="Felix hat am meisten Geld." /> **`Felix hat am meisten Geld.`** *(Felix has the most money.)*
* **From Your Notebook (Page 108):**
  * **Question:** <GermanAudio text="Welches Obst essen Sie am meisten?" /> **`Welches Obst essen Sie am meisten?`** *(Which fruit do You eat the most?)*
  * **Answer:** <GermanAudio text="Ich esse Äpfel am meisten." /> **`Ich esse Äpfel am meisten.`** *(I eat apples the most!)*

### Step-by-Step Formula 3: Comparing Two Things Directly (`so … wie` vs. `-er als`)

When you put two people or two things side by side, there are only two possibilities: either they are **equal** (`=`), or one is **more/bigger/better** than the other (`>`).

1. **When Two Things Are EQUAL (`=`): Use `so + [Basic Adjective] + wie` (*as … as*)**
   * **Formula:** `so` + **Positiv (Level 1)** + `wie`
   * <GermanAudio text="Der Tee ist so teuer wie der Kaffee." /> **`Der Tee ist so teuer wie der Kaffee.`** *(The tea is as expensive as the coffee — both cost 2 Euros!)*
   * <GermanAudio text="Anna ist genauso groß wie Maria." /> **`Anna ist (genau)so groß wie Maria.`** *(Anna is just as tall as Maria.)*
2. **When Two Things Are DIFFERENT (`>`): Use `[Comparative -er] + als` (*more/-er … than*)**
   * **Formula:** **Komparativ (Level 2)** + `als`
   * <GermanAudio text="Der Kaffee ist teurer als der Tee." /> **`Der Kaffee ist teurer als der Tee.`** *(The coffee is more expensive than the tea.)*
   * <GermanAudio text="Paul ist größer als Thomas." /> **`Paul ist größer als Thomas.`** *(Paul is taller than Thomas.)*
   * <GermanAudio text="Obst ist gesünder als Schokolade." /> **`Obst ist gesünder als Schokolade.`** *(Fruit is healthier than chocolate.)*

### Common Beginner Mistakes with Comparisons
| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right | Why? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *Der Apfel ist **guter** als die Banane.* | *Der Apfel ist **besser** als die Banane.* | *gut* is irregular: **gut ➔ besser ➔ am besten**. |
| *Ich esse **mehr gern** Pizza.* | *Ich esse **lieber** Pizza.* | *gern* is irregular: **gern ➔ lieber ➔ am liebsten**. |
| *Berlin ist **größer wie** Weimar.* | *Berlin ist **größer als** Weimar.* | After a comparative (`-er`), always use **`als`** (*than*), never *wie*! Use *wie* only for equals (*so groß wie*). |
| *Das Buch ist **mehr interessant**.* | *Das Buch ist **interessanter**.* | German never uses *mehr* to build a comparative adjective; always add **`-er`** to the word! |

## 2. Demonstrative & Question Pronouns (`dieser, diese, dieses` & `welcher, welche, welches`)

### The Mental Model: Pointing at Things ("This One!") & Asking ("Which One?")

Imagine you are at a clothing shop or a bakery in Germany. There are five sweaters on the table or five loaves of bread on the shelf.
* First, the seller asks you: **"Which sweater would you like?"** or **"Which bread are you buying?"**
  In German, the question word for **"which?"** is **`welcher / welche / welches`**.
* Next, you point with your finger at a specific sweater or bread and say: **"This sweater!"** or **"I am buying this bread!"**
  In German, the pointing word for **"this / these"** is **`dieser / diese / dieses`** (called a *Demonstrative Pronoun* because it demonstrates or points out a specific thing!).

Compare the difference between a general article (`der, die, das`) and pointing with `dieser, diese, dieses` (from `Complete-A1-Course-Notes.pdf` Pages 1589–1591):
* **General (`der / die / das`):**
  * *Der Tisch ist groß.* *(The table is big.)*
  * *Die Frau ist jung.* *(The woman is young.)*
  * *Das Auto ist schön.* *(The car is beautiful.)*
* **Pointing & Emphasizing (`dieser / diese / dieses` = THIS one right here!):**
  * <GermanAudio text="Dieser Tisch ist groß. Diese Frau ist jung. Dieses Auto ist schön." />
  * **`Dieser Tisch ist groß.`** *(**This** table is big!)*
  * **`Diese Frau ist jung.`** *(**This** woman is young!)*
  * **`Dieses Auto ist schön.`** *(**This** car is beautiful!)*

---

### Step-by-Step Formula: The Endings Copy `der, die, das` 100%!

Here is the easiest trick in German grammar:
You do **NOT** need to memorize a new table for `dieser` (*this*) or `welcher` (*which*)!
Why? Because their endings are **100% identical to the endings of `der, die, das, die` in Nominativ and `den, die, das, die` in Akkusativ**!

Look at how the last letters match side by side:

| Case & Gender | Definite Article (`the`) | Demonstrative (`this / these`) | Question Word (`which?`) | Secret Ending Pattern |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nominativ Masculine** (*der Pullover*) | d**er** | dies**er** | welch**er** | **`-er`** |
| **Nominativ Feminine** (*die Hose*) | di**e** | dies**e** | welch**e** | **`-e`** |
| **Nominativ Neuter** (*das Hemd*) | da**s** | dies**es** | welch**es** | **`-es`** |
| **Nominativ Plural** (*die Schuhe*) | di**e** | dies**e** | welch**e** | **`-e`** |
| **Akkusativ Masculine** (*den Pullover*) | d**en** ⚠️ | **diesen** ⚠️ | **welchen** ⚠️ | **`-en`** *(Only Masculine changes!)* |
| **Akkusativ Feminine** (*die Hose*) | di**e** | dies**e** | welch**e** | **`-e`** |
| **Akkusativ Neuter** (*das Hemd*) | da**s** | dies**es** | welch**es** | **`-es`** |
| **Akkusativ Plural** (*die Schuhe*) | di**e** | dies**e** | welch**e** | **`-e`** |

## 4. Asking About the Price (`Nach dem Preis fragen`) & Words of Frequency (`Häufigkeitsangaben`)

### Part A: Asking and Answering About Prices (`kostet` vs. `kosten`)

When you are shopping in Germany, there are two ways to ask *"How much does this cost?"*:
1. **`Was kostet …?`** *(Literally: "What costs …?")*
2. **`Wie viel kostet …?`** *(Literally: "How much costs …?")*

Both are 100% natural and polite! The only thing you need to watch is **Singular vs. Plural**:
* **For ONE thing or ONE unit of weight/volume (`ein Glas, ein Kilo, ein Liter`):** Use **`kostet`** (ending in **`-t`**)!
  * <GermanAudio text="Was kostet das Glas? Wie viel kostet das Glas? Das Glas kostet ein Euro fünfundneunzig." />
  * **`Was kostet das Glas?`** / **`Wie viel kostet das Glas?`** ➔ **`Das Glas kostet 1,95 Euro (ein Euro fünfundneunzig).`** *(From Batch 02 Page 29!)*
  * <GermanAudio text="Entschuldigung, was kostet ein Kilo Tomaten? Ein Kilo Tomaten kostet zwei Euro neunundneunzig." />
  * **`Entschuldigung, was kostet ein Kilo Tomaten?`** ➔ **`Ein Kilo Tomaten kostet 2,99 Euro.`** *(Even though "Tomaten" is plural, the subject is **ein Kilo** = singular, so we use **kostet**!)*
  * <GermanAudio text="Und wie viel kostet ein Liter Milch? Ein Liter Milch kostet ein Euro zwanzig." />
  * **`Und wie viel kostet ein Liter Milch?`** ➔ **`Ein Liter Milch kostet 1,20 Euro.`**
* **For PLURAL items (`die Äpfel, die Bananen, zwei Flaschen`):** Use **`kosten`** (ending in **`-en`**)!
  * <GermanAudio text="Und was kosten die Bananen? Die Bananen kosten ein Euro fünfzig pro Kilo." />
  * **`Und was kosten die Bananen?`** ➔ **`Die Bananen kosten 1,50 Euro pro Kilo.`**
  * <GermanAudio text="Was kosten die Äpfel? Die Äpfel kosten zwei Euro." />
  * **`Was kosten die Äpfel?`** ➔ **`Die Äpfel kosten 2,00 Euro.`**

#### How Germans Read Prices Out Loud (`1,95 €`)
Notice two small things about German prices:
1. German writes a **comma** (`,`) where English writes a decimal point: **`1,95 Euro`**.
2. In spoken German, you simply say `[Euros] + Euro + [Cents]`:
   * **`1,95 €`** ➔ **ein Euro fünfundneunzig** *(1 Euro 95)*
   * **`2,99 €`** ➔ **zwei Euro neunundneunzig** *(2 Euro 99)*
   * **`1,20 €`** ➔ **ein Euro zwanzig** *(1 Euro 20)*
   * **`4,20 €`** ➔ **vier Euro zwanzig** *(4 Euro 20)*

### Real-Life Germany: Supermarket & Checkout Culture (`5.1 & 5.5`)

Shopping at a German supermarket (*REWE, Edeka, ALDI, Lidl, Kaufland, Penny, Netto*) has a few famous everyday customs that every newcomer should know from Day 1:

#### 1. The Coin for the Shopping Cart (`Der Einkaufswagen`) & Finding Aisles (`Der Gang`)
* **der Einkaufswagen** (shopping trolley/cart) vs. **der Einkaufskorb** (hand basket):
  Most German shopping carts are chained together outside the entrance. To unlock one, you insert a **1-Euro or 50-Cent coin (`die Münze`)** or a plastic token (*der Einkaufswagenchip*) into the slot. When you return the cart and click the chain back in, your coin pops right back out!
* **Navigating the aisles (`der Gang`), shelves (`das Regal`), and special offers (`das Angebot`):**
  * *„Entschuldigung, **wo finde ich** den Reis / die Hafermilch?“* — *„**In Gang 3**, links **im Regal** / vorne **in der Kühltheke** (chilled section).“*
  * **im Angebot / das Sonderangebot:** On sale / special offer (*„Die Erdbeeren sind heute **im Angebot**!“*).

#### 2. The Bottle Deposit System (`Das Pfandsystem — Einweg vs. Mehrweg`)
In Germany, when you buy water, juice, soda, or beer in plastic bottles, cans, or glass bottles, you pay a small **refundable bottle deposit (`das Pfand`)** on top of the drink price:
* **Einwegpfand (`0,25 €`):** Single-use plastic bottles and aluminum cans carry the *DPG Pfand* logo and have a **25-cent deposit**.
* **Mehrwegpfand (`0,08 €` to `0,15 €`):** Reusable glass and sturdy plastic bottles carry a **8- to 15-cent deposit**.
* **How to get your deposit back (`das Leergut zurückbringen`):**
  Never throw *Pfand* bottles into the trash! Take your empty bottles (**das Leergut**) back to the supermarket, feed them into the bottle-return machine (**der Pfandautomat / der Leergutautomat**), press the green button, and take the printed deposit slip (**der Pfandbon**). Hand the **Pfandbon** to the cashier at the register, and the money is deducted from your grocery bill (or paid out in cash)!

#### 3. Surviving the High-Speed Checkout (`An der Kasse`) & Payment (`Bar oder mit Karte`)
German supermarket cashiers scan items very fast, and there are no baggers—most shoppers place scanned items straight back into their **Einkaufswagen** and pack them calmly at the packing counter behind the registers!

### Part B: Words to Show Frequency (`Häufigkeitsangaben` — How Often?)

When someone asks you **`Wie oft …?`** (*How often …?*), you can answer using the **6-step frequency scale** (from 100% down to 0%) or using exact time counts (*once, twice, three times per day/week*):

#### The 6-Step Frequency Scale (100% ➔ 0%)

```text
100%   ████████████████████   immer       (always)      / jeden Tag (every day)
 85%   █████████████████░░░   meistens    (mostly / usually)
 70%   ██████████████░░░░░░   oft         (often)
 40%   ████████░░░░░░░░░░░░   manchmal    (sometimes)
 15%   ███░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░   selten      (seldom / rarely)
```

#### 6. The German Everyday Culture Guide (`8.9 — Deutsche Alltagskultur`)

| # | Cultural Topic | What Every Newcomer to Germany Should Know | Useful German Phrase |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **die Pünktlichkeit** *(Punctuality)* | Arriving **5 minutes early** for appointments (*Arzt, Behörde, Arbeit*) is standard. If you are delayed by even 5–10 minutes, send a short message immediately! | *„Es tut mir leid, mein Zug hat **Verspätung**. Ich komme 10 Minuten später.“* |

27. What is the difference between **ein Einzelzimmer mit Halbpension** and **ein Doppelzimmer mit Vollpension** in a German hotel?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  * **ein Doppelzimmer mit Vollpension** = a **double room** (for 2 people) with **full board** (all 3 meals included).

</details>

<details>
