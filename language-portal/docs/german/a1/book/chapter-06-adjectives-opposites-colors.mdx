## 1. What is an Adjective? (Explained from Zero)

### Plain-English Explanation: What Does "Adjective" Mean?

In grammar books, you will see the word **Adjective** (*das Adjektiv*, plural: *die Adjektive*). In German schoolbooks, it is also lovingly called a **`Wie-Wort`** (a *"How-Word"*).

Why is it called a *"How-Word"*?
Because an **adjective** is simply a **describing word** that tells us **HOW** a person, place, animal, or thing is!

Look at these everyday English examples:
* *The coffee is **hot**.* (How is the coffee? ➔ **hot**!)
* *That is a **new** car.* (What kind of car is it? ➔ **new**!)
* *My neighbor is **friendly** and **hardworking**.* (How is my neighbor? ➔ **friendly** and **hardworking**!)
* *The jacket is **blue**.* (What color is the jacket? ➔ **blue**!)

Words like *hot, cold, new, old, big, small, fast, slow, expensive, cheap, friendly, clean, dirty, red,* and *blue* are all **adjectives**!

---

### Two Golden Basics About German Adjectives

#### 1. German Adjectives Are Written with a LOWERCASE First Letter!
As you learned in Chapter 3, all German **nouns** (people, places, and things like *der **T**isch, das **A**uto, die **B**lume*) begin with a **CAPITAL letter**.
**Adjectives** are NOT nouns—so unless an adjective happens to be the very first word of a sentence, it is always written with a **small (lowercase) first letter**:
* *Das **A**uto ist **schnell** und **modern**.* (`Auto` = Noun = Capital `A`; `schnell` & `modern` = Adjectives = lowercase `s` & `m`!)
* *Maria ist eine **gute** **F**reundin.* (`gute` = Adjective = lowercase `g`; `Freundin` = Noun = Capital `F`!)

#### 2. You Ask for an Adjective with the Question Word `Wie?` ("How? / What ... like?")
In Chapter 5, you learned the W-question word **`Wie?`** (*How?*). Whenever you want someone to describe a person, place, or thing with an adjective, ask **`Wie ist ...?`** (singular) or **`Wie sind ...?`** (plural):
* **Question:** <GermanAudio text="Wie ist das Wetter heute?" /> — **`Wie ist das Wetter heute?`** *(How is the weather today?)*
  * **Answer:** **`Es ist warm und sonnig.`** *(It is warm and sunny.)*
* **Question:** <GermanAudio text="Wie ist dein neues Zimmer?" /> — **`Wie ist dein neues Zimmer?`** *(What is your new room like?)*
  * **Answer:** **`Es ist groß, hell und sehr gemütlich!`** *(It is big, bright, and very cozy!)*
* **Question:** <GermanAudio text="Wie sind deine Kollegen?" /> — **`Wie sind deine Kollegen?`** *(What are your colleagues like?)*
  * **Answer:** **`Sie sind sehr nett und hilfsbereit.`** *(They are very nice and helpful.)*

## 2. The Complete A1 Opposites Bank (`Gegenteile` — Every Pair from Your Notebook)

### Why Learning Adjectives in Opposite Pairs (`Gegenteile`) is a Superpower!

Your brain remembers words **twice as fast** when you learn them as **opposites** (*das Gegenteil*, plural: *die Gegenteile*):
* Instead of memorizing *groß* (big) alone, you link it immediately to its twin opposite: **`groß` (big) ↔ `klein` (small)**!
* Instead of memorizing *billig* (cheap) alone, you link it to **`billig` (cheap) ↔ `teuer` (expensive)**!

In your class notebooks (Batch 02 Pages 23–24 and Batch 08 Page 107), we built a complete treasury of A1 adjectives and opposite pairs. Let us master all of them in 4 clear, logical groups with everyday example sentences!

### Group 1: Size, Speed, Age & Time (`Größe, Tempo, Alter und Zeit`)

| Adjective (+) | English | ↔ | Opposite (–) | English | Simple German Example Sentence |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`groß`** | big / large / tall | ↔ | **`klein`** | small / little / short (person) | *Berlin ist **groß**, aber mein Dorf ist **klein**.*<br/>*(Berlin is big, but my village is small.)* |
| **`lang`** | long | ↔ | **`kurz`** | short (length or time) | *Der Film ist **lang**, aber die Pause ist **kurz**.*<br/>*(The movie is long, but the break is short.)* |
| **`dick`** | thick / fat | ↔ | **`dünn`** *(or `schlank`)* | thin / slim | *Das Wörterbuch ist **dick**, aber das Heft ist **dünn**.*<br/>*(The dictionary is thick, but the notebook is thin.)* |
| **`schnell`** | fast / quick | ↔ | **`langsam`** | slow | *Der ICE-Zug ist **schnell**, aber der Bus ist **langsam**.*<br/>*(The ICE train is fast, but the bus is slow.)* |
| **`früh`** | early | ↔ | **`spät`** | late | *Am Montag stehe ich **früh** auf, nicht **spät**.*<br/>*(On Monday I get up early, not late.)* |
| **`jung`** | young *(people/living things)* | ↔ | **`alt`** | old | *Mein Sohn ist noch **jung**, und mein Großvater ist **alt**.*<br/>*(My son is still young, and my grandfather is old.)* |
| **`neu`** | new *(objects/things)* | ↔ | **`alt`** | old / used | *Mein Fahrrad ist **neu**, aber mein Auto ist **alt**.*<br/>*(My bicycle is new, but my car is old.)* |
| **`modern`** | modern | ↔ | **`altmodisch`** | old-fashioned | *Die Wohnung ist **modern**, aber das Sofa ist **altmodisch**.*<br/>*(The apartment is modern, but the sofa is old-fashioned.)* |

:::tip Notice the Two Opposites of `alt` (old)!
Just like in English:
* When talking about a **person's age**, the opposite of **`alt`** (*old*) is **`jung`** (*young*): *Der Mann ist **jung** ↔ **alt**.*
* When talking about an **object** (a car, a book, a phone, a house), the opposite of **`alt`** (*old*) is **`neu`** (*new*): *Das Handy ist **neu** ↔ **alt**.*
:::

| Adjective (+) | English | ↔ | Opposite (–) | English | Simple German Example Sentence |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`gut`** | good | ↔ | **`schlecht`** | bad | *Das Essen hier ist sehr **gut**, nicht **schlecht**.*<br/>*(The food here is very good, not bad.)* |
| **`schön`** | beautiful / nice | ↔ | **`hässlich`** | ugly | *Die Blumen sind **schön**. Die alte Fabrik ist **hässlich**.*<br/>*(The flowers are beautiful. The old factory is ugly.)* |
| **`hübsch` / `schick`** | pretty / stylish | ↔ | **`unattraktiv`** | unattractive | *Das Kleid ist sehr **hübsch** und **schick**!*<br/>*(The dress is very pretty and stylish!)* |
| **`einfach` / `leicht`** | easy / simple / light (weight) | ↔ | **`schwierig` / `schwer`** | difficult / hard / heavy (weight) | *Die Frage ist **einfach** (**leicht**), nicht **schwierig** (**schwer**).*<br/>*(The question is easy, not difficult.)* |
| **`billig` / `günstig`** | cheap / affordable (good value) | ↔ | **`teuer`** | expensive | *Die Banane ist **günstig** (**billig**), aber das Auto ist **teuer**.*<br/>*(The banana is affordable, but the car is expensive.)* |
| **`interessant`** | interesting | ↔ | **`langweilig`** | boring | *Das Buch ist **interessant**, aber der Film ist **langweilig**.*<br/>*(The book is interesting, but the movie is boring.)* |
| **`richtig`** | right / correct | ↔ | **`falsch`** | wrong / false | *Deine Antwort ist **richtig**, nicht **falsch**!*<br/>*(Your answer is correct, not wrong!)* |
| **`ganz` / `in Ordnung`** | whole / intact / working fine | ↔ | **`kaputt`** | broken / out of order | *Mein Computer ist **in Ordnung**, aber mein Handy ist **kaputt**.*<br/>*(My computer is working fine, but my cell phone is broken.)* |
| **`praktisch` / `nützlich`** | practical / useful | ↔ | **`unpraktisch`** | impractical | *Die App ist sehr **praktisch** und **nützlich**.*<br/>*(The app is very practical and useful.)* |
| **`wichtig`** | important | ↔ | **`unwichtig`** | unimportant | *Grammatik ist **wichtig**, aber Sprechen ist auch sehr **wichtig**!* |

:::info Helpful Nuance: `leicht` vs. `schwer` AND `billig` vs. `günstig`
1. **`leicht`** means BOTH **"easy"** (same as *einfach*) AND **"light in weight"** (*Der Koffer ist leicht = The suitcase is light*).
   **`schwer`** means BOTH **"difficult"** (same as *schwierig*) AND **"heavy in weight"** (*Der Koffer ist schwer = The suitcase is heavy*).
2. **`billig`** means *"cheap"* (and can sometimes sound like "low quality"), whereas **`günstig`** means *"affordable / good value / reasonably priced"*. In German shops, people love the word **`günstig`**!
:::

## 3. The Two Ways to Use Adjectives in Sentences (Why Do Adjectives Sometimes Have Endings?)

Many learners wonder: *"Why do I sometimes see **`rot`** (Das Auto ist **rot**), and other times **`rotes`** or **`rote`** (ein **rotes** Auto / das **rote** Auto)?"*

Let us look at the exact visual explanation from our course notes (`Complete-A1-Course-Notes.pdf` Pages 810–825 and `Batch 03` Page 31). Once you see the **Two Ways** an adjective can sit in a sentence, the mystery disappears completely!

```text
WAY 1: Adjective AFTER the Verb (at the end of the clause) ──➔ NO ENDING AT ALL! (Never changes!)
       • Das Auto ist rot.
       • Die Autos sind rot.
       • Ich finde den Film interessant.

WAY 2: Adjective RIGHT BEFORE a Noun (inside the noun block) ──➔ TAKES A SMALL ENDING (-er, -e, -es, -en)!
       • Das ist ein rotes Auto.
       • Das ist ein alter Mann.
       • Das ist eine schöne Blume.
```

Let us examine both ways step by step!

---

### Way 1: After the Verb (`sein`, `werden`, `bleiben`, `finden`) — NO ENDING AT ALL!

When the adjective stands **alone after the verb** (describing the subject or object from across the verb), it stays in its pure dictionary form with **ZERO ending**!

It does **NOT** matter whether the noun is masculine (`der`), feminine (`die`), neutral (`das`), or plural (`die`). The adjective **never changes**!

#### A. After `sein` (to be — *bin, bist, ist, sind, seid*), `werden` (to become), or `bleiben` (to stay/remain):
Look at the classroom examples from your Batch 03 notebook (Page 31) and course notes:
* **`Der Mann ist alt.`** (*The man is old.* — masculine `der Mann` ➔ **`alt`**)
* **`Die Frau ist alt.`** (*The woman is old.* — feminine `die Frau` ➔ **`alt`**)
* **`Das Haus ist alt.`** (*The house is old.* — neutral `das Haus` ➔ **`alt`**)
* **`Die Häuser sind alt.`** (*The houses are old.* — plural `die Häuser` ➔ **`alt`**)
* **`Das Auto ist rot.`** (*The car is red.*) / **`Die Autos sind rot.`** (*The cars are red.*)
* **`Das Goethe-Zentrum ist sauber.`** (*The Goethe Center is clean.*)
* **`Es wird kalt.`** (*It is getting cold.*)

#### B. After `Ich finde ...` ("I find X [adjective] / I think X is [adjective]" — Batch 03, Page 31!):
In your Batch 03 notebook (Page 31), you learned a wonderful A1 pattern for expressing your opinion:
> **`Ich finde + [Noun in Accusative] + [Adjective with NO ending]!`**

Look at how easy and powerful this is:
* **`Ich finde den Film interessant.`** (*I find the movie interesting / I think the movie is interesting.* — `der Film` becomes `den Film` after `ich finde`, and `interessant` has **NO ending**!)
* **`Ich finde die Klimaanlage kalt.`** (*I find the air conditioning cold.* — from Batch 03, Page 31!)
* **`Ich finde das Buch super / langweilig.`** (*I think the book is great / boring.*)
* **`Ich finde Deutsch einfach und logisch!`** (*I think German is easy and logical!*)
* **`Wie findest du die Wohnung? — Ich finde sie sehr gemütlich!`** (*How do you find the apartment? — I think it is very cozy!*)

---

### Way 2: Right BEFORE a Noun (`ein rotes Auto`, `der alte Mann`) — Why Does It Take a Small Ending?

Now, what happens when you put the adjective **directly in front of the noun**—squeezed between the article (`ein / der`) and the noun (`Mann / Blume / Auto`)?
* `ein` + `alt...` + `Mann` (*an old man*)
* `eine` + `schön...` + `Blume` (*a beautiful flower*)
* `ein` + `rot...` + `Auto` (*a red car*)

**Why does German put a small ending on the adjective here?**
Look at the indefinite article **`ein`** in the Nominative case (from Chapter 3):
* Masculine (`der Mann`) ➔ **`ein`** Mann
* Neutral (`das Auto`) ➔ **`ein`** Auto

Notice a problem? The word **`ein`** looks **identical** for masculine (`der`) and neutral (`das`)! You cannot see the **`-r`** of `de**r**` or the **`-s`** of `da**s**` on the word `ein`!
So the adjective steps up and says: **"Don't worry! Since I am standing right in front of the noun, I will wear the `-r` of `der`, the `-e` of `die`, and the `-s` of `das` so everyone knows the noun's gender!"**

---

### Pattern 1: Adjective Endings After `ein / eine / ein` (and `kein`, `mein`, `dein`) in the Nominative! (Batch 03, Page 31)

Look at how the adjective simply borrows the last letter(s) of **`der` (`-er`)**, **`die` (`-e`)**, and **`das` (`-es`)**:

| Gender | Definite Article Signal | Indefinite Article (`ein`) + **Adjective Ending** + Noun | Classroom Examples (From Batch 03 Page 31 & Course Notes!) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Masculine** | `de`**`r`** | **`ein`** + Adjective**`-er`** + Noun | • **`ein alter Mann`** (*an old man*)<br/>• **`ein guter Freund`** (*a good friend*)<br/>• **`ein neuer Tisch`** (*a new table*)<br/>• **`mein lieber Vater`** (*my dear father*) |
| **Feminine** | `di`**`e`** | **`eine`** + Adjective**`-e`** + Noun | • **`eine schöne Blume`** (*a beautiful flower*)<br/>• **`eine neue Tasche`** (*a new bag*)<br/>• **`eine junge Frau`** (*a young woman*)<br/>• **`meine gute Freundin`** (*my good female friend*) |
| **Neutral** | `da`**`s`** | **`ein`** + Adjective**`-es`** + Noun | • **`ein nettes Mädchen`** (*a nice girl* — `das Mädchen`!)<br/>• **`ein rotes Auto`** (*a red car*)<br/>• **`ein kleines Kind`** (*a small child*)<br/>• **`ein schönes Haus`** (*a beautiful house*) |
| **Plural** *(with `keine / meine`)* | `die` | **`keine / meine`** + Adjective**`-en`** + Noun *(or no article: Adj**`-e`**)* | • **`keine neuen Autos`** (*no new cars*)<br/>• **`meine guten Freunde`** (*my good friends*)<br/>• *(Without article: **schöne Blumen** = beautiful flowers)* |

Look at that Nominative `ein`-table again:
* Masculine (`der`) ➔ `ein alt`**`er`** `Mann` (**`-er`**)
* Feminine (`die`) ➔ `eine schön`**`e`** `Blume` (**`-e`**)
* Neutral (`das`) ➔ `ein rot`**`es`** `Auto` (**`-es`**)

**Memory Hook:** **`-er` · `-e` · `-es`** = **`der` · `die` · `das`**!

### Pattern 2: Adjective Endings After `der / die / das` (The Definite Article) in the Nominative!

What if you use **`der`**, **`die`**, or **`das`** in front of the adjective (*"the old man", "the beautiful flower", "the red car"*)?

Here, the article (**`der`**, **`die`**, **`das`**) **ALREADY** shows the gender (`-r`, `-e`, `-s`) loud and clear!
Because the article already did the hard work of showing gender, the adjective can relax! In the Nominative singular after `der`, `die`, and `das`, the adjective simply takes **`-e`** for all three genders (and **`-en`** in the plural):

| Gender | Definite Article + **Adjective Ending** + Noun | Examples (`Complete-A1-Course-Notes.pdf` P.813–819) | Compare with `ein / eine`! |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Masculine** | **`der`** + Adjective**`-e`** + Noun | • **`der alte Mann`** (*the old man*)<br/>• **`der neue Tisch`** (*the new table*) | *(vs. `ein alt`**`er`** `Mann`)* |
| **Feminine** | **`die`** + Adjective**`-e`** + Noun | • **`die schöne Blume`** (*the beautiful flower*)<br/>• **`die neue Tasche`** (*the new bag*) | *(same `-e`: `eine schön`**`e`** `Blume`)* |
| **Neutral** | **`das`** + Adjective**`-e`** + Noun | • **`das nette Mädchen`** (*the nice girl*)<br/>• **`das rote Auto`** (*the red car*) | *(vs. `ein rot`**`es`** `Auto`)* |
| **Plural** | **`die`** + Adjective**`-en`** + Noun | • **`die roten Autos`** (*the red cars*)<br/>• **`die schönen Blumen`** (*the beautiful flowers*) | *(same `-en` as `meine rot`**`en`** `Autos`)* |

### Pattern 3: Preview of Accusative Objects (Buying & Wearing Things — From Batch 06, Page 89!)

What happens when the noun is the **Direct Object (Accusative)**—after verbs like **`haben`** (*to have*), **`kaufen`** (*to buy*), **`tragen`** (*to wear*), **`suchen`** (*to look for*), **`brauchen`** (*to need*), or **`möchten`** (*would like*)?

Remember our golden rule of the Accusative case from Chapter 3:
* **Feminine (`die` / `eine`) and Neutral (`das` / `ein`) NEVER change in the Accusative!** They stay 100% identical to the Nominative (`eine schwarze Hose`, `ein gelbes Hemd`)!
* **ONLY Masculine (`der` ➔ `den` / `ein` ➔ `einen`) changes in the Accusative!** And in the Masculine Accusative, **both the article AND the adjective take `-en`**!
  * `der blaue Pullover` (Nominative) ➔ **`den blauen Pullover`** (Accusative!)
  * `ein blauer Pullover` (Nominative) ➔ **`einen blauen Pullover`** (Accusative!)

Look at the exact clothing & shopping examples from your Batch 06 notebook (Page 89):

| Noun Gender in Accusative | Article + Adjective + Noun | Complete Everyday Sentence (Batch 06, Page 89!) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Masculine (`der` ➔ `einen / den`)**<br/>*Double `-en`!* | **`einen blauen Pullover`**<br/>**`den schwarzen Mantel`**<br/>**`einen neuen Computer`** | • *Ich trage **einen blauen Pullover**.* (I am wearing a blue sweater.)<br/>• *Er kauft **den schwarzen Mantel**.* (He is buying the black coat.)<br/>• *Ich brauche **einen neuen Computer**.* (I need a new computer.) |
| **Feminine (`die` ➔ `eine / die`)**<br/>*Stays `-e`!* | **`eine schwarze Hose`**<br/>**`eine rote Jacke`**<br/>**`die schöne Tasche`** | • *Ich kaufe **eine schwarze Hose**.* (I am buying a black pair of trousers.)<br/>• *Sie trägt **eine rote Jacke**.* (She is wearing a red jacket.)<br/>• *Ich nehme **die schöne Tasche**.* (I am taking the beautiful bag.) |
| **Neutral (`das` ➔ `ein / das`)**<br/>*Stays `-es` (after `ein`) / `-e` (after `das`)!* | **`ein gelbes Hemd`**<br/>**`ein neues Auto`**<br/>**`das weiße T-Shirt`** | • *Ich kaufe **ein gelbes Hemd**.* (I am buying a yellow shirt.)<br/>• *Ich kaufe morgen **ein neues Auto**.* (I will buy a new car tomorrow.)<br/>• *Er trägt **das weiße T-Shirt**.* (He is wearing the white T-shirt.) |
| **Plural (`die` / no article)** | **`schwarze Schuhe`**<br/>**`die neuen Schuhe`** | • *Ich trage **schwarze Schuhe**.* (I am wearing black shoes.)<br/>• *Ich kaufe **die neuen Schuhe**.* (I am buying the new shoes.) |

   * **`hellgrün`** (light green), **`dunkelgrün`** (dark green)

### Describing People: Appearance & Character (`Personen beschreiben: Aussehen und Charakter`)

In everyday life in Germany—for example, when you are meeting a new colleague or language partner at the train station (*„Wie erkenne ich dich?“* = How do I recognize you?), describing your child at kindergarten, or talking about friends and family—you need to distinguish between **two questions** and **four core verbs**:

#### 1. The Two Questions & Four Verbs for Describing People

| What You Are Asking or Describing | German Question / Verb | Everyday Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Physical Appearance** (*What does he/she look like?*) | **`Wie sieht er / sie aus?`**<br/>*(Separable verb `aussehen`: **`aus`** goes to the end!)* | • *Wie **siehst** du **aus**?* (What do you look like?)<br/>• *Er **sieht** sportlich und sympathisch **aus**.* (He looks sporty and likeable.) |
| **Character & General Traits** (*What is he/she like?*) | **`Wie ist er / sie?`**<br/>Verb: **`sein`** (*bin, bist, ist, sind*) | • *Er **ist** groß, mittelgroß oder klein und schlank.* (He is tall, medium-height, or short and slim.)<br/>• *Sie **ist** freundlich, geduldig und pünktlich.* (She is friendly, patient, and punctual.) |
| **Hair, Eyes & Beard** (*What features do they have?*) | Verb: **`haben`** (*habe, hast, hat*) | • *Sie **hat** lange, glatte, schwarze Haare und braune Augen.* (She has long, straight, black hair and brown eyes.)<br/>• *Mein Bruder **hat** kurze, lockige Haare und **einen Bart**.* (My brother has short, curly hair and a beard.) |
| **Glasses, Beard & Clothes** (*What do they wear?*) | Verb: **`tragen`** (*ich trage, du trägst, er/sie trägt*) | • *Ich **trage** **eine Brille**.* (I wear glasses — singular in German!)<br/>• *Er **trägt** **einen Bart** und eine blaue Jacke.* (He wears/has a beard and a blue jacket.) |

#### 2. Complete Vocabulary Box: Appearance (`das Aussehen`) & Character (`der Charakter`)

* **Height & Build (`die Größe und die Figur`):**
  * **`groß`** (tall, e.g. *Ich bin 1,80 Meter groß*), **`mittelgroß`** (medium height), **`klein`** (short), **`schlank`** (slim), **`kräftig`** (sturdy / strong build), **`sportlich`** (sporty/athletic). *(Politeness tip: avoid calling people `dick` [fat] or `hässlich` [ugly]—`kräftig` is much politer!)*
* **Hair (`das Haar`, usually plural **`die Haare`**!) & Eyes (`die Augen`):**
  * **Length & Style:** **`kurze Haare`** (short hair), **`lange Haare`** (long hair), **`glatte Haare`** (straight hair), **`lockige Haare`** (curly hair).
  * **Color:** **`blonde / braune / schwarze / graue / rote Haare`** (blond / brown / black / grey / red hair); **`blaue / braune / grüne Augen`** (blue / brown / green eyes).
  * *(Grammar reminder: In German, hair is normally plural—`Ich habe kurze Haare` or `Meine Haare sind kurz`! And `die Brille` [glasses] is singular: `Ich trage eine Brille`!)*
* **Essential Character Traits (`der Charakter`):**
  * **`sympathisch`** (likeable/pleasant), **`freundlich`** (friendly), **`nett`** (nice/kind), **`humorvoll`** (humorous / having a good sense of humor), **`lustig`** (funny/cheerful), **`ruhig`** (calm/quiet), **`geduldig`** (patient), **`pünktlich`** (punctual / on time), **`höflich`** (polite), **`hilfsbereit`** (helpful / ready to help).

### Step 3: Way 1 vs. Way 2 — When Do Adjectives Take an Ending? (Questions 15–22)

Fill in the adjective with the right ending—or **NO ending** if it stands after the verb!

<details>
  <summary><strong>15. After `sein` (Way 1):</strong><br/>a) „Das Auto ist ________ (rot).“<br/>b) „Die Autos sind ________ (rot).“<br/>c) „Das Goethe-Zentrum ist ________ (sauber).“</summary>

  **Answer:** a) **`rot`** · b) **`rot`** · c) **`sauber`** (NO ending at all!).
  *Why:* Whenever an adjective stands after the verb (`ist / sind`), it NEVER takes an ending—even in the plural!
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>16. After `Ich finde ...` (From Batch 03, Page 31!):</strong><br/>a) „Ich finde den Film ________ (interessant).“<br/>b) „Ich finde die Klimaanlage ________ (kalt).“</summary>

  **Answer:** a) **`interessant`** · b) **`kalt`** (NO ending!).
  *Why:* In the `Ich finde + [Object] + [Adjective]` pattern, the adjective stands at the end after the noun, so it stays in its base form without any ending!
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>17. Nominative with `ein / eine / ein` (The `-er, -e, -es` Rule from Batch 03, Page 31!):</strong><br/>Fill in the ending on the adjective:<br/>a) <em>der Mann:</em> Das ist ein alt____ Mann.<br/>b) <em>die Blume:</em> Das ist eine schön____ Blume.<br/>c) <em>das Mädchen:</em> Das ist ein nett____ Mädchen.</summary>

  **Answer:**
  a) **`ein alter Mann`** (`der` ➔ **`-er`**)
  b) **`eine schöne Blume`** (`die` ➔ **`-e`**)
  c) **`ein nettes Mädchen`** (`das` ➔ **`-es`**)
  *Why:* After `ein/eine/ein` in the Nominative, the adjective shows the gender of the noun (`-er, -e, -es`)!
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>18. More Nominative with `ein / eine / ein`:</strong><br/>a) <em>der Tisch:</em> Das ist ein neu____ Tisch.<br/>b) <em>die Tasche:</em> Das ist eine modern____ Tasche.<br/>c) <em>das Auto:</em> Das ist ein schnell____ Auto.</summary>

  **Answer:**
  a) **`ein neuer Tisch`** (`der` ➔ **`-er`**)
  b) **`eine moderne Tasche`** (`die` ➔ **`-e`**)
  c) **`ein schnelles Auto`** (`das` ➔ **`-es`**)
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>19. Nominative with Definite Articles (`der, die, das`):</strong><br/>Fill in the ending on the adjective:<br/>a) <strong>Der</strong> alt____ Mann liest.<br/>b) <strong>Die</strong> schön____ Blume blüht.<br/>c) <strong>Das</strong> rot____ Auto fährt schnell.<br/>d) <strong>Die</strong> rot____ Autos sind teuer. (Plural!)</summary>

  **Answer:**
  a) **`Der alte Mann`** (**`-e`**)
  b) **`Die schöne Blume`** (**`-e`**)
  c) **`Das rote Auto`** (**`-e`**)
  d) **`Die roten Autos`** (**`-en`** in plural!)
  *Why:* Because `der, die, das` already shows the singular gender clearly, the adjective simply takes **`-e`** in all three singular genders, and **`-en`** in the plural!
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>20. Accusative Masculine Shopping Rule (From Batch 06, Page 89!):</strong><br/><em>der Pullover (masculine)</em>: „Ich trage ein____ blau____ Pullover.“</summary>

  **Answer:** **`einen blauen`** (*„Ich trage **einen blauen** Pullover.“*)
  *Why:* After `ich trage` (Accusative direct object), a masculine (`der`) noun takes **`-en`** on both the article (`einen`) and the adjective (`blauen`)!
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>21. Accusative Feminine & Neutral Shopping Rule (From Batch 06, Page 89!):</strong><br/>a) <em>die Hose (feminine)</em>: „Ich kaufe ein____ schwarz____ Hose.“<br/>b) <em>das Hemd (neutral)</em>: „Ich kaufe ein____ gelb____ Hemd.“</summary>

  **Answer:**
  a) **`eine schwarze`** (*„Ich kaufe **eine schwarze** Hose.“*)
  b) **`ein gelbes`** (*„Ich kaufe **ein gelbes** Hemd.“*)
  *Why:* Feminine (`-e`) and Neutral (`-es`) are identical in Nominative and Accusative!
</details>

<details>
  <summary><strong>22. Compare Side by Side:</strong> Translate both sentences into German:<br/>a) <em>The car is new.</em><br/>b) <em>That is a new car (`das Auto`).</em></summary>

  **Answer:**
  a) **`Das Auto ist neu.`** (After `ist` ➔ no ending!)
  b) **`Das ist ein neues Auto.`** (Before `Auto` with `ein` ➔ `-es` ending!)
</details>
