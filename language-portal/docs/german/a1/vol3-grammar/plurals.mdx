# 3.2 Plurals (Der Plural)

## 🎯 What you will learn
* I know that the plural article in German is ALWAYS **die**, regardless of the singular gender.
* I understand why *ein* and *eine* do not exist in the plural.
* I can recognize the 5 common German plural patterns without memorizing endless lists.
* I can order multiples of items in a supermarket or bakery (*drei Brötchen, zwei Äpfel*).

---

## 1. What is a Plural and Why is German Different?

In English, making a word plural is simple: you almost always add an **-s** (*one book ➔ two books, one car ➔ two cars*).

In German, plural formation looks different, but it comes with **one huge simplification**:

### The Golden Rule: Every Plural Word Takes "die"
No matter whether a word was *der*, *die*, or *das* in the singular, **every single plural noun uses the article DIE**:
* *der* Mann ➔ **die** Männer
* *die* Frau ➔ **die** Frauen
* *das* Kind ➔ **die** Kinder

### No "a" or "an" in the Plural
In English, you cannot say *"a books"*. The same is true in German. **ein** and **eine** simply disappear in the plural:
* Singular: *Das ist **ein** Buch.* (That is a book.)
* Plural: *Das sind Bücher.* (Those are books. ❌ Never say *Das sind eine Bücher!*)

---

## 2. The 5 Common Plural Patterns

Do not attempt to memorize plural rules like mathematical formulas. Instead, train your eye to recognize the **5 main patterns**:

### Pattern 1: Add "-n" or "-en" (The Feminine Pattern)
Most feminine (*die*) words take this ending in the plural (a few, like *die Mutter → die Mütter* or *die Stadt → die Städte*, don't):
* *die Frau* ➔ **die Frauen** (women)
* *die Wohnung* ➔ **die Wohnungen** (apartments)
* *die Flasche* ➔ **die Flaschen** (bottles)
* *die Rechnung* ➔ **die Rechnungen** (bills)

### Pattern 2: Add "-e" (Very common for 'der' and 'das' words)
Often accompanied by an Umlaut on the vowel:
* *der Tisch* ➔ **die Tische** (tables)
* *der Tag* ➔ **die Tage** (days)
* *der Freund* ➔ **die Freunde** (friends)
* *der Arzt* ➔ **die Ärzte** (doctors — with Umlaut!)

### Pattern 3: Add "-er" (Very common for short 'das' words)
Usually adds an Umlaut if the vowel is a, o, or u:
* *das Kind* ➔ **die Kinder** (children)
* *das Bild* ➔ **die Bilder** (pictures)
* *das Buch* ➔ **die Bücher** (books — with Umlaut!)
* *das Haus* ➔ **die Häuser** (houses — with Umlaut!)

### Pattern 4: Add "-s" (Borrowed and international words)
Words ending in -a, -i, -o, -u, and words borrowed from English/French take a simple "-s":
* *das Auto* ➔ **die Autos** (cars)
* *das Foto* ➔ **die Fotos** (photos)
* *das Hotel* ➔ **die Hotels** (hotels)
* *das Restaurant* ➔ **die Restaurants**

### Pattern 5: No Ending Added (Only the article changes!)
Masculine and neuter words ending in **-er, -el, -en** (and *-chen*) usually do not change their spelling at all (or only add an Umlaut). Feminine ones take *-n*: *die Schwester → die Schwestern*, *die Kartoffel → die Kartoffeln*.
* *der Lehrer* ➔ **die Lehrer** (teachers — identical spelling, only the article changes!)
* *das Brötchen* ➔ **die Brötchen** (bread rolls — identical!)
* *der Apfel* ➔ **die Äpfel** (apples — only the Umlaut changes!)

## 3. Real-Life Germany: Supermarket Quantities & Price Tags

When grocery shopping in Germany, you will read and hear plurals constantly on price signs:
* *„Äpfel — 2,49 € pro Kilo“* (Apples)
* *„Bananen im Angebot“* (Bananas on sale)
* *„Bitte legen Sie zwei Brötchen in die Tüte.“* (Please place two bread rolls in the bag).

Notice that when you state a specific number (*zwei, drei, vier...*), you use the number directly with the plural noun:
* *Ich möchte **drei Äpfel** und **zwei Brötchen**, bitte.*
