# 3.3 The Nominative Case (Der Nominativ)

## 🎯 What you will learn
* I understand what a grammatical "case" means in German.
* I can identify the **Subject** (the doer of the action) in any sentence.
* I know that words following the verb **sein** (to be) remain in the Nominative case.
* I can use *der, die, das* and *ein, eine* to identify people and objects accurately.
* I can ask *Wer ist das?* (for people) and *Was ist das?* (for things).

---

## 1. What is a "Case" and Why Does German Have Them?

In English, word order does almost all the work:
* *"The dog bites the man."* (The dog is doing the biting).
* *"The man bites the dog."* (The man is doing the biting).

In German, word order is flexible. To make sure listeners know **who is doing the action** and **who is receiving it**, German changes the form of the articles (*der, die, das*). 

These different forms and roles are called **Cases (Kasus)**. 

### The Nominative = The Base Case
The **Nominative Case (Nominativ)** is the standard, original form of every word. When you look up any noun in the dictionary, it is in the Nominative. 
You use the Nominative for:
1. The **Subject** of the sentence (the person or thing performing the action).
2. Identifying what something is after the verb **sein** (to be).

---

## 2. Articles & Pronouns in the Nominative

### The Article Table
| Gender | Definite ("The") | Indefinite ("A / An") | Negative ("No / Not a") |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Masculine (m)** | **der** Mann | **ein** Mann | **kein** Mann |
| **Feminine (f)** | **die** Frau | **eine** Frau | **keine** Frau |
| **Neutral (n)** | **das** Kind | **ein** Kind | **kein** Kind |
| **Plural (pl)** | **die** Autos | *(none)* Autos | **keine** Autos |

### Pronouns in the Nominative (The Doers)
* **ich** (I), **du** (you), **er** (he), **sie** (she), **es** (it)
* **wir** (we), **ihr** (you all), **sie** (they), **Sie** (You formal)

---

## 3. How to Use the Nominative

### A. The Subject of the Sentence (The "Doer")
Ask yourself: **Wer oder was handelt?** (Who or what is performing the verb?)
* **Der Lehrer** trinkt Kaffee. *(Who drinks? The teacher ➔ Der Lehrer = Nominative subject)*
* **Sie** lernt Deutsch. *(Who learns? She ➔ Sie = Nominative subject)*

### B. Contrast: Subject vs. Object
In German, even if the word order flips, the Nominative article tells you who the subject is:
* **Der Mann** sieht den Hund. *(The man sees the dog. Subject = Der Mann)*
* Den Hund sieht **der Mann**. *(Still: The man sees the dog! Because "der" marks the Nominative subject, the man is doing the seeing, even at the end of the sentence!)*

### C. The "sein" (to be) Equation
Normally, a sentence has one Subject (Nominative) and one Object. But the verb **sein** acts like an equals sign (=). **Both sides of the verb stay in the Nominative:**
* Das = **ein Tisch**. ➔ *Das ist **ein Tisch**.*
* Der Mann = **ein Lehrer**. ➔ *Der Mann ist **ein Lehrer**.*
* Ich = **eine Ärztin**. ➔ *Ich bin **eine Ärztin**.*

### D. Asking Questions: "Wer" vs. "Was"
* **Wer ist das?** (Who is that?) ➔ Used for people: *„Das ist mein Kollege.“*
* **Was ist das?** (What is that?) ➔ Used for things: *„Das ist ein Buch.“*
