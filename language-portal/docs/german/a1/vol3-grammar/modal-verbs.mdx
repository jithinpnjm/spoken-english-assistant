# 3.6 Modal Verbs (Modalverben)

## 🎯 What you will learn
* I can explain what a modal verb does and why German uses it.
* I can express what I can, must, want to, should, and am allowed to do.
* I know the difference between *nicht müssen* (optional) and *nicht dürfen* (forbidden).
* I can build sentences using the "verb bracket" pattern (modal verb in position 2, second verb at the end).
* I can conjugate the 6 essential modal verbs without adding incorrect endings to *ich* and *er/sie/es*.

---

## 1. What are Modal Verbs and Why Do We Need Them?

### What is it?
In English and German, a standard sentence states a factual action:
* *Ich schwimme.* (I swim.)
* *Ich lerne Deutsch.* (I learn German.)

A **modal verb** is a helper verb that changes the *meaning* of that action. Instead of saying that an action simply happens, it tells us the condition or attitude around it:
* **Ability:** *Ich **kann** schwimmen.* (I **can** swim / I know how to swim.)
* **Necessity:** *Ich **muss** Deutsch lernen.* (I **have to** learn German.)
* **Desire:** *Ich **möchte** Deutsch lernen.* (I **would like to** learn German.)
* **Permission:** *Ich **darf** hier schwimmen.* (I **am allowed to** swim here.)

### How does it work?
Whenever you use a modal verb, your sentence has **two verbs**:
1. The **modal verb** (can, must, want) which shows the condition or intention.
2. The **main action verb** (swim, learn, go) which names what is actually being done.

Before we look at the word order, here are three simple terms we will use:
* **Subject:** The person or thing doing the action (for example: *ich*, *du*, *mein Kollege*).
* **Conjugation:** Changing the verb ending to match the subject (for example: *ich lern**e***, *du lern**st***).
* **Infinitive:** The basic, unchanged dictionary form of a verb ending in *-en* (for example: *schwimm**en***, *lern**en***).

## 2. The 6 Essential Modal Verbs

Here are the six verbs you will encounter every day in Germany:

| Verb | Meaning | Expresses | Everyday Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **können** | can / to be able to | ability, possibility | *Ich **kann** heute kommen.* (I can come today.) |
| **müssen** | must / to have to | necessity, obligation (no choice) | *Ich **muss** arbeiten.* (I have to work.) |
| **wollen** | to want (direct, strong plan) | intention, strong will | *Wir **wollen** ein Haus kaufen.* (We want to buy a house.) |
| **möchten\*** | would like (polite request) | polite desire, ordering | *Ich **möchte** einen Tee, bitte.* (I would like a tea, please.) |
| **dürfen** | may / to be allowed to | permission, rules, law | *Sie **dürfen** hier parken.* (You are allowed to park here.) |
| **sollen** | should / supposed to | advice, instruction from someone else | *Ich **soll** Tabletten nehmen.* (I am supposed to take tablets.) |

*\*Note on möchten:* In formal grammar, *möchten* is a polite form of *mögen*. At A1, treat it simply as a modal verb that means "would like".

## 3. Crucial Contrasts (Beginner Traps)

German modal verbs have four distinctions that English speakers often mix up:

### A. *wollen* vs. *möchten* (Demanding vs. Polite)
* **wollen** is direct and forceful. In a bakery or restaurant, saying *"Ich will ein Brot"* sounds demanding or childish (like *"I want bread!"*).
* **möchten** is the polite, standard way to ask for things in German:
  * ❌ *Ich will einen Kaffee.* (Too blunt for customer service)
  * ✅ *Ich **möchte** einen Kaffee, bitte.* (Natural and polite)

### B. *können* vs. *dürfen* (Ability vs. Permission)
* **können** = It is physically or practically possible for you to do it.
* **dürfen** = You are allowed to do it – by a rule, a law, or a person (parent, boss, doctor). *Darf ich …?* is also the polite way to ask for permission.
  * *Ich **kann** hier parken.* (My car fits in this space.)
  * *Ich **darf** hier nicht parken.* (There is a no-parking sign; it is illegal.)

### C. *müssen* vs. *sollen* (Own Necessity vs. Someone Else's Instruction)
* **müssen** = You must do it because circumstances give you no choice.
  * *Ich **muss** jetzt gehen, mein Bus kommt.* (I have to go, my bus is coming.)
* **sollen** = Another person (a doctor, boss, parent) told you to do it.
  * *Der Chef sagt, ich **soll** den Bericht schreiben.* (The boss says I should write the report.)

### D. The Danger Trap: *nicht müssen* vs. *nicht dürfen*
In English, "you must not" means "it is forbidden". **In German, *nicht müssen* does not mean that!**
* **nicht dürfen** = **forbidden / not allowed**
  * *Hier **darfst du nicht** rauchen.* (You are **not allowed** to smoke here.)
* **nicht müssen** = **not necessary / optional** (you don't have to, but you can if you want)
  * *Du **musst nicht** kommen.* (You **don't have to** come. It is optional.)

## 4. How the Sentence Works: The "Verb Bracket" (Satzklammer)

In English, two verbs sit together:
> *"I **must call** the doctor today."*

German places the two verbs at opposite ends of the sentence, creating a **bracket** around the other words:

```text
[Position 1: Subject]   [Position 2: Modal Verb]   [Middle: Details & Objects]   [End: Action Verb (Infinitive)]
       Ich                      muss                    heute den Arzt                       anrufen.
```

### The 4-Step Sentence Recipe:
1. **Choose your subject** (*ich, du, er, wir...*) and put it in Position 1.
2. **Conjugate the modal verb** to match the subject and put it in **Position 2**.
3. **Put all other information** (time, place, objects) in the middle.
4. **Put the main action verb at the very end** in its basic dictionary form (the **Infinitive**, ending in *-en*). Do **not** conjugate this verb!

#### Try the recipe with a new example:
* Subject: **Wir**
* Modal verb: **wollen** (for *wir* → *wollen*)
* Details: **heute Pizza**
* Main action verb: **essen** (stays in the infinitive at the end)
* ➔ **Wir wollen heute Pizza essen.** *(We want to eat pizza today.)*

### Contrast: Sentence with and without a modal verb
* Without modal verb: *Ich **gehe** heute nach Hause.* (One verb, conjugated in Position 2)
* With modal verb: *Ich **muss** heute nach Hause **gehen**.* (*muss* is conjugated in Position 2; *gehen* is pushed to the end in the infinitive)

In a **Yes/No question**, the modal verb simply moves to Position 1, but the action verb still stays at the very end:
* *„**Kannst** du mir bitte **helfen**?“* (Can you please help me?)

## 5. How to Conjugate Modal Verbs

Modal verbs follow a consistent, logical pattern across the entire group:
1. **The `ich` and `er / sie / es` forms are identical and have NO ending.** There is no *-e* for *ich* and no *-t* for *er*.
2. **The vowel changes in the singular** (*ich, du, er/sie/es*), except for *sollen* and *möchten*.
3. **The plural forms (*wir, ihr, sie/Sie*) are completely regular.** They keep the original dictionary vowel.

### Conjugation Table

| Pronoun | können (can) | müssen (must) | wollen (want) | möchten (would like) | dürfen (may) | sollen (should) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ich** | kann | muss | will | möchte | darf | soll |
| **du** | kannst | musst | willst | möchtest | darfst | sollst |
| **er / sie / es** | kann | muss | will | möchte | darf | soll |
| **wir** | können | müssen | wollen | möchten | dürfen | sollen |
| **ihr** | könnt | müsst | wollt | möchtet | dürft | sollt |
| **sie / Sie** | können | müssen | wollen | möchten | dürfen | sollen |

**Shortcut:** learn only the *ich* form. *er/sie/es* is the same word, and *du* is the *ich* form + **-st**:
*kann → kannst*, *muss → musst*, *will → willst*, *darf → darfst*, *soll → sollst* (*möchte → möchtest*).
The plural is the dictionary form (*wir können*) with the usual *ihr* **-t** (*ihr könnt*).

**Memory hook (from class): W-S-K-D-M-M-M** = **w**ollen, **s**ollen, **k**önnen, **d**ürfen, **m**üssen,
**m**ögen, **m**öchten. *mögen* (*ich mag, du magst, er mag*) follows the same pattern, but at A1 it is mostly used
**without** a second verb – *Ich mag Kaffee* (see [2.6 Likes & Preferences](../vol2-people/likes-preferences.mdx)).

### ⚠️ False friend: *ich will* ≠ "I will"
*Ich **will** Deutsch lernen* = I **want** to learn German. For English "will" use the present tense with a time
word (*Ich lerne ab Montag Deutsch*) – or *werden* (*Ich werde Deutsch lernen*, see [3.4](./present-tense-verbs.mdx)).

### *können* without a second verb: *Ich kann Deutsch.*
With a language, *können* can stand alone and means "know / speak": *Ich **kann** ein bisschen Deutsch.* (I know a
little German.) · *Kannst du Englisch?* This is short for *… Deutsch sprechen*.

That gives English "know" three German partners:

| Verb | "know" what? | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **kennen** (*ich kenne, du kennst*) | a person, place or thing – be familiar with | *Ich kenne Frau Weber.* · *Kennst du das Café?* |
| **wissen** (*ich weiß, du weißt, er weiß*) | a fact, information | *Ich weiß die Adresse nicht.* · *Das weiß ich leider nicht.* |
| **können** | a language or skill | *Er kann Deutsch.* · *Kannst du Auto fahren?* |

Spelling note: *weiß* (I know) is also the colour *weiß* (white) – the context tells you which. Write *weiß*
with **ß**; *weiss* is the Swiss spelling (or a keyboard without ß).

### Pronunciation Focus:
* **können** [Syllables: kön-nen | Stress: **KÖN**-nen] — Short rounded *ö* sound.
* **müssen** [Syllables: müs-sen | Stress: **MÜS**-sen] — Short rounded *ü* sound.
* **dürfen** [Syllables: dür-fen | Stress: **DÜR**-fen] — Short rounded *ü* sound.

## 6. Common Beginner Mistakes

### ❌ Mistake 1: Conjugating both verbs
* ❌ *Ich kann schwimme.*
* ✅ *Ich **kann schwimmen**.*
* **Why:** Only the modal verb in Position 2 changes to match the person. The action verb at the end remains in the infinitive (*-en*).

### ❌ Mistake 2: English word order (putting verbs together)
* ❌ *Ich muss anrufen meinen Arzt heute.*
* ✅ *Ich **muss** heute meinen Arzt **anrufen**.*
* **Why:** In German main clauses, the second verb must go to the very end of the clause.

### ❌ Mistake 3: Adding "-t" to the *er/sie/es* form
* ❌ *Er kannt gut kochen.* / *Er musst arbeiten.*
* ✅ *Er **kann** gut kochen.* / *Er **muss** arbeiten.*
* **Why:** For modal verbs, the third person singular (*er/sie/es*) is always identical to *ich* with zero ending.

### ❌ Mistake 4: Using *will* for the English future
* ❌ *Ich will morgen anrufen.* (meant: "I will call tomorrow")
* ✅ *Ich **rufe** morgen **an**.*
* **Why:** *will* is from *wollen* (= want). It sounds like a strong wish, not a simple future.

### ❌ Mistake 5: Confusing "nicht müssen" with "nicht dürfen"
* ❌ *Du musst hier nicht parken!* (Intending: Parking is forbidden!)
* ✅ *Hier **darfst** du nicht parken!*
* **Why:** *Nicht müssen* means optional ("you don't have to"). *Nicht dürfen* means forbidden ("you are not allowed to").

## 7. Real-Life Germany

### Where You Will See and Hear This Every Day:

#### 1. Prohibitions & Signs (*dürfen*)
In Germany, signs often use short nouns, but spoken language uses *dürfen*:
* Sign: **Rauchen verboten** *(Smoking forbidden)*
  * Spoken: *„Hier **darf** man nicht **rauchen**.“*
* Sign: **Betreten verboten** *(Do not enter / trespassing prohibited)*
  * Spoken: *„Hier **darf** man nicht **hineingehen**.“*

#### 2. Doctor's Instructions (*sollen*)
When a doctor or pharmacist explains a prescription:
* The doctor says: *„Nehmen Sie die Tablette einmal täglich.“* – You tell your partner: *„Ich **soll** die Tablette einmal täglich **nehmen**.“* (I'm supposed to take the pill once a day.)
* *„Der Arzt sagt, ich **soll** zwei Tage im Bett **bleiben**.“* (The doctor says I should stay in bed for two days.)

#### 3. Service Interactions & Customer Service (*möchten*)
* In a bakery: *„Guten Tag, ich **möchte** bitte drei Brötchen.“*
* On the phone: *„Guten Tag, mein Name ist Weber. Ich **möchte** bitte Herrn Müller **sprechen**.“*
