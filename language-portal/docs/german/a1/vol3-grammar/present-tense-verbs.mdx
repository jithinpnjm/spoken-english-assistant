# 3.4 Present Tense Verbs (Verben im Präsens)

To talk about what you do, what you are doing, or even what you *will* do, you need the present tense. German verbs change their endings depending on who is doing the action (I, you, he, we).

---

## 1. At the Office: Talking about the Weekend

Imagine you are standing at the coffee machine on Monday morning. You and your colleague are talking about what you normally do on the weekend.

**Anna:** Was **machst** du am Wochenende, Max? *(What do you do on the weekend, Max?)*
**Max:** Ich **spiele** am Samstag Fußball. Und du? Was **machst** du? *(I play football on Saturday. And you? What do you do?)*
**Anna:** Ich **arbeite** meistens am Samstag. Aber am Sonntag **schlafe** ich lange. *(I mostly work on Saturday. But on Sunday I sleep late.)*
**Max:** **Fährst** du am Sonntag zu deinen Eltern? *(Are you driving to your parents on Sunday?)*
**Anna:** Nein, wir **bleiben** zu Hause. *(No, we are staying home.)*

Notice how the verbs change depending on who is speaking: *ich spiele*, but *wir bleiben*.

---

## 2. How German Verbs Work

Almost every German verb in the dictionary (the **Infinitive**) ends in **-en** or **-n** (e.g., *spielen, machen, kommen*).

To use the verb, you chop off the *-en*. What is left is called the **Stem**.
*   *spielen* (to play) -> Stem: **spiel-**
*   *machen* (to do/make) -> Stem: **mach-**

You then add a specific ending to the stem based on the pronoun. 
*(Memorize this pattern: **e, st, t, en, t, en**)*.

| Pronoun | Ending | Example: *spielen* (to play) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ich** | **-e** | ich spiel**e** |
| **du** | **-st** | du spiel**st** |
| **er/sie/es** | **-t** | er spiel**t** |
| **wir** | **-en** | wir spiel**en** |
| **ihr** | **-t** | ihr spiel**t** |
| **sie/Sie** | **-en** | sie spiel**en** |

---

## 3. The Two Big Exceptions

Of course, language is never perfectly regular. There are two common exceptions you need to know for daily life.

### Exception A: The "d/t" Rule (Adding an extra 'e')
If the verb stem ends in a **-d** or **-t** (like *arbeiten* -> *arbeit-*), it is physically hard to pronounce the normal *-st* or *-t* endings. 
So, German adds an extra **-e-** for *du, er/sie/es*, and *ihr*!
*   *arbeiten* (to work): du arbeit**e**st, er arbeit**e**t, ihr arbeit**e**t.
*   *finden* (to find): du find**e**st, er find**e**t.

### Exception B: The Vowel Change (Strong Verbs)
Some very common verbs change the vowel inside their stem. **This only happens for "du" and "er/sie/es"!**

*   **e changes to i (or ie)**
    *   **sprechen** (to speak): ich spreche, du spr**i**chst, er spr**i**cht, wir sprechen.
    *   **essen** (to eat): ich esse, du **i**sst, er **i**sst, wir essen.
    *   **lesen** (to read): ich lese, du l**ie**st, er l**ie**st, wir lesen.
*   **a changes to ä**
    *   **fahren** (to drive): ich fahre, du f**ä**hrst, er f**ä**hrt, wir fahren.
    *   **schlafen** (to sleep): ich schlafe, du schl**ä**fst, er schl**ä**ft, wir schlafen.

---

## 4. The Present Tense as the Future

In English, if you talk about tomorrow, you have to use the future tense: "I *will fly* to Berlin tomorrow." 
In German, you can simply use the present tense, as long as you include a "time word" (like tomorrow, next week, later).
*   *Ich **fliege** morgen nach Berlin.* (I am flying / will fly to Berlin tomorrow.)
*   *Wir **spielen** am Freitag Tennis.* (We will play tennis on Friday.)
