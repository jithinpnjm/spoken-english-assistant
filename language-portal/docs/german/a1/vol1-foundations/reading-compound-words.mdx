# 1.2 Reading German Words & Compound Nouns (Komposita)

## 🎯 What you will learn
* I understand how German joins smaller words together to create long words (*Komposita*).
* I can use the "Right-to-Left" decoding rule to find the core meaning of any compound noun.
* I know how to find the gender (*der, die, das*) of a compound word instantly.
* I understand why an extra letter (like *-s-* or *-n-*) sometimes connects the parts.
* I can decode everyday signs and notices at train stations and supermarkets.

---

## 1. What are Compound Words and Why Does German Love Them?

When English speakers want to describe a new object, they usually put separate words side by side with spaces:
* *apple juice*
* *train station*
* *front door key*

**German does something different: it glues words together into a single word.** These are called **Compound Nouns (Komposita)**.

Instead of writing three separate words with spaces, German builds one solid word:
* *Apfel* (apple) + *Saft* (juice) = **der Apfelsaft**
* *Haus* (house) + *Tür* (door) + *Schlüssel* (key) = **der Haustürschlüssel**

When you see a 20-letter word on a street sign or form, don't feel overwhelmed. You are not looking at one impossible new word; you are looking at two or three simple words holding hands.

---

## 2. The Two Golden Rules for Decoding

### Rule 1: Read from Right to Left for the Core Meaning
The most important part of any German compound noun is the **very last word** (called the *Grundwort* or base word). 
The last word tells you **what the thing fundamentally is**. All the words before it merely describe *what kind* of thing it is.

Look at these examples:
* **der Orangensaft:**
  * Last word: **der Saft** (the juice) ➔ The object is a *juice*.
  * First word: **die Orange** (the orange) ➔ It describes what flavor of juice.
  * Meaning: *orange juice*.
* **das Schlafzimmer:**
  * Last word: **das Zimmer** (the room) ➔ The object is a *room*.
  * First word: **schlafen** (to sleep) ➔ It describes what happens in the room.
  * Meaning: *bedroom*.

### Rule 2: The Last Word Dictates the Gender
German has three genders: masculine (*der*), feminine (*die*), and neutral (*das*).
In a compound noun, **only the gender of the last word counts**:
* *das Haus* (neutral) + **die Tür** (feminine) = **die Haustür** (the front door)
* *die Hand* (feminine) + **der Schuh** (masculine) = **der Handschuh** (the glove — literally "hand-shoe"!)
* *der Wein* (masculine) + **das Glas** (neutral) = **das Weinglas** (the wine glass)

You almost never have to guess the gender of a 15-letter word; you only need to know the gender of the final small word. (Rare exceptions exist, e.g. *der Mittwoch* although *die Woche* – learn those when you meet them.)

---

## 3. The "Glue Letters" (Fugenelemente)

Sometimes when two words connect, German adds a connector letter between them to make pronunciation smooth. You will see this constantly:

1. **The connecting "-s-":**
   * *die Geburt* (birth) + **-s-** + *der Tag* (day) = **der Geburtstag** (birthday)
   * *die Arbeit* (work) + **-s-** + *der Platz* (place) = **der Arbeitsplatz** (workplace)
2. **The connecting "-n-" or "-en-":**
   * *die Suppe* (soup) + **-n-** + *der Löffel* (spoon) = **der Suppenlöffel** (soup spoon)
   * *die Straße* (street) + **-n-** + *die Bahn* (railway) = **die Straßenbahn** (tram)

Do not let these extra letters confuse your reading. Simply look past the *-s-* or *-n-* to spot the root words!

## 4. Everyday Words You Already Know How to Read

Here is how common everyday German words are constructed:

| Compound Word | Broken Down | Literal Meaning | English Translation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **der Kühlschrank** | *kühl* (cool) + *der Schrank* (cupboard) | "cool-cupboard" | refrigerator / fridge |
| **der Kindergarten** | *die Kinder* (children) + *der Garten* (garden) | "children-garden" | kindergarten |
| **der Hauptbahnhof** | *Haupt* (main) + *die Bahn* (rail) + *der Hof* (court) | "main-train-station" | central station |
| **die Fahrkarte** | *fahren* (to travel) + *die Karte* (card/ticket) | "travel-card" | ticket |
| **das Krankenhaus** | *krank* (sick) + *das Haus* (house) | "sick-house" | hospital |
| **der Hausmeister** | *das Haus* (house) + *der Meister* (master) | "house-master" | building manager / caretaker |

## 5. Real-Life Germany: Navigating the Train Station (DB)

When you walk into a German train station (*Bahnhof*), almost every sign is a compound word. Once you know the parts, navigating is effortless:

* **der Fahrkartenautomat:**
  * *fahren* (travel) + *die Karte* (ticket) + *der Automat* (machine)
  * ➔ **Ticket vending machine**
* **der Informationsschalter:**
  * *die Information* (info) + *der Schalter* (counter)
  * ➔ **Information desk**
* **die Abfahrtstafel:**
  * *abfahren* (depart) + *die Tafel* (board)
  * ➔ **Departure board**
* **der Wartesaal:**
  * *warten* (wait) + *der Saal* (hall/room)
  * ➔ **Waiting room**
