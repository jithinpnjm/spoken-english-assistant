# 1.1 The German Alphabet & Pronunciation (Alphabet und Aussprache)

## 🎯 What you will learn
* I can say the German alphabet from A to Z with correct letter names.
* I can pronounce the four special German characters: **ä, ö, ü**, and **ß**.
* I can distinguish the most common sound pairs (*ei* vs. *ie*, *w* vs. *v*).
* I can spell my first and last name out loud in German.

---

## 1. Why German Pronunciation is Friendlier than English

In English, the same letters can make wildly different sounds (compare *th**ou**gh*, *thr**ou**gh*, *t**ou**gh*, and *th**ou**ght*). 

**German is fundamentally different: it is largely phonetic.** 
Once you learn the sound attached to a letter or letter combination, it almost always makes that exact sound. When you see a new word on a street sign or in a letter, you can usually pronounce it correctly on your very first try.

German uses the same 26 Latin letters as English, plus **four unique characters**:
* Three **Umlauts** (vowels with two dots above them): **ä**, **ö**, **ü**
* One unique sharp S: **ß** (called *Eszett* or *scharfes S*)

## 2. The Alphabet & Letter Names

In Germany, you will frequently need to spell your name, address, or email address on the phone. To do that, you must know how each letter is named in German:

| Letter | German Name | Sound Description | Everyday Word |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A a** | [aː] "Ah" | Open open "ah" | **der Apfel** (apple) |
| **B b** | [beː] "Bay" | Standard b | **die Bahn** (train) |
| **C c** | [tseː] "Tsay" | Before e/i sounds like *ts* | **das Café** (café) |
| **D d** | [deː] "Day" | Standard d | **danke** (thank you) |

| **H h** | [haː] "Hah" | Breathy h at start; silent in middle | **das Haus** (house) |
| **I i** | [iː] "Ee" | Long "ee" sound | **der Igel** (hedgehog) |
| **J j** | [jɔt] "Yott" | Always sounds like English **Y** | **ja** (yes) |

| **P p** | [peː] "Pay" | Standard p | **die Post** (post office) |

| **Y y** | [ˈʏpsilɔn] "Üpsilon" | Usually sounds like German ü (*der Typ*); at the start of loanwords often like *j* (*das Yoga*) | **der Typ** (type; guy) |
| **Z z** | [tsɛt] "Tsett" | **Always** sounds like **TS** in *cats* | **der Zug** (train) |

## 3. The 4 Special Characters (Umlauts & ß)

Beginners often treat Umlauts as decorative dots. **They are completely distinct letters with distinct sounds.** Leaving off an Umlaut changes the word entirely (for example, *schon* means "already", but *schön* means "beautiful")!

### 1. The Letter **Ä / ä**
* **How to make the sound:** Open your mouth slightly wider than for a short "e" (like the vowel in English *bed* or *melon*).
* **Examples:**
  * *der Bär* (bear)
  * *die Äpfel* (apples, plural)
  * *spät* (late)

### 2. The Letter **Ö / ö**
* **How to make the sound:** Shape your lips into an "O" shape, but try to say the vowel sound "E". Do not move your lips while making the sound.
* **Examples:**
  * *schön* (beautiful)
  * *öffnen* (to open)
  * *Köln* (Cologne)

### 3. The Letter **Ü / ü**
* **How to make the sound:** Shape your lips tightly as if you are about to whistle, and make the sound "EE".
* **Examples:**
  * *fünf* (five)
  * *über* (over / about)
  * *die Tür* (the door)

### 4. The Letter **ß** (Eszett / scharfes S)
* **What it is:** A sharp unvoiced "S" sound (like the *ss* in *lesson*).
* **The rule:** It never starts a word. It comes after a long vowel or a diphthong (*au, ei, eu*): *Straße, heißen*. After a short vowel you write *ss*: *essen, Wasser*. (In Switzerland, *ß* is not used at all.)
* **Examples:**
  * *groß* (big)
  * *die Straße* (street)
  * *heißen* (to be named)

## 4. Key Letter Combinations: The Rules You Must Know

### A. The Classic Beginner Trap: *ei* vs. *ie*
This is one of the most common pronunciation mistakes English speakers make:
* **ei** sounds like the English word **"eye"** (or *my*, *pie*):
  * *mein* (my)
  * *zwei* (two)
  * *Nein* (no)
* **ie** sounds like a long **"ee"** (like *see*, *tree*):
  * *sie* (she / they)
  * *vier* (four)
  * *das Bier* (beer)

*Memory trick:* Look at the **second** letter. In *ei*, the second letter is *i* ("eye"). In *ie*, the second letter is *e* ("ee").

### B. Other Double Vowels (Diphthongs)
* **eu** and **äu** both make the sound **"oy"** (like in *boy*):
  * *der Euro* (euro)
  * *heute* (today)
  * *die Häuser* (houses)
* **au** makes the sound **"ow"** (like in *cow*):
  * *das Haus* (house)
  * *das Auto* (car)

### C. Consonant Rules
* **w** always sounds like an English **V**:
  * *das Wetter* (the weather)
  * *wohnen* (to live)
* **v** almost always sounds like an English **F** in native German words:
  * *der Vater* (the father)
  * *vier* (four)
  * *viel* (much / a lot)
* **z** always sounds like **"ts"** in *cats*:
  * *die Zeit* (time)
  * *zehn* (ten)
  * *das Zimmer* (room)
* **sp** and **st** at the start of a word sound like **shp** and **sht**:
  * *der Sport* (sports)
  * *die Stadt* (city)
  * *stehen* (to stand)
* **sch** sounds like **"sh"** in *shoe*:
  * *die Schule* (school)
  * *schnell* (fast)
* **ch** has two distinct sounds:
  * **The soft "Ich-Laut" (hissing like a cat):** After *e, i, ä, ö, ü*, or consonants (*l, n, r*):
    * *ich* (I), *die Milch* (milk), *sprechen* (to speak), *das Mädchen* (girl)
  * **The throaty "Ach-Laut" (from the back of the throat):** After *a, o, u, au*:
    * *das Buch* (book), *machen* (to make), *kochen* (to cook), *auch* (also)
  * **Two extras you will meet at A1:** *chs* usually sounds like **ks** (*sechs, wachsen*), and a word ending in **-ig**
    is usually said like *-ich* in standard German (*zwanzig, billig, richtig*; in the south you will also hear a *k*).
* **Final b, d, g sound hard (p, t, k)** at the end of a word or syllable: *der Tag* sounds like "tahk", *das Kind* like
  "kint", *halb* like "halp". Spelling keeps the letter because the sound comes back in other forms (*die Tage*).

## 5. Real-Life Germany: The German Spelling Alphabet

When you call an office, clinic, or utility provider in Germany, you will regularly hear:
> *„Können Sie Ihren Namen bitte buchstabieren?“* (Can you please spell your name?)

Because letters like *M/N* or *B/P* are easy to confuse on the phone, people use a **spelling alphabet** (*Buchstabiertafel*). Many Germans still use the traditional **name** version below. Since 2022 the official standard (DIN 5009) uses **city names** instead (A wie Aachen, B wie Berlin, C wie Chemnitz, D wie Düsseldorf, E wie Essen …). Either version is understood – the important thing is to say *„… wie …“* clearly.

| Letter | Word | Letter | Word |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A** | Anton | **N** | Nordpol |
| **B** | Berta | **O** | Otto |
| **C** | Cäsar | **P** | Paula |
| **D** | Dora | **Q** | Quelle |
| **E** | Emil | **R** | Richard |
| **F** | Friedrich | **S** | Samuel |
| **G** | Gustav | **T** | Theodor |
| **H** | Heinrich | **U** | Ulrich |
| **I** | Ida | **V** | Viktor |
| **J** | Julius | **W** | Wilhelm |
| **K** | Kaufmann | **X** | Xanthippe |
| **L** | Ludwig | **Y** | Ypsilon |
| **M** | Martha | **Z** | Zacharias |
| **Ä** | Ärger | **Ö** | Ökonom |
| **Ü** | Übermut | **ß** | Eszett |

*Example:* If your last name is **Müller**, on the phone you can say:
> *„M wie Martha, Ü wie Übermut, L wie Ludwig, L wie Ludwig, E wie Emil, R wie Richard.“*  
> City version: *„M wie München, Umlaut-U wie Unna, L wie Leipzig, L wie Leipzig, E wie Essen, R wie Rostock.“*

### Spelling tricks you will need on the phone
* **Double letters:** Germans often say *„Doppel-L“*, *„Doppel-S“* (for *Müller*: *M – Ü – Doppel-L – E – R*).
* **Umlauts:** say *„U mit Punkten“* or *„Umlaut-U“* if the other person is unsure; in forms and emails *ü* can be written
  *ue* (*Mueller*) – but on official documents use the spelling in your passport.
* **ß:** say *„Eszett“* or *„scharfes S“*.
* **Email addresses:** *@* = *„at“* (some people say *„Klammeraffe“*), *.* = *„Punkt“*, *-* = *„Bindestrich“*,
  *_* = *„Unterstrich“*, all small letters = *„alles klein“*.
  *jithin.joseph@web.de* → *„j-i-t-h-i-n Punkt j-o-s-e-p-h at web Punkt d-e, alles klein“*.
