# D. Pronunciation & Spelling Coach (Aussprache und Buchstabieren)

Welcome to your **German Pronunciation & Spelling Repair Workshop**! You first met the 30 characters of the German alphabet in [Chapter 1: Basics — Alphabet & Sounds](../a1/book/chapter-01-basics-alphabet-greetings.mdx), and you will use spoken German every single day in the [Survival Kit (Chapter 0)](../a1/book/chapter-00-survival-kit.mdx), on the telephone in [Chapter 15: Letters, Emails & Telephone Masterclass](../a1/book/chapter-15-letters-emails-telephone-masterclass.mdx), and in your [Goethe A1 Sprechen](../a1/exam-center/goethe-sprechen.mdx) and [telc A1 Speaking](../a1/exam-center/telc-writing-speaking.mdx) exams.

Here is the good news right at the start: **you do not need a "perfect native accent" to succeed in Germany or pass your A1 exam with top marks.** What you need is **clear, comfortable intelligibility**—knowing which specific sounds change the meaning of a word (like *vier* = 4 vs. *für* = for, or *Wein* = wine vs. *Wien* = Vienna) and how to spell your name, street, and email address over the phone without a single misunderstanding.

In this hands-on guide, we use **zero confusing dictionary symbols** and **zero academic jargon**. Every single sound is explained in plain everyday English using simple mouth, tongue, and lip positions you already know how to make.

## 1. The "Priority Five" Sounds for English Speakers

When English speakers speak German, about **80% of all misunderstandings** come from just **five sound groups**. Fix these five, and German listeners—at the bakery, at the *Bürgeramt*, at work, and in the exam room—will immediately understand you much more easily.

### Summary Table: The Priority Five at a Glance

| Priority Sound | Why It Matters in Real Life | What to Do With Your Mouth, Tongue & Lips | Classic Minimal Pairs (Say Both!) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. The Umlauts: ü, ö, ä** | Changes word meaning and marks plurals (*Bruder ➔ Brüder*, *schon ➔ schön*, *vier ➔ für*). | Keep your tongue in the front vowel position (**ee** or **eh**), then round your lips firmly in front. | *vier – für*, *Kiste – Küste*, *kennen – können*, *schon – schön*, *Meere – Möhre* |
| **2. Soft `ch` (Front Hiss)** | Used in ultra-common A1 words (*ich, mich, dich, nicht, Milch, Kirche, zwanzig, richtig*). | Smile slightly, press tongue tip behind lower teeth, hiss air gently over the middle of the tongue (like the **h** in *huge* or *human*). | *Kirche – Kirsche*, *ich – isch*, *dich – Tisch* |
| **3. Throat `ch` (Back Scrape)** | Used after dark vowels **a, o, u, au** (*acht, Nacht, nach, Buch, kochen, auch, Bauch*). | Keep your mouth open and make a gentle, airy throat-clearing sound at the back of the roof of your mouth. | *Nacht – nackt*, *nach – Nacken*, *Buch – Bock* |
| **4. `ei` vs. `ie`** | Confusing these turns *Wein* (wine) into *Wien* (Vienna) and *leider* (unfortunately) into *Lieder* (songs). | Look at the **second letter**! **e-i** sounds like English **"eye"**; **i-e** sounds like a long English **"ee"**. | *Wein – Wien*, *leider – Lieder*, *mein – Miete*, *Bein – Biene* |
| **5. Long vs. Short Vowels** | Changes completely different words (*Miete* = rent vs. *Mitte* = center; *Staat* = state vs. *Stadt* = city). | **1 consonant / silent h / ie** = hold the vowel out smoothly. **2+ consonants** = clip the vowel short and crisp. | *Miete – Mitte*, *Staat – Stadt*, *ihn – in*, *wen – wenn* |

### Deep Dive 1A: How to Pronounce `ü`, `ö`, and `ä` Step by Step

Many beginners think *ü* and *ö* are mysterious German sounds that do not exist in English. In reality, **your tongue already knows the exact position**—you just have to combine an English tongue position with an English lip position!

#### How to Make `ü` (U-Umlaut) in 3 Seconds
1. **Step 1 (Tongue):** Smile wide and say a clear, long English **"eeee"** (as in *see*, *meet*, or *green*). Notice how the middle of your tongue rises high toward the front roof of your mouth, and the tip of your tongue touches right behind your bottom front teeth.
2. **Step 2 (Freeze the tongue):** Lock your tongue in that exact **"eeee"** spot. Do not let your tongue move backward!
3. **Step 3 (Round the lips):** While you are still saying **"eeee"**, push your lips tightly forward into a small round circle, as if you are about to whistle or blow out a birthday candle.
4. **Result:** Out comes a pure, crystal-clear German **ü**!

* **❌ Wrong:** Saying *"fyoor"* or *"foo"* for **für** (gliding from *y* to *oo* moves your tongue backward).
* **✅ Right:** Keep the tongue locked on **"ee"** with whistling lips: **für** (for), **fünf** (five), **über** (over / about), **die Tür** (the door), **die Küche** (the kitchen), **natürlich** (of course), **pünktlich** (on time), **zurück** (back).

<GermanAudio text="vier, für. Kiste, Küste. Miete, müde. die Tür, fünf, grün, natürlich, pünktlich." label="Listen & Repeat: ee vs. ü (vier – für, Kiste – Küste)" />

#### How to Make `ö` (O-Umlaut) in 3 Seconds
1. **Step 1 (Tongue):** Say a clean, steady **"ay / eh"** sound (like the start of *day* or the vowel in *bed*), keeping your tongue forward in your mouth.
2. **Step 2 (Freeze the tongue):** Hold your tongue completely still in that front position. Do **not** curl your tongue backward like an American English **"r"** (never curl the tongue as in *bird* or *her*).
3. **Step 3 (Round the lips):** Round your lips forward into a smooth **"oh"** shape while still voicing that front **"eh"** sound.
4. **Result:** Out comes a warm, smooth German **ö**!

* **❌ Wrong:** Saying *"shon"* (which means **schon** = already!) or *"shern"* with an English R when you want to say **schön** (beautiful / nice).
* **✅ Right:** Front tongue + rounded lips, zero R-curl: **schön** (nice / beautiful), **können** (can / to be able to), **zwölf** (twelve), **das Brötchen** (the bread roll), **hören** (to hear / listen), **öffnen** (to open), **der Löffel** (the spoon).

<GermanAudio text="schon, schön. kennen, können. Meere, Möhre. zwölf, das Brötchen, hören, öffnen." label="Listen & Repeat: o/e vs. ö (schon – schön, kennen – können)" />

#### How to Make `ä` (A-Umlaut) in 1 Second
Good news: **ä** is the easiest Umlaut of all!
* **Short `ä`** (before two consonants, like **Männer** = men, **Äpfel** = apples, **Bäcker** = baker, **kälter** = colder) sounds **100% identical** to a short German **e** (like the *e* in English *bed* or *men*).
* **Long `ä`** (before a single consonant or silent *h*, like **spät** = late, **wählen** = to choose/dial, **der Käse** = the cheese, **das Mädchen** = the girl) sounds like the open **"air"** vowel in English *care* or *bear*—just stretch the **"eh"** sound smoothly without curling your tongue into an R.

<GermanAudio text="der Mann, die Männer. der Apfel, die Äpfel. spät, der Käse, das Mädchen, die Bäckerei." label="Listen & Repeat: ä in Everyday Words" />

### Deep Dive 1B: The Two German `ch` Sounds (Front Hiss vs. Back Scrape)

In German spelling, the letters **ch** represent two sibling sounds—and neither of them is the English *"ch"* in *church* or *chair*! (To write the English *church* sound, German uses four letters: **tsch**, as in **Deutsch** or **Tschüss**.)

How do you know which of the two German **ch** sounds to use? **Just look at the vowel right before `ch`!**

#### 1. Soft Front `ch` (After Front Vowels `i, e, ä, ö, ü, ei, eu/äu` and Consonants `l, n, r` + in `-chen`)
Whenever **ch** comes after a bright front vowel (**i, e, ä, ö, ü, ei, eu, äu**), after a consonant (**l, n, r**), or in the little diminutive ending **-chen** (*Mädchen, Brötchen*), your tongue is already at the front of your mouth.
* **How to make it:** Say the English word **"huge"** or **"human"** very slowly, and freeze right on the initial **H-Y** sound before you say *-uge*. Feel how the air hisses softly over the raised middle of your tongue while the tip of your tongue rests gently behind your bottom front teeth? That gentle cat-like hiss is the exact German soft **ch**!
* **Watch out for the two classic traps:**
  * **Trap A (Turning `ch` into `k`):** Saying *"ik"* instead of **ich**. Keep a small gap between your tongue and the roof of your mouth so the air keeps flowing!
  * **Trap B (Turning `ch` into `sch` / "sh"):** Saying *"isch"* instead of **ich**, or mixing up **die Kirche** (the church — soft *ch* with flat lips) and **die Kirsche** (the cherry — *sch* with pushed-out "sh" lips).

| Word with Soft `ch` | Meaning | Sound Guide | Contrast Pair |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ich / mich / dich** | I / me / you | Soft front hiss after *i* | Not *"ik"* and not *"isch"* |
| **nicht / nichts** | not / nothing | Soft front hiss + crisp *t* | **nicht** (not) vs. **Nacht** (night — throat *ch*) |
| **sprechen / echt / rechts** | to speak / really / right | Soft front hiss after *e* | Smooth air hiss before *t* |
| **die Küche / die Bücher** | the kitchen / the books | Round lips for *ü*, then soft front hiss | Compare **das Buch** (singular = throat *ch*!) vs. **die Bücher** (plural = soft *ch*!) |
| **die Milch / welcher** | the milk / which | Soft front hiss after *l* | Never *"Milk"* with a K! |
| **die Kirche** | the church | Soft front hiss (keep lips flat/smiling) | **die Kirsche** (the cherry — round lips for *"sh"*) |
| **das Brötchen / das Mädchen** | the bread roll / the girl | Always soft front hiss in **-chen** | *Bröt-chen*, *Mäd-chen* |

<GermanAudio text="ich, mich, dich, nicht, sprechen, die Milch, die Küche, das Brötchen, die Kirche, die Kirsche." label="Listen & Repeat: Soft Front ch (Plus Kirche vs. Kirsche)" />

#### 2. Back Throat `ch` (After Dark Back Vowels `a, o, u, au`)
Whenever **ch** comes after one of the four dark back vowels—**a, o, u, au**—your tongue is already resting low at the back of your mouth.
* **How to make it:** Open your mouth as if saying *"ah"*, keep your tongue low, and gently breathe air past the back of your throat—like fogging up a pair of glasses with a slightly rougher throat sound, or like the Scottish word *Loch*. Do not scrape hard or hurt your throat; keep it light and airy!
* **Memory Trick ("The 4 Dark Vowels"):** Remember the phrase **„Auch Buch-Nacht-Kochen“** (**au, u, a, o**). After **a, o, u, au**, always use the back throat **ch**!

| Vowel Before `ch` | Everyday A1 Examples | English Meaning | Minimal Pair / Contrast |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| After **a** | **acht, die Nacht, nach, machen, lachen** | eight, the night, to/after, to make/do, to laugh | **die Nacht** (night) vs. **nach** (after/to) vs. **nackt** (naked — with *ck*) |
| After **o** | **kochen, die Woche, noch, die Tochter, doch** | to cook, the week, still/yet, the daughter, yes (after negation) | **kochen** (to cook — airy throat *ch*) |
| After **u** | **das Buch, suchen, der Kuchen, der Besuch** | the book, to look for, the cake, the visit | **das Buch** (throat *ch*) vs. **die Bücher** (soft front *ch*) |
| After **au** | **auch, der Bauch, rauchen, brauchen** | also, the stomach, to smoke, to need | **auch** (also) vs. **Auge** (eye — hard *g*) |

<GermanAudio text="acht, die Nacht, nach, machen, die Woche, noch, das Buch, suchen, der Kuchen, auch, brauchen." label="Listen & Repeat: Back Throat ch After a, o, u, au" />

### Deep Dive 1C: `ei` vs. `ie` — The Second-Letter Rule

In English, words with *ei* and *ie* are notoriously chaotic (*receive, believe, friend, height, weight*). In German, **`ei` and `ie` follow a 100% unbreakable rule**:

> **Look at the SECOND letter and say its English name!**
> * In **e-i**, the second letter is **I** ➔ pronounced like English **"eye"** (*mein, dein, sein, nein, eins, zwei, drei, Wein, Zeit, arbeiten, heißen, schreiben*).
> * In **i-e**, the second letter is **E** ➔ pronounced like a long English **"ee"** (*sie, wir, vier, sieben, hier, Bier, Wien, Miete, lieben, spielen, studieren*).

Let's practice the classic minimal pairs where flipping **ei** and **ie** turns your sentence into a completely different word:

| `ei` = "eye" | Meaning | `ie` = long "ee" | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **der Wein** | the wine | **Wien** | Vienna |
| **leider** | unfortunately | **die Lieder** | the songs |
| **das Bein** | the leg | **die Biene** | the bee |
| **reisen** | to travel | **der Riese** | the giant |
| **frei** | free | **der Frieden** | the peace |
| **schreiben** | to write | **geschrieben** | written |

* **❌ Wrong:** *„Ich trinke gern **Wien** in **Wein**.“* (I like drinking Vienna in wine!)
* **✅ Right:** *„Ich trinke gern **Wein** in **Wien**.“* (I like drinking wine in Vienna!)
* **❌ Wrong:** *„Ich habe **Lieder** keine Zeit.“* (I songs have no time!)
* **✅ Right:** *„Ich habe **leider** keine Zeit.“* (I unfortunately have no time!)

<GermanAudio text="der Wein, Wien. leider, die Lieder. das Bein, die Biene. mein, die Miete. schreiben, spielen." label="Listen & Repeat: ei ('eye') vs. ie ('ee')" />

## 2. Every Other Tricky German Sound Explained Simply

Once you have the Priority Five under your belt, let's walk through the remaining German consonant and vowel combinations so that no word in [Chapters 1 to 17](../a1/book/chapter-01-basics-alphabet-greetings.mdx) ever catches you off guard.

### 2.1 `z` = Crisp `ts` (Never English "z"!)
In German, the letter **z** (and the combination **tz**) is **always** pronounced like a sharp **"ts"**—the exact sound at the end of the English word *ca**ts*** or in the middle of *pi**zz**a*.
* Even at the very beginning of a word, put a crisp silent **t** right before the **s**:
  * **zehn** (*tsayn* — 10), **zwanzig** (*tsvan-tsich* — 20), **zwölf** (*tsvölff* — 12)
  * **die Zeit** (*tsayt* — time), **das Zimmer** (*tsim-mer* — room), **der Zug** (*tsook* — train)
  * **zahlen / bezahlen** (*tsah-len / be-tsah-len* — to pay), **zusammen** (*tsoo-zam-men* — together)
  * **der Platz** (*plats* — place/seat/square), **die Katze** (*kat-se* — cat), **jetzt** (*yetst* — now)

<GermanAudio text="zehn, zwölf, zwanzig, die Zeit, das Zimmer, der Zug, bezahlen, zusammen, der Platz, jetzt." label="Listen & Repeat: German z and tz = crisp 'ts'" />

### 2.2 `w` = English `v` vs. `v` = English `f`
If your native language or variety of English blends **v** and **w** together, pay extra attention here!
* **German `w`** is always made by resting your **top front teeth lightly on your bottom lip** and buzzing gently—just like English **V** in *very* or *voice*:
  * **das Wasser** (*vas-ser* — water), **wohnen** (*voh-nen* — to live), **wir** (*veer* — we), **wo / wann / wer / wie / warum** (*vo / van / vair / vee / va-room*), **der Wein** (*vayn* — wine), **wichtig** (*vich-tich* — important).
* **German `v`** in native German words is voiceless—it sounds **100% like an English `f`** in *father* or *fish*:
  * **der Vater** (*fah-ter* — father), **vier** (*feer* — four), **viel / viele** (*feel / fee-le* — much / many), **von** (*fon* — from), **vor** (*for* — before / in front of), **verstehen** (*fer-shtay-en* — to understand), **der Vogel** (*foh-gel* — bird).
  * *(Exception in international loanwords only: in foreign words like **die Vase**, **das Visum**, **der Pullover**, **der November**, **vegetarisch**, `v` sounds like English `v`.)*

* **Practice Contrast (Teeth on lip with voice = `w` vs. Teeth on lip with air only = `f / v`):**
  * **wir** (we — *veer*) vs. **vier** (four — *feer*)
  * **der Wein** (the wine — *vayn*) vs. **fein** (fine — *fayn*)
  * **die Wand** (the wall — *vant*) vs. **fand** (found — *fant*)

<GermanAudio text="wir, vier. der Wein, fein. das Wasser, der Vater, wohnen, viel, verstehen, wichtig." label="Listen & Repeat: w ('v') vs. v/f ('f')" />

### 2.3 `s` Before a Vowel = Buzzing English `z` (and `ß` / `ss` = Sharp `s`)
In German, the letter **s** changes its sound depending on where it sits:
1. **`s` at the start of a word or syllable before a vowel (`sa-, se-, si-, so-, su-`):** Buzzes softly like an English **Z** in *zoo* or *zero*!
   * **sieben** (*zee-ben* — 7), **die Sonne** (*zon-ne* — sun), **der Salat** (*za-laht* — salad), **sehen** (*zay-en* — to see), **sagen** (*zah-gen* — to say), **suchen** (*zoo-khen* — to look for), **die Suppe** (*zoop-pe* — soup), **langsam** (*lang-zahm* — slow), **lesen** (*lay-zen* — to read).
2. **`ss`, `ß`, or `s` at the end of a word:** Always a crisp, unvoiced **hissing S** (like English *bus* or *glass*)!
   * **das Haus** (*hows*), **der Bus** (*boos*), **essen** (*es-sen* — short vowel before *ss*), **die Straße** (*shtrah-se* — long vowel before *ß*), **groß** (*grohs* — big/tall), **heißen** (*hay-sen* — to be called).

<GermanAudio text="sieben, die Sonne, sagen, lesen, langsam. das Haus, essen, die Straße, groß, heißen." label="Listen & Repeat: Voiced s ('z') vs. Sharp ss / ß ('s')" />

### 2.4 `st` = `sht` and `sp` = `shp` (At the Start of a Word or Stem)
Whenever **st** or **sp** stands at the **beginning of a word** (or at the beginning of the main root inside a compound word like *ver**st**ehen* or *Beispiel*), Germans pronounce the **s** as **"sh"** (*sch*):
* **`st-` = `sht-`:** **die Straße** (*shtrah-se* — street), **die Stadt** (*shtat* — city), **der Bahnhof / die Haltestelle** (*hal-te-shtel-le* — stop), **der Stuhl** (*shtool* — chair), **die Stunde** (*shtoon-de* — hour), **studieren** (*shtoo-dee-ren* — to study), **stehen / verstehen** (*shtay-en / fer-shtay-en* — to stand / understand).
* **`sp-` = `shp-`:** **sprechen** (*shpre-chen* — to speak), **spielen** (*shpee-len* — to play), **der Sport** (*shport* — sport), **spät** (*shpayt* — late), **sparen** (*shpah-ren* — to save), **die Speisekarte** (*shpay-ze-kar-te* — menu).
* *(Note: If **st** or **sp** is in the middle or at the end of a syllable—like **ist**, **bist**, **das Fest**, **der Gast**, **die Post**, **fast**—it is pronounced as a normal crisp **st**, NOT "sht"!)*

<GermanAudio text="die Straße, die Stadt, der Stuhl, verstehen, sprechen, spielen, der Sport, spät. Aber: ist, die Post, der Gast." label="Listen & Repeat: st ('sht') and sp ('shp')" />

### 2.5 The Two Faces of German `r`: Throat `r` vs. Vocalic `-er` ("uh / a")
English speakers often curl the tip of their tongue backward in the middle of the mouth to make an English *r*. If you do that in German, it immediately alters the vowel right before it. Instead, German uses two much easier sounds for **r**:
1. **`r` before a vowel (at the start of a word or syllable: *rot, reisen, rechts, Büro, Straße, Brot, fragen*):**
   * Keep the tip of your tongue resting flat behind your bottom teeth and make a gentle vibration or friction at the back of your throat (like a very soft gargle or a voiced version of the back-throat *ch*). In southern Germany, Austria, and Switzerland, a light front tongue-tap is also completely normal!
2. **`-er` at the end of a word (or in prefixes `er-, ver-, zer-`) — The Easy "Short A" Secret:**
   * At the end of a word, Germans **do not pronounce an R at all!** The ending **-er** (and even a final **-r** after a long vowel like *Uhr, wir, vier, hier, Tür, nur*) relaxes into a soft, open **"uh / short a"** sound (like the *a* at the end of *sofa* or *pizza*):
   * **der Vater** ➔ sounds like *"Fah-ta"*
   * **die Mutter** ➔ sounds like *"Moot-ta"*
   * **das Wasser** ➔ sounds like *"Vas-sa"*
   * **der Lehrer** ➔ sounds like *"Lay-ra"*
   * **das Zimmer** ➔ sounds like *"Tsim-ma"*
   * **immer / super / aber / oder** ➔ *"im-ma / zoo-pa / ah-ba / oh-da"*
   * **die Uhr / die Tür / vier** ➔ *"Oo-a / Tü-a / fee-a"*

<GermanAudio text="rot, reisen, fragen, das Brot. der Vater, die Mutter, das Wasser, der Lehrer, das Zimmer, die Uhr, vier." label="Listen & Repeat: Initial r vs. Final -er ('a')" />

### 2.6 Final Consonant Hardening (*Auslautverhärtung*): Final `b, d, g` Sound Like `p, t, k`
In English, the final consonant in *bad* (voiced *d*) and *bat* (unvoiced *t*) must be kept distinct. In German, whenever **b, d, or g** stands at the **very end of a word or syllable**, it automatically loses its voice and becomes crisp and sharp like **p, t, or k**!
* **Final `-b` ➔ sounds like `-p`:**
  * **gelb** (yellow) ➔ sounds like *"gelp"*
  * **halb** (half, as in *halb vier* = 3:30) ➔ sounds like *"halp"*
  * **ab** (from / off, as in *abfahren* = to depart) ➔ sounds like *"ap"*
  * **der Urlaub** (the vacation) ➔ sounds like *"Oor-lowp"*
* **Final `-d` ➔ sounds like `-t`:**
  * **der Hund** (the dog) ➔ sounds like *"Hoont"* (plural **die Hunde** brings the *d* back: *"Hoon-de"*)
  * **das Kind** (the child) ➔ sounds like *"Kint"* (plural **die Kinder**: *"Kin-da"*)
  * **und** (and) ➔ sounds like *"oont"*
  * **das Geld** (the money) ➔ sounds like *"Gelt"*
  * **das Fahrrad** (the bicycle) ➔ sounds like *"Fahr-raht"*
  * **der Abend** (the evening) ➔ sounds like *"Ah-bent"*
* **Final `-g` ➔ sounds like `-k`:**
  * **der Tag** (the day) ➔ sounds like *"Tahk"* (plural **die Tage** brings the *g* back: *"Tah-ge"*)
  * **der Zug** (the train) ➔ sounds like *"Tsook"* (plural **die Züge**: *"Tsü-ge"*)
  * **der Weg** (the way / path) ➔ sounds like *"Vayk"*
  * **der Montag / Dienstag / Freitag** ➔ ends in crisp *"-tahk"*!

<GermanAudio text="gelb, halb, ab, der Urlaub. der Hund, die Hunde. das Kind, die Kinder. und, das Geld. der Tag, die Tage. der Zug." label="Listen & Repeat: Final b, d, g Hardening to p, t, k" />

### 2.7 The `-ig` Ending in Numbers & Adjectives (`zwanzig, dreißig, richtig, wichtig, günstig`)
What happens when a word ends in the letters **-ig**?
* **Standard High German Rule:** At the end of a word, **-ig** is pronounced just like the soft front **-ich**!
  * **zwanzig** (20) ➔ *"tsvan-tsich"*
  * **dreißig** (30) ➔ *"dray-sich"*
  * **vierzig, fünfzig, sechzig, siebzig, achtzig, neunzig** ➔ all end in *"-tsich"*!
  * **richtig** (correct) ➔ *"rich-tich"*
  * **wichtig** (important) ➔ *"vich-tich"*
  * **günstig / billig** (inexpensive / cheap) ➔ *"güns-tich / bil-lich"*
  * **entschuldigen / Entschuldigung** (excuse me) ➔ *"ent-shool-di-goong"* (here an ending follows *-ig-*, so the *g* stays a normal hard *g*!)
* *(Good to know for your ears: In southern Germany, Austria, and Switzerland, many native speakers pronounce final **-ig** as **"-ik"**—like "tsvan-tsik" or "rich-tik". Both are 100% understood and accepted in every exam!)*

### 2.8 Four More Consonant & Vowel Pairs You Need Every Day (`kn, pf, qu, tsch` + `eu/äu, au`)

## 3. Sound-Alike Hints from Class Notes — What Works and Where to Be Careful

When beginners start learning German, class notes and flashcards often write German sounds using quick English (or Indian-English / Hindi) sound-alikes. Those shortcuts are super helpful on Day 1—but a few of them can lock in bad habits if you take them too literally.

Use this repair table to check which shortcuts are 100% accurate and which ones need a small upgrade:

| Class-Note Shortcut | Is It Accurate? | ❌ What Happens If Taken Literally | ✅ The Simple Upgrade for Clear German |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`ei` = English "eye"** and **`ie` = English "ee"** | **100% True!** Keep using it! | Nothing—this is one of the best rules in German. | Look at the second letter: **mein** (*"eye"*), **vier** (*"ee"*). |
| **`eu / äu` = English "oy"** (*boy*) | **100% True!** Keep using it! | Learners only forget it on cognates like **Euro** or **Europa**. | Say **Oy-ro** and **Oy-ro-pa**, never *"Yoo-ro"*. |
| **`ß` = sharp "ss"** | **100% True!** | Sometimes learners forget that the vowel *before* **ß** is **long**. | Hold the vowel before **ß** smoothly: **die Straße** (*"Shtrah-se"*), **groß** (*"grohs"*), **der Fuß** (*"foos"*). |
| **`ü` = English "you"** | **Careful!** Only a rough hint. | English *"you"* starts with a *y*-glide and pulls the tongue back to *"oo"* (*"fyoor"* for **für**). | No *y*-glide! Say **"ee"** and round your lips tightly while your tongue stays at the front: **für**, **fünf**, **Tür**. |
| **`ö` = English "er" / "bird"** | **Careful!** Misleading if you add an R. | Adding an American or Indian English *r* curls the tongue back (*"shern"* for **schön**). | Say **"eh"** (as in *bed* or *day*) and round your lips into an **O**. Keep the tongue tip down behind your lower teeth! |
| **`j` = English "j" (*jump*)** | **Wrong for German words!** | Saying *"dja"* for **ja**, *"djetzt"* for **jetzt**, or *"Djanuar"* for **Januar**. | German **j** is **always English Y** (*yes*): **ja** (*yah*), **jetzt** (*yetst*), **das Jahr** (*yahr*), **jung** (*yoong*), **jede** (*yay-de*). (Only modern English loanwords like **der Job** or **die Jeans** use English *j*.) |
| **`ch` = English "ch" (*church*)** | **Wrong for German words!** | Saying *"tsh"* in **ich**, **Milch**, **acht**, or **Buch**. | After front vowels/consonants (**ich, Milch**): soft front hiss. After **a, o, u, au** (**Buch, acht**): gentle back-of-throat breath. English *"ch"* is written **tsch** (**Deutsch**). |
| **`z` = English "z" (*zoo*)** | **Wrong!** | Saying *"zoo-g"* for **Zug** or *"zeen"* for **zehn**. | German **z** is **always "ts"** in every position: **die Zeit** (*tsayt*), **der Zug** (*tsook*), **zwölf** (*tsvölff*), **die Heizung** (*hay-tsoong*). |
| **`w` = English "w", `v` = English "v"** | **Wrong!** | Saying *"was-ser"* with rounded lips or *"vah-ter"* with a voiced V. | Shift by one step: **w** = English **v** (teeth on lower lip: **Wasser, wir**); **v** = English **f** in German words (**Vater, vier, viel, von**). |
| **`d / t` = Retroflex (curled-back) D/T** | **Careful!** Sounds heavy to German ears. | Curling the tongue back to the roof of the mouth makes *Tag* and *danke* sound muffled. | Touch the tip of your tongue lightly right behind your **upper front teeth**. And give **p, t, k** at the start of a word a tiny puff of air: **der Tag, die Post, der Kaffee**. |
| **`r` = English curled R** | **Wrong!** | Curling the tongue distorts every vowel before *r* (*Arbeit, Karte, Uhr, Vater*). | Use a light throat *r* before vowels (**rot, reisen**), and relax **-er** at the end of words into a soft **"a"** (**Vater** ≈ *"Fah-ta"*, **Wasser** ≈ *"Vas-sa"*). |
| **Pure `o` and `e` = English gliding "ow" and "ey"** | **Careful!** | In English, *no* glides from *o* to *w* (*"no-oo"*), and *day* glides from *e* to *y* (*"de-ee"*). | Keep your lips and jaw **completely steady** during long German **o** (**wo, so, das Brot, groß, wohnen**) and long **e** (**der Tee, der See, gehen, lesen**). |

## 4. Long vs. Short Vowels — The Easy Visual Rules

In German, whether you hold a vowel out **long and smooth** or clip it **short and crisp** is not random—and it can completely change the word you are saying!

Look at these everyday A1 minimal pairs where **only the length of the vowel** changes the meaning:

| Long Vowel Word (Smooth & Held) | Meaning | Short Vowel Word (Quick & Clipped) | Meaning | How the Spelling Tells You |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **die Miete** (*mee-te*) | the rent | **die Mitte** (*mit-te*) | the middle / city center | `ie` + single `t` (long) vs. double `tt` (short) |
| **der Staat** (*shtaaht*) | the state / country | **die Stadt** (*shtat*) | the city / town | double `aa` (long) vs. two consonants `dt` (short) |
| **ihn** (*een*) | him (accusative) | **in** (*in*) | in (preposition) | silent `h` (long) vs. single short word `in` |
| **ihr** (*ee-a*) | you all / her | **irren** (*ir-ren*) | to be mistaken | silent `h` (long) vs. double `rr` (short) |
| **wen** (*vayn*) | whom (accusative) | **wenn** (*ven*) | when / if | single `n` (long) vs. double `nn` (short) |
| **den** (*dayn*) | the (accusative masc.) | **denn** (*den*) | because / then | single `n` (long) vs. double `nn` (short) |
| **der Weg** (*vayk*) | the way / path | **weg** (*vek*) | away / gone | noun with single `g` (long) vs. adverb `weg` (short) |
| **kam** (*kahm*) | came | **der Kamm** (*kam*) | the comb | single `m` (long) vs. double `mm` (short) |
| **beten** (*bay-ten*) | to pray | **die Betten** (*bet-ten*) | the beds | single `t` (long) vs. double `tt` (short) |
| **der Ofen** (*oh-fen*) | the oven | **offen** (*of-fen*) | open | single `f` (long) vs. double `ff` (short) |

<GermanAudio text="die Miete, die Mitte. der Staat, die Stadt. ihn, in. wen, wenn. den, denn. der Weg, weg. der Ofen, offen." label="Listen & Repeat: Long vs. Short Vowel Minimal Pairs" />

### The 4 Visual Signs of a LONG Vowel
Whenever you see any of these four spelling patterns, hold the vowel smoothly for an extra half-beat:
1. **Followed by a single consonant:** **der Name** (name), **sagen** (to say), **lesen** (to read), **hören** (to hear), **gut** (good), **schön** (nice), **der Tag** (day), **der Zug** (train).
2. **Followed by a silent `h` (*Dehnungs-h*):** When **h** comes right after a vowel (**ah, eh, ih, oh, uh, äh, öh, üh**), you **never pronounce the `h`**—it is just a silent traffic sign telling you: *"Make this vowel long!"*
   * **die Uhr** (*oo-a* — clock/o'clock), **fahren** (*fah-ren* — to drive/go), **sehen** (*zay-en* — to see), **gehen** (*gay-en* — to go), **wohnen** (*voh-nen* — to live), **nehmen** (*nay-men* — to take), **der Bahnhof** (*bahn-hohf* — train station), **der Stuhl** (*shtool* — chair), **das Jahr** (*yahr* — year), **bezahlen** (*be-tsah-len* — to pay).
3. **Doubled vowel (`aa, ee, oo`):**
   * **der Tee** (*tay* — tea), **der Kaffee** (*kaf-fay* — coffee), **der See / die See** (*zay* — lake / sea), **das Haar** (*hahr* — hair), **das Paar** (*pahr* — pair/couple), **das Boot** (*boht* — boat), **der Zoo** (*tsoh* — zoo).
4. **Written as `ie` (for long `i`) or followed by `ß`:**
   * **vier** (4), **sieben** (7), **hier** (here), **lieben** (to love), **die Straße** (street), **groß** (big), **der Fuß** (foot), **heißen** (to be called).

### The Visual Sign of a SHORT Vowel
Whenever a vowel is followed by **two or more consonants**—especially a **double consonant (`mm, nn, ll, tt, ss, ff, pp, ck, tz`)**—clip the vowel short, energetic, and quick:
* **kommen** (to come), **der Sommer** (summer), **das Zimmer** (room)
* **die Sonne** (sun), **kennen** (to know), **können** (can), **der Mann** (man)
* **das Bett** (bed), **bitte** (please), **die Mutter** (mother), **das Wetter** (weather)
* **das Wasser** (water), **essen** (to eat), **müssen** (must), **wissen** (to know)
* **alle** (all), **schnell** (fast), **billig** (cheap), **voll** (full)
* **der Apfel** (apple), **der Kaffee** (short *a* before *ff*!), **der Treffpunkt** (meeting point)
* **das Brötchen / die Küche** (note: in **Küche**, *ü* is short; in **Kuchen**, *u* is long!)

## 5. Word Stress & Sentence Melody

Even if you pronounce every single letter accurately, putting the stress on the wrong syllable—or using the wrong pitch melody at the end of a question—can confuse a listener. Fortunately, German word stress and sentence melody follow four crystal-clear patterns.

### 5.1 The 4 Golden Rules of German Word Stress

In the examples below, the **bold underlined syllable** gets the strongest beat and slightly higher pitch:

#### Rule 1: Native German Words Stress the FIRST Syllable
Unlike English or French, where stress jumps around, **most everyday German words put the main beat right on the very first syllable**:
* **<u>Ar</u>**beit (work), **<u>ar</u>**beiten (to work)
* **<u>Woh</u>**nung (apartment), **<u>woh</u>**nen (to live)
* **<u>Kin</u>**der (children), **<u>Mut</u>**ter (mother), **<u>Va</u>**ter (father)
* **<u>Mor</u>**gen (morning / tomorrow), **<u>A</u>**bend (evening), **<u>Wo</u>**chenende (weekend)
* **<u>Bah</u>**nhof (train station), **<u>Flug</u>**hafen (airport), **<u>Su</u>**permarkt (supermarket), **<u>Kran</u>**kenhaus (hospital)

#### Rule 2: Separable Verbs Stress the PREFIX (First Part)
As you learn in [Chapter 7: Modal Verbs & Separable Verbs](../a1/book/chapter-07-modal-verbs-separable-verbs-daily-routine.mdx), separable prefixes (**an-, auf-, aus-, ein-, mit-, nach-, vor-, weg-, zu-, zurück-**) always carry the main stress! In fact, that heavy beat on the prefix is how your ears know a verb is separable:
* **<u>an</u>**rufen (to call on the phone) ➔ *Ich rufe dich **<u>an</u>**.*
* **<u>ein</u>**kaufen (to go shopping) ➔ *Wir kaufen im Supermarkt **<u>ein</u>**.*
* **<u>auf</u>**stehen (to get up) ➔ *Ich stehe um sieben Uhr **<u>auf</u>**.*
* **<u>ab</u>**fahren (to depart) ➔ *Der Zug fährt um acht Uhr **<u>ab</u>**.*
* **<u>an</u>**kommen (to arrive) ➔ *Wann kommst du in Berlin **<u>an</u>**?*
* **<u>aus</u>**steigen / **<u>ein</u>**steigen / **<u>um</u>**steigen (to get off / get on / transfer)

#### Rule 3: Inseparable Verbs Stress the ROOT VERB (Second Part)
Unstressed prefixes (**be-, emp-, ent-, er-, ge-, miss-, ver-, zer-**) never split off and **never** take the stress. Jump quickly over the prefix and put the beat on the main verb stem:
* be**<u>zah</u>**len (to pay), be**<u>kom</u>**men (to receive/get), be**<u>stel</u>**len (to order), be**<u>su</u>**chen (to visit)
* ver**<u>ste</u>**hen (to understand), ver**<u>kau</u>**fen (to sell), ver**<u>mie</u>**ten (to rent out), ver**<u>ges</u>**sen (to forget)
* er**<u>klä</u>**ren (to explain), er**<u>zäh</u>**len (to tell)
* ge**<u>fal</u>**len (to please / like), ge**<u>hö</u>**ren (to belong to)
* ent**<u>schul</u>**digen (to excuse)

#### Rule 4: International Loanwords & `-ieren` Verbs Stress the END
Many international words that came into German from French, Latin, or Greek put the stress right near the **end of the word**:
* **Verbs ending in `-ieren`** (always stressed on **-<u>ie</u>-**): stu**<u>die</u>**ren (to study), telefo**<u>nie</u>**ren (to phone), buchsta**<u>bie</u>**ren (to spell), repa**<u>rie</u>**ren (to repair), reser**<u>vie</u>**ren (to reserve), fotogra**<u>fie</u>**ren (to photograph).
* **Everyday International Nouns:** das Bü**<u>ro</u>** (office), die Mu**<u>sik</u>** (music), der Ter**<u>min</u>** (appointment), das Ho**<u>tel</u>** (hotel), das Restau**<u>rant</u>** (restaurant), die Bäcke**<u>rei</u>** (bakery), der Stu**<u>dent</u>** (university student), der Poliz**<u>ist</u>** (police officer), die Informa**<u>tion</u>** (information), die Universi**<u>tät</u>** (university), das Pa**<u>pier</u>** (paper).

### 5.2 Sentence Melody: When Your Voice Goes Up (`↗`) vs. Down (`↘`)

As you saw in [Chapter 5: Questions, Time & Weather](../a1/book/chapter-05-questions-time-prepositions-weather.mdx), German uses two clear sentence melodies:

| Sentence Type | Voice Melody at the End | Why It Matters | Everyday Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Normal Statements** | **Falling (`↘`)** | Sounds calm, confident, and complete. | *Ich komme aus Indien.* **↘** / *Der Zug fährt um acht Uhr ab.* **↘** |
| **2. W-Questions** (*Wer, Was, Wo, Wann, Wie, Warum, Woher, Wohin*) | **Falling (`↘`)** | The W-word at the start already signals a question, so the voice drops gently at the end (just like English *"Where do you live? ↘"*). | *Wie heißen Sie?* **↘** / *Wo wohnen Sie?* **↘** / *Was kostet das?* **↘** |

#### 4 Complete Spoken Spelling Scripts (Practice Out Loud!)

1. **Spelling a First & Last Name (*Jithin Joseph*):**

3. **Spelling a Street Address and Postcode (*Goethestraße 14, 80331 München*):**

## 7. The 5-Minute Daily Repair Routine & 28 Guided Workouts

### Your 5-Minute Daily Repair Routine
You do not need an hour of pronunciation drills. Five focused minutes a day builds muscle memory fast:
1. **Minute 1 (Pick One Sound):** Choose **one** sound from Section 1 or Section 2 (for example, `ü` or soft `ch`).
2. **Minute 2 (Minimal Pairs):** Click the `<GermanAudio />` button and repeat the minimal pairs slowly 3 times, then at normal speed 3 times (*vier – für, Miete – Mitte, Wein – Wien*).
3. **Minute 3 (Survival Sentence):** Put the sound inside one real sentence you actually use in Germany:
   * *„Ich bin **für** einen Ter**min** **hier**.“*
   * *„Ich **möchte** **zwölf** **Brötchen**, **bitte**.“*
   * *„**Sprechen** Sie **bitte** **langsam**!“*
4. **Minute 4 (Spell Out Loud):** Spell your own first name, last name, street, and email address out loud without pausing on **A, E, I, J, G, V, W, Z**.
5. **Minute 5 (Voice Check):** Practice with the interactive AI Pronunciation Coach at the bottom of this page!

### Interactive 5-Step Pronunciation & Spelling Workout (28 Self-Check Drills)

Test your ears and eyes with these 28 targeted exercises before moving on to the AI coach. Click each `<details>` box to verify your answer!

#### Step A: Minimal Pair & Sound Choice (Items 1–6)

1. You want to order **four** bread rolls (*4 Brötchen*). Do you say **vier** (*"fee-a"*) or **für** (*"fü-a"*)?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer & Mouth Tip</summary>

  **vier** (*"fee-a"* — with unrounded, smiling lips and a long **ee** sound because of **i-e**). **für** (rounded whistling lips) means *"for"*!
</details>

2. You want to say *"I have unfortunately no time"* (*Ich habe **leider** keine Zeit*). How do you pronounce **leider**—with **"eye"** or **"ee"**?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer & Mouth Tip</summary>

  With **"eye"** (*"lay-da"*), because in **e-i** the second letter is **I**! Saying *"lee-da"* turns the word into **die Lieder** (the songs).
</details>

3. Which kind of **ch** is in **die Küche** (the kitchen), and which kind is in **der Kuchen** (the cake)?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer & Mouth Tip</summary>

  * **die Küche** has the **soft front `ch`** (because it comes after the front vowel **ü**; the vowel *ü* is also short).
  * **der Kuchen** has the **back throat `ch`** (because it comes after the dark back vowel **u**; the vowel *u* is long).
</details>

4. How do you pronounce the difference between **die Kirche** (the church) and **die Kirsche** (the cherry)?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer & Mouth Tip</summary>

  * **die Kirche** uses the **soft front `ch`** with flat/smiling lips (like the *h* in *huge*).
  * **die Kirsche** uses **`sch`** (*"sh"*) with pushed-out, rounded lips (like English *ship*).
</details>

5. How do the first letters sound in **wir** (we) vs. **vier** (four)?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer & Mouth Tip</summary>

  * **wir** starts with German **w**, which sounds like a buzzing English **V** (*"vee-a"*).
  * **vier** starts with German **v**, which sounds like an unvoiced English **F** (*"fee-a"*).
</details>

6. How do you pronounce the first letter of **das Zimmer** (the room) and **der Zug** (the train)?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer & Mouth Tip</summary>

  Always as a crisp **"ts"** (*"Tsim-ma"* and *"Tsook"*), never like an English buzzing *z*!
</details>

---

#### Step B: Long vs. Short Vowels & Final Consonants (Items 7–12)

7. Look at **die Miete** (the rent) and **die Mitte** (the middle). Which one has a long vowel, and how does the spelling show it?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **die Miete** has a **long "ee"** sound because it is written with **ie** and followed by only **one `t`**. **die Mitte** has a **short, clipped "i"** (like English *sit*) because it is followed by **double `tt`**.
</details>

8. Look at **der Staat** (the state) and **die Stadt** (the city). Which one has a long **"ah"** sound?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **der Staat** has a **long "ah"** because the vowel **aa** is doubled. **die Stadt** has a **short, crisp "a"** because it is followed by two consonants (**dt**).
</details>

9. In the words **wohnen**, **sehen**, **fahren**, and **die Uhr**, how do you pronounce the letter **h**?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  You **do not pronounce the `h` at all**! After a vowel, **h** is completely silent (*Dehnungs-h*) and simply tells you to hold the vowel before it **long and smooth**.
</details>

10. How do the final consonants sound in **der Tag**, **der Hund**, and **gelb**?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  Because of final consonant hardening (*Auslautverhärtung*), final **b, d, g** sound like **p, t, k**:
  * **der Tag** ➔ *"Tahk"*
  * **der Hund** ➔ *"Hoont"*
  * **gelb** ➔ *"gelp"*
</details>

11. What happens to the **g** and **d** when **der Tag** and **der Hund** become plural (**die Tage**, **die Hunde**)?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  Because the consonant is no longer at the end of the word, its normal voiced sound comes right back: **die Tage** (*"Tah-ge"*) and **die Hunde** (*"Hoon-de"*).
</details>

12. How do you pronounce the ending **-ig** in **zwanzig**, **dreißig**, **richtig**, and **wichtig** in standard German?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  Like the soft front **-ich** (*"tsvan-tsich, dray-sich, rich-tich, vich-tich"*). (In southern Germany and Austria, *"-ik"* is also widely used.)
</details>

---

#### Step C: Word Stress & Sentence Melody (Items 13–18)

13. Which syllable is stressed in the separable verb **anrufen** (to call) vs. the inseparable verb **bezahlen** (to pay)?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  * **<u>an</u>**rufen stresses the separable prefix **an-** (first syllable).
  * be**<u>zah</u>**len skips over the inseparable prefix *be-* and stresses the root syllable **-zah-** (second syllable).
</details>

14. Which syllable is stressed in **verstehen** (to understand) vs. **einkaufen** (to shop)?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  * ver**<u>ste</u>**hen (inseparable prefix *ver-* ➔ stress on **-ste-**).
  * **<u>ein</u>**kaufen (separable prefix *ein-* ➔ stress on **ein-**).
</details>

15. Where does the stress fall in **studieren**, **telefonieren**, and **buchstabieren**?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  All verbs ending in **-ieren** put the main stress right on **-<u>ie</u>-**: stu**<u>die</u>**ren, telefo**<u>nie</u>**ren, buchsta**<u>bie</u>**ren.
</details>

16. Where is the stress in the international nouns **das Büro**, **der Termin**, and **die Musik**?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  On the **final syllable**: das Bü**<u>ro</u>**, der Ter**<u>min</u>**, die Mu**<u>sik</u>**.
</details>

17. Does your voice go **up (`↗`)** or **down (`↘`)** at the end of the W-question *„Wo wohnen Sie?“*?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **Down (`↘`)**! W-questions start with a question word (*Wo*), so the melody falls calmly at the end: *Wo wohnen Sie?* **↘**
</details>

18. Does your voice go **up (`↗`)** or **down (`↘`)** at the end of the Yes/No question *„Haben Sie morgen Zeit?“*?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **Up (`↗`)**! Yes/No questions start with the verb (*Haben*) and rise clearly on the final stressed word (*Zeit?* **↗**).
</details>

#### Step D: Telephone Spelling Traps & Decoding (Items 19–28)

19. A German receptionist spells a code over the phone: **„E – I – G – E – N“** (she says: *"ay – ee – gay – ay – enn"*). What 5 letters do you write down?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **E – I – G – E – N** (*eigen*). Watch out: *"ay"* is **E**, *"ee"* is **I**, and *"gay"* is **G**!
</details>

20. Someone on the phone spells their last name: *"yott – ah – kah – oh – bay"*. Write the name!
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **J – A – K – O – B** (**Jakob**). Remember: *"yott"* = **J**, *"ah"* = **A**!
</details>

21. Someone spells a German city: *"vay – ee – ay – enn"*. Which city is it?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **W – I – E – N** (**Wien** = Vienna). *"vay"* = **W**, *"ee"* = **I**, *"ay"* = **E**, *"enn"* = **N**.
</details>

22. Someone spells another city: *"tsett – üh – err – ee – tsay – hah"*. Which city is it?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **Z – Ü – R – I – C – H** (**Zürich**). *"tsett"* = **Z**, *"üh"* = **Ü**, *"tsay"* = **C**.
</details>

23. How do you spell the name **Viktor** out loud in German letter names?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **V – I – K – T – O – R** = **„faow – ee – kah – tay – oh – err“** (never say *"vee"* or *"eye"*!).
</details>

24. How do you spell the last name **Weber** out loud in German letter names?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **W – E – B – E – R** = **„vay – ay – bay – ay – err“**.
</details>

25. How do you spell the last name **Hoffmann** out loud using **Doppel-**?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **H – O – Doppel-F – M – A – Doppel-N** = **„hah – oh – Doppel-eff – emm – ah – Doppel-enn“**.
</details>

26. How do you spell the street name **Bahnhofstraße** out loud in German?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **B – A – H – N – H – O – F – S – T – R – A – Eszett – E** = **„bay – ah – hah – enn – hah – oh – eff – ess – tay – err – ah – Eszett – ay“**.
</details>

27. How do you read the email address **max_mueller@web.de** out loud on the phone?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **„M – A – X – Unterstrich – M – U – E – Doppel-L – E – R – ät – W – E – B – Punkt – D – E.“**
</details>

28. How do you read the email address **info-berlin@gmx.de** out loud on the phone?
<details>
  <summary>Show Answer</summary>

  **„I – N – F – O – Bindestrich – B – E – R – L – I – N – ät – G – M – X – Punkt – D – E.“** (Remember: **I** is *"ee"*, **E** is *"ay"*, **G** is *"gay"*, **X** is *"iks"*!)
</details>
