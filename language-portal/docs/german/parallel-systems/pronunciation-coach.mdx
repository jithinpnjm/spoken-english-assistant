# D. Pronunciation Coach (Aussprache)

The letters and their names are in [1.1 Alphabet & Pronunciation](../a1/vol1-foundations/alphabet-pronunciation.mdx).
This page is your **repair workshop**: the sounds that most often make English speakers hard to understand, and
how to fix them.

## 1. The "priority five" for English speakers

| Problem | What to do with your mouth | Minimal pairs (say both) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ü** | Say "ee", keep your tongue there, then round your lips tightly like whistling. | *vier – für*, *Kiste – Küste* |
| **ö** | Say "eh", keep the tongue, round your lips like "o". | *kennen – können*, *Meere – Möhre* |
| **ch after e, i, ä, ö, ü, l, n, r** (*ich, Milch*) | Say "h" as in *huge*, but let air hiss over the middle of the tongue. Not "sh", not "k". | *Kirche – Kirsche* (*ch* vs *sch*) |
| **ch after a, o, u, au** (*acht, Buch*) | A soft scrape at the back of the throat, like clearing your throat gently. | *Bach – back* (German vs English) |
| **ei vs. ie** | *ei* = "eye", *ie* = "ee". Look at the **second** letter. | *Wein – Wien*, *leider – Lieder* |

## 2. More sounds worth fixing

- **z = ts** at the start of a word: *Zug, zehn, Zeit* – never English "z".
- **w = English v**: *Wasser, wohnen*. **v** usually = f: *Vater, viel* (in some loanwords v: *Vase*).
- **s + vowel** is usually voiced like English z, especially in the north: *Sonne, sieben*.
- **sp / st** at the start of a word or word part = "shp / sht": *Sport, Straße, verstehen*.
- **r:** German uses a back-of-the-throat r (in some regions a rolled r). At the end of a word *-er* sounds almost like
  a short "a": *Lehrer* ≈ "Lehra", *Wasser* ≈ "Wassa". Don't pronounce an English r.
- **Final consonants harden** (*Auslautverhärtung*): at the end of a word or syllable, b/d/g sound like p/t/k:
  *Tag* ≈ "Tak", *und* ≈ "unt", *gelb* ≈ "gelp". Then in the plural they come back: *Tage*.
- **-ig** at the end is usually said like *ich* in standard German (*zwanzig, König*); in the south you will also hear "-ik".
- **Long vs short vowels** change meaning: *Staat – Stadt*, *ihn – in*, *Miete – Mitte*. A double consonant after a vowel
  usually means the vowel is short (*Mitte, kommen*); *h* after a vowel means long (*sehen, Uhr*).

## 2b. Sound-alike hints – and where they mislead

Class notes often write German sounds with English (or Hindi) sound-alikes. That helps on day one, but a few of these
hints lead to a noticeable accent if you take them literally:

| Handy hint | How far it is true | Fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *ei* = English "eye", *ie* = English "ee" | True – keep it. | Look at the second letter: *mein* ("eye"), *vier* ("ee"). |
| *ü* = English "you" | Only roughly. "you" starts with a y-glide and ends as "oo". | No glide: say "ee" and round your lips while the tongue stays put – *für* is not "fyoor". |
| *ö* = "eo" / "er" | Only roughly. | Say "eh" with rounded lips; don't add an English r (*schön*, *zwölf*). |
| *j* = English "j" | Wrong for German words. | *j* = English **y**: *ja, jetzt, Januar* ("yah", "yetst", "yanuar"). English "j" only in loanwords like *Job, Jeans*. |
| *ch* = English "ch" (*church*) | Wrong for German words. | After *e, i, ä, ö, ü* and consonants: soft hiss (*ich, Milch*); after *a, o, u, au*: back of the throat (*Buch, acht*). English "ch" is written *tsch* (*Deutsch*). |
| *z* = English "z" | Wrong. | *z* = "ts" at every position: *Zeit, zwölf, Pizza*. |
| *ß* = "ss" | True. | *Straße* ≈ "Strahsse"; the vowel before *ß* is long. |
| *w* = English "w", *v* = "v" | Wrong. | *w* = English **v** with the top teeth on the lower lip (*Wasser*); *v* = **f** in most German words (*Vater*). If your English merges v and w, practise *Wein – fein*, *wir – vier*. |
| *d / t* = the Indian-English retroflex sounds | Not quite. | German *t* and *d* are made with the tongue tip just behind the upper teeth, and *t, p, k* at the start of a word come with a small puff of air: *Tag, Post, Kaffee*. |
| *r* = English r | Wrong. | Back-of-the-throat r (or a light roll); *-er* at the end ≈ short "a". |

## 3. Word stress

- Most native German words are stressed on the **first syllable**: ***Ar**beit, **Woh**nung, **Kin**der*.
- Separable verbs: stress on the prefix – ***an**rufen, **ein**kaufen*.
- Non-separable verbs: stress on the verb – *be**zah**len, ver**ste**hen*.
- Many loanwords are stressed at the end: *Bü**ro**, Mu**sik**, Informa**tion**, Stu**dent**, Ter**min***.

## 4. The repair routine (5 minutes a day)

1. Pick **one** sound from the tables.
2. Say the minimal pair slowly, then fast, five times.
3. Put it in a survival sentence you really use (*Ich bin **für** einen Termin hier. Ich möchte **vier** Brötchen.*).
4. Record yourself on your phone and compare with a native recording (e.g. dictionary audio).
5. Ask the AI teacher below to give you a pronunciation cue.
