# Tense Control

**Level:** B1-B2 · **Goal:** explain past, present and future ideas without mixing tenses in the same answer.

At this level you know the tenses. The problem is **control under pressure**: in a real conversation you start in the past and drift into the present, or reach for the present perfect when a finished time is on the table. Tense tells the listener *when* something happened and *whether it still matters* — get it wrong and the meaning changes completely.

This module helps you establish an anchor in time and remain consistent as you construct your thoughts.

## The Core Strategy

> **Decide the time first → choose the tense → keep it until the time changes.**

Before you answer, mentally ask yourself three questions:

1. **When** is this happening — past, right now, or future?
2. Is the action **completely finished**, or does it connect to **now**?
3. Did I (or the other person) use a **time word** (*yesterday, since, for, next week*)?

### Tense Matrix at a Glance

| Tense | Core meaning | Signal words | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Past simple | finished, at a known time | *yesterday, last…, …ago, in 2020* | *We fixed the bug yesterday.* |
| Past continuous | background in progress | *while, when, at 3 pm yesterday* | *I was presenting when the call dropped.* |
| Present perfect | past → now: experience, result, unfinished period | *ever, never, already, yet, just, since, for, so far* | *I've worked here for three years.* |
| Past perfect | earlier than another past event | *by the time, before, already* | *The meeting had started when I arrived.* |
| going to / planning to | a plan decided before | *next week, this summer* | *We're going to migrate the database next month.* |
| will / might | prediction (certain / possible) | *probably, I think, maybe* | *It'll probably take two days. It might fail.* |

## Deep Dive: The Present Perfect vs Past Simple Battle

The most common trap at B1-B2 level is confusing Present Perfect and Past Simple. 
- Use **Past Simple** when the "time door" is closed (e.g., last week, in 2019, yesterday). 
- Use **Present Perfect** when the "time door" is open (e.g., this week, today, in my life so far).

**Compare:**
- ❌ *I've sent the report yesterday.* (Time door is closed 'yesterday' = Past Simple)
- ✅ *I sent the report yesterday.*
- ✅ *I've sent the report this morning.* (If it is still morning, the time door is open).

### Experience vs Specific Events
Often, conversations start with Present Perfect to establish an experience, and then switch to Past Simple to give the details:
- **A:** *Have you ever negotiated a software contract?* (Present Perfect)
- **B:** *Yes, I have. In my last job, I negotiated the Microsoft renewal. It took three months.* (Past Simple)

## Deep Dive: 'For' and 'Since'

When talking about how long something has been happening (Present Perfect Continuous or Present Perfect Simple):
- Use **since** for a *point* in time: *since 2020*, *since January*, *since I graduated*.
- Use **for** for a *duration* of time: *for three years*, *for two months*, *for a long time*.

## Advanced Tip: Using Tenses for Politeness

In professional English, tenses can make your requests sound softer and more polite.
- **Direct (Present Simple):** *I want to ask you a question.*
- **Softer (Past Simple):** *I wanted to ask you a question.* (Commonly used at the start of a meeting)
- **Softest (Past Continuous):** *I was wondering if I could ask you a question.*

## Real-World Model Dialogues

**Situation 1: The Stand-up Meeting**
- *I usually work from home, but today I'm working from the office.* (Routine vs Right now)
- *Yesterday I fixed the authentication issue and updated the QA team.* (Completed action)
- *Right now, I'm waiting for the code review.* (Action in progress)
- *Next week I'm going to prepare the release plan; it'll probably take two days.* (Plan & Prediction)

**Situation 2: The Incident Report**
- *When the incident happened, we were deploying a new version.* (Interrupted background)
- *By the time I logged onto the server, the CPU had already hit 100%.* (Earlier past)
- *I've worked on similar problems before, so I knew where to look.* (Experience linked to past action)

## Common mistakes and how to fix them

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *I have visited Switzerland last year.* | *I visited Switzerland last year.* | Finished time → past simple. |
| *I am working here since 2020.* | *I have been working here since 2020.* | Past → now needs the present perfect (continuous). |
| *I have been here since 3 years.* | *I have been here for 3 years.* | *for* + duration, *since* + point in time. |
| *Yesterday I go to the office and check logs.* | *Yesterday I went to the office and checked the logs.* | Keep the whole story in the past. Avoid tense drift! |
| *Now I work on this issue.* | *Right now I'm working on this issue.* | Action in progress → present continuous. |
| *Tomorrow I will going.* | *Tomorrow I will go.* / *I'm going.* | *will* + base verb. |
| *When I reached, the meeting already started.* | *When I arrived, the meeting had already started.* | Earlier past → past perfect. |

## Practice drills

1. **Time first:** make one sentence each with *yesterday / now / usually / since 2022 / next month*.
2. **Experience → detail:** *Have you ever…? — Yes, I have. I … (past simple + when).* Do three pairs.
3. **Background + interruption:** *I was ___ when ___.*
4. **Timeline talk:** describe your career: past simple for jobs you left, present perfect for your current job.
5. **Polite Requests:** Change "I want to speak with the manager" into a polite past continuous form.

## Checkpoint: I can…

- [ ] use past simple with finished-time words and never with *since/for* + now
- [ ] use the present perfect for experience, results, and unfinished periods
- [ ] use *for* and *since* correctly without translating directly from my native language
- [ ] set a background with the past continuous and sequence events with the past perfect
- [ ] shift tenses for politeness (e.g. *I was wondering*)
- [ ] give a 60-second update without switching tense accidentally
