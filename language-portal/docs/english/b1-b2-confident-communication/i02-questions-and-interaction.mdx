# Questions and Interaction

**Level:** B1-B2 · **Goal:** ask grammatically correct questions and keep a conversation moving with follow-ups, clarifications and polite interruptions.

A conversation is not a series of answers. Good speakers **ask**: to get information, to clarify, to show interest, and to check understanding. Question word order is a common B1 challenge; repeated short practice helps it become automatic. The target is *What are you doing?*, not *What you are doing?*

In some professional or unfamiliar situations, a direct question (*Where is the report?*) may sound abrupt without context or a softener. Indirect questions and clarifying phrases give you options; direct questions are also normal when the relationship and situation make them appropriate.

## The Core Pattern

> **(Question word) + auxiliary + subject + main verb + rest?**

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Yes/no | auxiliary + subject + verb | ***Did** you **check** the logs?* |
| Wh- | wh- + auxiliary + subject + verb | *When **does** the meeting **start**?* |
| Subject question | who/what + verb (no *do*) | ***Who** called you?* · ***What** happened?* |
| Indirect | polite opener + **statement order** | *Could you tell me **where the station is**?* |
| Tag | statement + opposite tag | *You're coming, **aren't you**? It isn't ready, **is it**?* |

No auxiliary in the statement? Use **do / does / did**: *He works here → Does he work here?*

## Deep Dive: Direct vs. Indirect Questions

In a formal request or when you need a softer tone, an indirect question can sound less abrupt. Direct questions are also natural in many everyday conversations and when you need a quick, clear answer.

**Rule:** After the polite opener, the question turns into a normal sentence structure (Subject + Verb). **Do not use do/does/did.**

- **Direct:** *Why did the server crash?*
- **Indirect:** *Could you explain why the server crashed?* (NOT: *Could you explain why did the server crash?*)

- **Direct:** *Where is the project file?*
- **Indirect:** *Do you know where the project file is?* (NOT: *Do you know where is the project file?*)

**Polite Openers:**
- *Could you tell me...*
- *Do you know...*
- *I was wondering if...*
- *Would you mind explaining...*

## The Art of Active Interaction

Being fluent isn't just about speaking without pauses; it's about making the other person feel heard.

**1. Acknowledging & Showing Interest:**
- *Oh, really?*
- *That makes sense.*
- *I see what you mean.*

**2. Clarifying (When you didn't understand):**
- *Sorry, what do you mean by "soon"?*
- *Could you clarify what you mean by "restructure"?*
- *So, if I understand correctly, we need to deploy on Friday. Is that right?*

**3. Interrupting Politely:**
- *Sorry to interrupt, but could I just ask a quick question?*
- *Can I just jump in here for a second?*

## Vocabulary and meanings

| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **follow-up question** | a question that asks for more detail after an answer | *Can I ask a follow-up question about the timeline?* |
| **clarify** | make something easier to understand | *Could you clarify what “soon” means here?* |
| **elaborate on** | give more detail about a point | *Could you elaborate on the second option?* |
| **jump in** | join a conversation, often while someone else is speaking | *Can I jump in with one quick question?* |
| **interrupt** | speak before another person has finished | *Sorry to interrupt, but I have one concern.* |
| **confirm** | check that something is correct or agreed | *Let me confirm the deadline: Friday at noon.* |

See [Speaking Confidence: Vocabulary and Meanings](../vocabulary/speaking-confidence.mdx) for more useful chunks and plain-English definitions.

## Common mistakes and how to fix them

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *What you are doing?* | *What are you doing?* | Auxiliary before the subject. |
| *You checked the logs?* | *Did you check the logs?* | Use *did* for a neutral past-tense question. A rising statement can also be a natural conversational check or express surprise. |
| *When meeting start?* | *When does the meeting start?* | *does* for present simple with it/he/she. |
| *Who did call you?* | *Who called you?* | *who* is the subject → no *did*. |
| *Can you tell me where is the station?* | *Can you tell me where the station is?* | Indirect questions use statement order. |
| *You are coming, no?* | *You're coming, aren't you?* | Use a proper question tag instead of "no?". |
| *Would you mind to send it?* | *Would you mind sending it?* | *mind* + -ing. |

## Practice drills

1. **Flip it:** turn statements into questions — *He works remotely. / They finished it. / It will take two days.*
2. **Indirect upgrade:** turn *Where is the office? / Why did he leave? / When does it finish?* into indirect questions.
3. **Follow-up chain:** answer a question, add one detail, ask one question back, then respond to the new answer.
4. **Clarify:** respond to *"Just fix it soon"* with questions about the meaning of *fix*, the deadline, and the expected result.
5. **Repair:** explain an idea badly on purpose, notice the listener's confusion, and restart with *What I mean is…*.

### Worked exchange: clarify without sounding confrontational

> **Manager:** Could you update the report soon and use the usual numbers? <br />
> **You:** Sure. Just so I use the right figures, could you clarify which numbers you mean? <br />
> **Manager:** The monthly sales figures. <br />
> **You:** Thanks. And when would you need the report? <br />
> **Manager:** By Thursday afternoon. <br />
> **You:** Let me check I've understood: I'll update the monthly sales figures and send you the report by Thursday afternoon. Is that right?

Notice the sequence: **acknowledge → ask one clear question → ask the next question → confirm the full task**. You do not need to wrap every question in a long indirect phrase.

## Intensive interaction workout (20 minutes)

### Round 1 — Make the question automatic (4 minutes)

Say each question aloud. Keep the auxiliary beside the subject; do not pause between them.

| Prompt | Your question | One possible answer |
|---|---|---|
| She works in finance. Ask about her department. | *Which department does she work in?* | *She works in Finance.* |
| They sent the files. Check yes/no. | *Did they send the files?* | *Yes, they sent them this morning.* |
| The call starts at 2:30. Ask the time. | *What time does the call start?* | *At half past two.* |
| Someone changed the schedule. Ask who. | *Who changed the schedule?* | *Maya changed it.* |
| The update will take two days. Ask how long. | *How long will the update take?* | *It'll take two days.* |

**Extra challenge:** ask each question once directly and once indirectly. For example: *When does the call start? → Could you tell me when the call starts?*

### Round 2 — Turn answers into conversation (4 minutes)

For each answer, respond with **acknowledgement + follow-up + your own related detail**.

1. *“I started a new course last month.”* <br />
   Model: *“Oh, nice. What are you studying? I started learning photography last year.”*
2. *“The launch has moved to June.”* <br />
   Model: *“Thanks for the update. What caused the change? We may need to adjust the training plan too.”*
3. *“I usually work from home on Fridays.”* <br />
   Model: *“That sounds convenient. Do you find it easier to concentrate at home? I like having a quiet morning.”*

Now make a new follow-up question for each answer. Avoid asking a question that can only be answered *yes* or *no* unless you have a reason to check a detail.

### Round 3 — Make vague language specific (4 minutes)

For each message, ask two questions: one about **what** is needed and one about **when / for whom / in what format**.

- *“Send me the latest version later.”*
- *“Can you make this more professional?”*
- *“We need to deal with the client issue quickly.”*

Model for the first: *“Which version do you mean—the draft or the approved copy? When would you like me to send it?”* A useful clarification narrows the options instead of repeating the vague word.

### Round 4 — Interrupt and re-enter (4 minutes)

Practise these three moves aloud:

1. **Enter:** *“Sorry to jump in—could I check one detail?”*
2. **Ask:** *“When you say ‘next week’, do you mean by Tuesday or by Friday?”*
3. **Return the floor:** *“Thanks, that clears it up. Please go on.”*

Try again with a different opener: *“Can I add one quick point?”* or *“Before we move on, could I ask about the deadline?”* Use a shorter interruption when the discussion is informal.

### Round 5 — Repair a misunderstanding (4 minutes)

Take turns as speaker and listener. The listener deliberately misunderstands one detail. The speaker should correct the meaning calmly, without apologising repeatedly.

> **Speaker:** *We should send the draft on Wednesday.* <br />
> **Listener:** *So the final version is due Wednesday?* <br />
> **Speaker:** *Not quite. What I mean is, Wednesday is for the draft. We can send the final version after the review.*

Useful repair phrases: *“Let me put that another way…” · “I may not have been clear…” · “The main point is…” · “Yes, except that…”*

### Final challenge — three-minute conversation

Discuss a change to a shared plan. During the exchange, do all four:

- ask one open question and one specific follow-up;
- clarify one vague detail;
- politely enter the conversation once;
- summarize the agreement and check that the other person agrees.

Afterwards, review **one** question-order improvement and **one** interaction skill that helped the conversation continue.
