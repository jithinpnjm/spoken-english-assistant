# Advanced Fluency

**Level:** B2-C1 · **Goal:** speak at length — in meetings, interviews and presentations — with a controlled pace, clear structure, purposeful emphasis, and the ability to recover when you lose a word.

At C1, fluency is **control**, not speed. A fluent speaker pauses deliberately, signals structure, stresses what matters, rephrases smoothly when a word won't come, and keeps going after a correction.

## The four habits

### 1. Pause between chunks, not inside them

> *The main risk / is the vendor dependency. // If they slip, / we slip. // So I'd suggest / a fallback option.*

A half-second pause after a key point sounds confident. A pause in the middle of a phrase (*the main… risk… is the…*) sounds hesitant.

### 2. Replace fillers with useful thinking time

| Instead of… | Say… |
|---|---|
| *um, uh, erm* | a silent pause |
| *like, basically, actually, you know* (as fillers) | nothing — or a precise word |
| *what to say…* | *Let me think about that for a second.* |
| *how do you call it…* | *What's the word… · It's a kind of…* |
| *I mean… I mean…* | *In other words, …* / *To put it another way, …* |

### 3. Signpost your structure

*There are two reasons. First, … Second, … · The key point is… · Let me give you an example. · So, to sum up, …*

Structure words give you **planning time** and make you easier to follow.

### 4. Rephrase instead of stopping

When the word won't come, **describe it and move on**:

- *It's the process where we check the code before merging — the code review.*
- *We need someone who takes responsibility for the whole thing — an owner.*

## Vocabulary and meanings

| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **signpost** | show listeners how your ideas are organized | *I'll signpost the two main risks before I explain the recommendation.* |
| **weigh up** | compare factors before making a decision | *We should weigh up the cost and the benefit.* |
| **trade-off** | a gain in one area that requires giving up something in another | *The trade-off is speed versus flexibility.* |
| **tentative** | not final; likely to change | *We have a tentative date, pending approval.* |
| **on balance** | considering all the points overall | *On balance, the benefits outweigh the risks.* |
| **rephrase** | express the same idea in different words | *Let me rephrase that: we need more time to test.* |
| **acknowledge** | show that you recognize a point, concern, or contribution | *I'd like to acknowledge the support team before we move on.* |
| **concise** | brief but still clear and complete | *I'll keep this concise and focus on the decision we need.* |

For more level-organized vocabulary with meanings and model sentences, see [Speaking Confidence: Vocabulary and Meanings](../vocabulary/speaking-confidence.mdx).

## Useful thinking-aloud language

- *My initial thought is… but let me think it through.*
- *There are a couple of ways to look at this.*
- *If I had to choose, I'd say…*
- *I'm not 100% sure, but my best guess is…*
- *That's a good question — let me break it down.*

## Worked model: make and defend a recommendation

**Question:** *Should the team trial a four-day workweek?* <br />
**Plan:** recommendation → two reasons → trade-off → safeguards → measure of success.

> *I'd recommend a three-month trial rather than a permanent change straight away. // First, a shorter week could help people focus on the highest-priority work. For example, the team could protect two meeting-free mornings. // Second, it may help us retain experienced staff. That said, the main trade-off is customer coverage; we can't simply compress every task into fewer days. // I'd suggest staggering team schedules and agreeing response times in advance. We should also measure delivery, customer response, and staff feedback against the current arrangement. // If service levels fall, we can adjust the trial. On balance, a time-limited pilot would give us useful evidence before we make a long-term decision.*

### Why this answer sounds considered

| Move | Phrase | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| State a position | *I'd recommend a three-month trial…* | Gives the listener the answer early. |
| Organize reasons | *First… Second…* | Makes a longer answer easier to follow. |
| Add a concession | *That said, the main trade-off is…* | Shows that you have considered a downside. |
| Offer action | *I'd suggest staggering…* | Moves from concern to a practical response. |
| Set a condition | *If service levels fall…* | Shows how you would adapt if the plan fails. |
| Conclude | *On balance…* | Makes the final recommendation clear. |

The pauses mark idea boundaries, not dramatic gaps. Replace the topic and evidence with details you can explain confidently; do not memorize the paragraph word for word.

## Intensive speaking lab (25–30 minutes)

### Round 1 — Build a 90-second answer (6 minutes)

Choose one prompt. Take two minutes to prepare **six keywords**, not a script. Then speak using **position → reason → example → counterpoint → response → conclusion**.

| Prompt | Useful angle to explore |
|---|---|
| Should meetings default to 25 or 50 minutes instead of half an hour or an hour? | Focus time versus discussion time |
| Is it better to specialize deeply or keep a broad skill set? | Expertise versus adaptability |
| Should teams prioritize speed or reliability? | Short-term delivery versus long-term trust |

Opening frames if you need them: *“My initial view is…” · “The main reason is…” · “A good example is…” · “The strongest counterargument is…” · “On balance…”*

### Round 2 — Add nuance without hiding your position (5 minutes)

Upgrade each overconfident statement so it remains clear but allows for evidence or exceptions:

| Too absolute | More considered |
|---|---|
| *This will solve the problem.* | *This could address the main cause, although we'd need to test it first.* |
| *Everyone agrees with the plan.* | *The feedback has been broadly positive, though we still need to hear from Support.* |
| *The other option is bad.* | *The other option is less suitable here because it would increase the handover time.* |

Now give your own recommendation using one careful phrase—*likely, in most cases, based on what we know, one risk is…*—and one firm conclusion.

### Round 3 — Explain, don't freeze (5 minutes)

Explain each word **without saying it**. Give its purpose, an example, or a contrast. Then reveal the word and use it in a work-related sentence.

- **bottleneck** — a stage that slows the whole process;
- **stakeholder** — a person or group affected by a decision;
- **trade-off** — a benefit gained at the cost of another benefit;
- **fallback** — a backup plan used if the first plan fails.

Model: *“It's the stage in a process where work starts to pile up and delays everything else. For example, if only one person can approve requests, that approval step may become a…”* (**bottleneck**).

### Round 4 — Respond to a challenge (5 minutes)

After your recommendation, have a partner ask one challenge question. Answer directly, acknowledge the concern, then return to your main point.

> **Partner:** *“What if the trial reduces customer coverage?”* <br />
> **Speaker:** *“That's a fair concern. We'd need to stagger schedules and monitor response times. If coverage drops below the agreed level, we'd revise the schedule before extending the trial.”*

Practise with these challenges: *“How much will it cost?” · “What evidence supports that?” · “What could go wrong?” · “What would make you change your mind?”*

### Round 5 — Recover when interrupted or lose a word (4 minutes)

Resume with a bridge instead of restarting:

- *“Coming back to the main point…”*
- *“Let me put that another way…”*
- *“The term isn't coming to me, but it's the step where…”*
- *“I haven't considered that angle. My first thought is…, but I'd want to check…”*

Give a one-minute answer. Ask a partner to interrupt once with a relevant question; answer it in two sentences, then reconnect to your point.

### Round 6 — Repeat and review (5 minutes)

Repeat Round 1 with the **same position** but no notes. Keep the strongest example, improve one transition, and shorten one sentence. Review with these questions:

- Could the listener identify my recommendation in the first 20 seconds?
- Did each reason have an example or explanation?
- Did I acknowledge the strongest concern and respond to it?
- When interrupted or unsure, did I recover and continue?

Choose one item to improve next time. Do not judge this round by speed or by eliminating every filler.

## Common mistakes

| Habit | Better | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *And then… and then… and also…* | *First… Second… Finally…* | Signposts beat chains of *and*. |
| Stopping when a word is missing | Paraphrase and continue | The listener needs the meaning, not the exact word. |
| Speaking faster to sound fluent | Slower, with pauses at chunk boundaries | Speed without pauses is harder to follow. |
| Apologising for every mistake | Correct once, briefly, and continue | Over-apologising draws attention to errors. |
| Equal stress on every word | Stress the key word: *We need a DECISION today.* | Stress carries meaning in English. |

## Checkpoint: I can…

- [ ] speak for two minutes with clear structure and signposting
- [ ] use pauses and thinking phrases to organize an answer
- [ ] paraphrase a missing word and keep going
- [ ] self-correct once and continue without losing the thread
- [ ] use stress to show contrast and emphasis

## Homework

Pick a work topic. Give a two-minute explanation on three different days. Each time, improve one thing—structure, emphasis, or recovery when a word is missing. Compare your message's clarity and ease with your own previous attempt, not a filler count.
