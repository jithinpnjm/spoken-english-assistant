# English Speaking Methodology

This standard applies to the spoken-English track in `docs/english/`. It is separate from the German
curriculum methodology in this folder.

## Course promise

Help learners communicate what they mean with growing ease, clarity, flexibility, and independence in
everyday, social, and professional situations. Treat confidence as something practice builds—not a
personality trait, a promise of perfect speech, or a requirement to sound like a native speaker.

The long-term course target is independent, nuanced communication at C1. Describe CEFR levels by what
learners can do; do not equate C1/C2 with native-speaker status or imply every learner needs C2.

## Principles for every speaking lesson

1. **Teach, model, then speak.** Give a useful phrase or pattern, a natural model, and a supported first try
   before asking for open-ended performance.
2. **Fade support gradually.** Move from a model or sentence frame, to a prompt or keywords, to a familiar
   scenario, then to a new scenario with a real conversational response.
3. **Practise meaning, not just sentences.** Include listening, follow-up questions, clarification, turn
   taking, repair, and a clear outcome—not only monologues or grammar transformations.
4. **Repeat, then transfer.** After feedback, let the learner retry the same idea; then ask them to use the
   improvement in a fresh sentence, question, or situation.
5. **Protect fluency practice.** During a story or uninterrupted speaking turn, do not interrupt for minor
   errors. Note one high-value improvement and give it after the learner finishes. During focused accuracy
   practice, correct the lesson's target directly.
6. **Make feedback selective and usable.** Start with one specific success. Address the error that most
   affects meaning, today's target, or a recurring pattern; give a short explanation and a natural model.
   Do not overload the learner with a list of corrections.
7. **Treat pronunciation as intelligibility.** Coach sounds, stress, rhythm, and pausing when they help the
   listener understand. Never grade accent or present one accent as the goal.
8. **Make challenge adjustable.** Offer planning time, a shorter first turn, sentence starters, or a second
   attempt. Increase spontaneity and audience pressure over time; never equate anxiety with lack of ability.

## Vocabulary section standard

Give each substantial English lesson a clearly labelled **Vocabulary and meanings** section, separate from
the grammar explanation. Prioritize about 5–10 words or phrases the learner can use in that lesson. Use a
three-column table: **word or phrase · plain-English meaning · natural spoken example**. Teach useful chunks
and collocations together, explain register when it matters, and avoid unexplained synonym lists. Link to the
level-organized [Speaking Confidence: Vocabulary and Meanings](../docs/english/vocabulary/speaking-confidence.mdx)
and the relevant daily-life or workplace bank when learners need more.

## Practice-depth standard

Every substantial speaking chapter should include a worked example and a graduated practice set—not only a
list of lesson titles or generic prompts. A useful minimum is:

1. A model answer or exchange with its structure made visible.
2. Controlled practice with enough examples to notice and repeat the target pattern (include a key or model
   answers when there is a correct form).
3. Supported speaking with prompts, useful phrases, and realistic follow-up questions.
4. Independent practice with a new topic, listener, or constraint.
5. A retry after focused feedback, followed by transfer to a fresh situation.
6. A clear reflection/checkpoint based on communication, interaction, and recovery—not speed alone.

Model answers show one possible response, not the only acceptable answer. Open speaking tasks should include
planning guidance, success criteria, and at least one example of a strong response at that level.

## Progression across the track

| Track | Speaking progression | Evidence of readiness |
|---|---|---|
| A1–A2 Foundations | Familiar words and frames → short connected answers → simple everyday exchanges | Can get a basic message across, answer a follow-up, and recover with a taught phrase |
| B1–B2 Confident Communication | Longer turns → stories and explanations → interactive discussion and workplace roleplay | Can sustain an exchange, give reasons/examples, clarify, and repair a misunderstanding |
| B2–C1 Professional Fluency | Unprepared contributions → audience-aware structure → nuance, negotiation, and recovery under pressure | Can adapt register, make a clear case, respond to challenge, and rephrase without losing the thread |

Do not advance learners by speaking speed or error-free performance alone. Look for successful communication,
comprehensibility, turn management, recovery, and growing independence in more than one topic.

## A repeatable practice cycle

Use this cycle in a lesson, AI practice, or independent study:

1. **Prepare:** choose one real topic; note up to three keywords, not a full script.
2. **Try:** speak for a level-appropriate time or complete a short exchange.
3. **Notice:** identify one thing that communicated well and one improvement worth making.
4. **Improve:** hear or read a natural model; practise the corrected phrase aloud.
5. **Retry:** repeat the same task once, focusing on that improvement.
6. **Transfer:** answer a fresh follow-up or use the phrase in a new context.
7. **Reflect:** record what felt easier and what to practise next—not just the number of mistakes.

Short, regular practice is more useful than a single high-pressure performance. Recordings are optional; when
used, compare the learner with their own earlier attempt, not with a native speaker.

## Checkpoint rubric

Use observable behaviours rather than a generic confidence score. A learner can answer each question with
**not yet / with support / independently**:

- Did I communicate the main message clearly enough?
- Could I keep the exchange going with a detail, follow-up, or response?
- When I lost a word or made a mistake, could I pause, rephrase, or continue?
- Could I do it with less support, or in a new situation?
- What felt easier than on my previous attempt?

Give feedback on the lesson objective and one next step. A fluent answer can contain mistakes; a pause, accent,
or self-correction is not by itself a failure.

## Author checklist

- Does the learner see/hear a natural model before being asked to perform?
- Does the chapter include a worked model and several graduated, level-appropriate practice examples?
- Are sample answers or answer keys provided where they help the learner check controlled practice?
- Does the practice move from supported to less-supported speaking?
- Is there genuine interaction as well as any monologue work?
- Does the learner get a retry and a small transfer task?
- Is there a separate vocabulary section with useful meanings and spoken examples?
- Is feedback limited to a useful priority, with specific recognition of what worked?
- Is success defined by communication and independence rather than speed or native-like speech?
- Does the checkpoint make progress visible without treating confidence as a single number?
